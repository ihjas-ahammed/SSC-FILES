Unit 11 The Biology of Gender, from DNA to the Brain Karissa Sanbonmatsu

Introduction Karissa Y. Sanbonmatsu is an American structural biologist from Los
Alamos National Laboratory. Her research focuses on the mechanisms of non-coding
RNA complexes, such as the ribosome, riboswitches, long non-coding RNAs
(lncRNAs), and chromatin. She was the first scientist to carry out an atomistic
simulation of the ribosome, to determine the secondary structure of a complete
lncRNA, and to publish a simulation involving one billion atoms of a
biomolecular complex.

Karissa Sanbonmatsu, born in Rochester, New York, pursued Physics at Columbia
University, where she utilized the Very Large Array radio telescope to estimate
the distance to the supernova remnant G27.4+0.0, later identified as a magnetar.
She completed her Ph.D. in astrophysical sciences at the University of Colorado
Boulder under Martin V. Goldman, focusing on nonlinear wave interactions in
plasmas and the interplay between Langmuir wave-wave and wave-particle effects.
Following her doctorate in 1997, she joined Los Alamos National Laboratory as a
postdoctoral researcher, investigating kinetic processes in plasmas under Donald
F. Dubois. Transitioning from Plasma Physics to questions on the nature of life,
Sanbonmatsu leveraged Los Alamos' Q-machine supercomputer in 2002 to conduct
groundbreaking biological simulations, culminating in the first atomistic
simulation of the ribosome in 2005, during which she identified the
“accommodation corridor” of the ribosome.

In her 2014 TEDx Talk, Karissa Sanbonmatsu discussed her research on epigenetics
and publicly came out as transgender. Later, in November 2018, she presented a
TED talk at TEDWomen titled “The Biology of Gender, from DNA to the Brain,”
where she explored topics such as epigenetics, the impact of trauma and diet on
DNA, and how her personal journey through gender transition inspired her to
investigate the connection between epigenetics and gender identity.
Additionally, Sanbonmatsu has been a board member of Equality New México.

Text Karissa Sanbonmatsu's talk, “The Biology of Gender, from DNA to the Brain,”
critically examines the complexity of gender identity through the lenses of
genetics, neuroscience, and epigenetics. She begins by challenging traditional
assumptions that equate womanhood solely with XX chromosomes, pointing out that
chromosomal variations such as mosaics (e.g., XX, XY, and XXX combinations)
exist. Similarly, cultural expectations of femininity, such as marriage and
childbearing, fail to capture the essence of gender identity. Sanbonmatsu
introduces recent neuroscientific findings that reveal the human brain as a
patchwork mosaic, comprising both male-typical and female-typical features
rather than fitting into rigid binary categories. This evidence undermines
simplistic notions of sexual dimorphism and calls for a deeper scientific
understanding of what constitutes gender.

Central to her discussion is the concept of epigenetics, which explores how
environmental and experiential factors influence gene expression without
altering the DNA sequence. Sanbonmatsu explains that DNA within cells undergoes
structural modifications, forming “knots” that serve as molecular markers of
experience. These modifications regulate whether specific genes are expressed,
shaping physiological and psychological outcomes. For instance, traumatic
experiences and stress can cause DNA to coil in ways that silence genes involved
in stress reduction, leading to long-term behavioral and emotional effects.
Sanbonmatsu highlights that such epigenetic programming plays a crucial role in
identity formation, including gender identity, by influencing brain development
at critical prenatal stages.

Building on this, she addresses how biological processes unfold during fetal
development, noting that the differentiation of genitals occurs in the first
trimester, while brain sexual differentiation occurs in the second trimester.
This temporal gap allows for variations in gender identity, suggesting that
biological sex and gender identity can diverge. To better understand these
processes, Sanbonmatsu and her team at Los Alamos National Laboratory employ
high-performance computing to simulate gene structures and epigenetic changes at
an atomic level. These simulations represent one of the largest biomolecular
studies to date and are providing unprecedented insights into how hormonal and
epigenetic signals guide cellular decisions during development.

Sanbonmatsu concludes by connecting scientific inquiry with her personal
narrative as a transgender woman, emphasizing both the challenges and resilience
associated with her journey. She advocates for social acceptance, mental health
support, and inclusivity, while asserting that gender identity emerges from a
dynamic interplay of biology, experience, and societal context. Her research
reframes gender as neither fixed nor purely social, but as a complex phenomenon
deeply rooted in molecular processes and human diversity. She blends science and
humanity. Biology is not destiny, she reminds us—it is fluid, responsive, and
complex. Gender is not just something written in our chromosomes, but something
shaped by a beautiful and intricate interplay between genes, brain, body, and
lived experience. She reminds us that rather than forcing people into narrow
categories, we should celebrate the diversity of gender as a reflection of the
richness of human biology.

Glossary Chromosomes: Thread-like structures in cells that carry genetic
information (DNA) Mosaics: In biology, an organism or tissue made up of cells
with different genetic makeup Dimorphism: The occurrence of two distinct forms
in one species, often male and female differences Epigenetics: The study of how
environmental factors affect gene expression without changing DNA sequence.
