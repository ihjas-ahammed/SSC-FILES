Introduction
About the Director:
Kon Ichikawa is a Japanese film director, screenwriter and a producer who was born on November 20, 1915 at Mie in Japan. His contributions in the field includes genres like Drama, Psychological Drama, War Drama, Period Film, Family Drama, and Anti-War Film. After studying animation and working in the J.O. Studios animation department, he eventually made his directing debut in the 1946 puppet movie “A Girl of Dojo Temple.” He was previously known as the “Japanese Frank Capra”. He produced a number of comedies that parodied Japanese social mores before focusing on depressing antiwar dramas and stories of sinister obsession. Ichikawa is an important modernist filmmaker. He helped to bridge the gap between the classic films of the 1950s and the more experimental films of the 1960s. He rejects many structures or inclinations of his contemporaries like Shohei Imamura or Nagisa Oshima. However, his films do have beautiful visuals owing credits to his background as an illustrator. Many of his films focus on individuals who are isolated or different from Japanese society.
Later he directed the 1975 film adaptation of Natsume Soseki’s novel, I Am a Cat (Wagahai wa Neko de Aru). The movie is a comedy-drama that follows a cat observing the life of a poor schoolteacher and his neighbours in late 19th century Japan. This movie focuses on the character named Kushami, the teacher and the narrator, the cat. Patricia Erens observed “Kon Ichikawa is noted for a wry humour that often resembles black comedy, for his grim psychological studies—often of misfits and outsiders—and for the visual beauty of his films”. He is noted as one of Japan’s foremost cinematic stylists with a painter like observation.
About the author:
Natsume Soseki (9th February 1867 – 9th December 1916) was a Japanese novelist. He was born in the town of Babashitame, Ushigome, Edo (present Kiku, Shinjuku, Tokyo) as the fifth son of village head (nanushi) Natsume Kohi Noakatsu and his wife Chie. His father, a powerful and wealthy nanushi, owned all land from Ushigome to Takadanobaba in Edo and handled most civil lawsuits at his doorstep. He was a descendant of Natsume Yoshinobu, a Sengoku period samurai and retainer of Tokugawa Ieyasu. Soseki began his life as an unwanted child, born to his mother late in her life, forty years old and his father then fifty-three. When he was born, he already had five siblings. Having five children and a toddler had created family insecurity and was in some ways a disgrace to the Natsume family. A childless couple, Shiobara Masanosuke and his wife, adopted him in 1868 and raised him until the age of nine, when the couple divorced. He returned to his biological family and was welcomed by his mother although regarded as a nuisance by his father. His mother died when he was fourteen, and his two eldest brothers died in 1887, intensifying his sense of insecurity.
Later when he had education, he was attracted to Japanese literature. Thus at the age of fifteen he dreamt of becoming a writer. Then with his familial interventions he joined the Tokyo Imperial University in September 1884 to becoming an architect by profession, but he learned English and Chinese literature too. In 1887, Soseki met Masaoka Shiki, a friend who would give him encouragement on the path to become an author. He taught him the art of writing haiku poems. In 1895, Soseki joined the profession at Matsuyama Middle School in Shikoku, which later became the setting of his novel Botchan. Along with fulfilling his teaching duties, Soseki published haiku and Chinese poetry in a number of newspapers and periodicals.
His best known literary works includes Kokoro, Botchan, I am a Cat, Kusamakura, The Tower of London, Kairo, The Miner, Ten Nights of Dream, Sanshiro and so on. Through his works he satirizes the political backdrop of the then society. Indications of world wars and the predicament of Japan during the time of war too is slightly mentioned in the narratives.
About the movie
The movie is a cinematic version of Natsume Soseki’s famous novel, known for its satirical portrayal of Japanese society. The story is told from the perspective of an unnamed cat who lives with a school teacher named Kushami (played by Tatsuya Nakadai). The film uses the cat’s observations to critique the foibles and pretensions of the characters, particularly intellectuals and those aspiring to be part of the upper class. Besides Kushami, the movie features various neighbours and acquaintances who visit the teacher’s house, each with their own quirks and social aspirations. Kon Ichikawa, known for his diverse filmography, brings his directorial vision to this adaptation, adding visual elements and spacing that complement the novel’s tone. The film has been praised for its faithfulness to the novel’s spirit and its ability to translate the literary humor into a visual medium.
The movie is a real indulgence integrating mordant wit and humour. The movie revolves around the character, Kushami Sensei who clearly embodies some of the attributes of Natsume Soseki, the Meiji Restoration-era novelist who also was a haiku poet, a writer of Chinese-style fairy tales and an expert on British literature. His 1905 novel “I Am A Cat” was a satirical look at Japanese society near the beginning of its career as an imperialist power. Storyline (Plot Summary)
The 1975 film I Am a Cat (Wagahai wa Neko de Aru), directed by Kon Ichikawa, is an episodic satirical comedy-drama set in Meiji-era Japan around 1905. It loosely follows the perspective of an unnamed stray cat who adopts the household of middle-school English teacher Mr. Kushami (Tatsuya Nakadai) — a frustrated, lazy, and perpetually complaining intellectual.
The cat observes the absurdities of human life with detached amusement. Kushami is constantly interrupted in his half-hearted attempts at painting or writing by a stream of eccentric visitors. Chief among them is the garrulous and pretentious friend Meitei (Juzo Itami), who bombards everyone with half-remembered or fabricated Western quotations (from philosophers like William James or Marcus Aurelius) while offering unsolicited opinions on everything. Another regular is Kangetsu (a former student), a physics student and violinist in love with the daughter of the wealthy businessman Kaneda. Kangetsu dithers over whether to pursue a doctorate or marriage.
The film highlights everyday domestic chaos: Kushami’s three young daughters struggling with simple tasks like eating rice, financial struggles, a brief infatuation with a beautiful niece, and a running feud with the noisy military-style school next door (backed by the influential Kaneda family). Baseballs are constantly hit into Kushami’s yard, and students trespass through the house. A burglary occurs, and the police later bring the thief for a personal apology — returning only yams.
Subplots include Kangetsu’s romantic dilemmas, ghostly visitations, and social climbing. The cat has its own adventures: rivalries with tougher neighborhood cats (like the tough “Blacky”), a tragic romance with a pampered neighbor cat that dies, and finally, after tasting beer at a celebration, the intoxicated cat falls into a water barrel and drowns — delivering a poignant, philosophical end.
The story is not tightly plotted but a series of humorous, talk-heavy vignettes that satirize Meiji Japan’s rapid Westernization, intellectual pretensions, rising capitalism, class snobbery, and the gap between lofty ideals and mundane reality.
Main Dialogues / Key Quotes (from the spirit of the film/novel)

The Cat (narrator’s opening style): “I am a cat. As yet I have no name. I’ve no idea where I was born...”
Kushami (frequently complaining): “I’m no good at it...” (referring to his painting or life projects).
Meitei (pontificating with fake erudition): Drops misquoted Western philosophy while discussing life, art, or society.
Kushami (on his visitors): Often remarks that Meitei is making things up or misquoting sources.
Famous novel/film tone lines include observations like humans being “puffed up by their extreme self-satisfaction” and the cat’s wry commentary on human follies.

The film ends on a touching note between Kushami and his wife, with Kushami seen writing “I Am a Cat,” blending humor with quiet melancholy.
