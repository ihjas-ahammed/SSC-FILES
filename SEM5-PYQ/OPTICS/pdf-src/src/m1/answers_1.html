1PYQ N20 Q1 · adapted

Define optical path. How is it related to the time taken by light?

Know firstT1P1

The optical path between two points is the product of the refractive index of the medium and the geometrical path length: $L = n\,l$. For a path through several media, or a medium of varying index, $L = \int n\,ds$.

Since light travels at $c/n$ in the medium, the time taken is $\tau = \int \frac{n\,ds}{c} = \frac{L}{c}$. So the optical path is the distance light would travel in vacuum in the same time.

$L = \int n\,ds = c\,\tau$
2PYQ N22 Q11

Explain Fermat's principle of extremum path.

Know firstT2T1M3

Fermat's principle: the actual path of a ray between two points is the one for which the optical path $\int n\,ds$ is an extremum (or, more generally, stationary) compared with all neighbouring paths: $\delta\int_A^B n\,ds = 0$.

The extremum may be a minimum (straight-line propagation, reflection at a plane mirror, refraction), a maximum (reflection at a concave mirror curving more sharply than the ellipse touching it at the point of reflection), or constant (elliptical reflector). So "least time" is only a special case.

3PYQ N20 Q11

Write down the three results obtained from Fermat's principle.

Know firstT2T3T4
  1. Rectilinear propagation: in a homogeneous medium light travels in straight lines, since $\int n\,ds = n\times$ length is least for a straight line.
  2. Laws of reflection: the incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal are coplanar, and the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
  3. Laws of refraction: the incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal are coplanar, and $n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2$ (Snell's law).
4PYQ N19 Q11

State Fermat's principle of stationary time. Explain with respect to an elliptical reflector.

Know firstT2T5M5

Statement: a ray of light travels between two points along the path for which the time taken (optical path) is stationary with respect to small variations of the path.

Elliptical reflector: for a point source at one focus $S_1$ of an elliptical (ellipsoidal) mirror, every point P on the mirror satisfies $S_1P + PS_2 = $ constant. So all paths from $S_1$ to $S_2$ via the mirror take equal time. The time is neither a maximum nor a minimum but constant, i.e. stationary. Hence every ray from $S_1$ reaches $S_2$ after reflection.

What is meant by a ray of light? When is the ray description valid?

Know firstT1

A ray is the path along which light energy travels, drawn as a line (normal to the wavefronts). Rays are an idealisation: if we try to isolate a single ray with a very small aperture, diffraction spreads the light.

The ray (geometrical-optics) description is valid when all apertures and obstacles are very large compared with the wavelength, so that diffraction is negligible. Geometrical optics is the limit $\lambda\to0$.

6

Why does a spherical mirror of large aperture not form a sharp image? Name the defect.

Know firstT5T6

A spherical surface makes the optical paths from an object point to the image point equal only for paraxial rays (only up to terms in $\theta^2$). Rays striking the mirror far from the axis cross the axis at different points (nearer the mirror for a concave mirror), so a point object gives a blurred patch. This defect is called spherical aberration. A paraboloidal mirror is free from it for parallel light.

7

Show by Fermat's principle that a paraboloidal mirror focuses parallel rays to one point.

Know firstT5M5T2

For a parabola, every point Q is equidistant from the focus S and the directrix: $QS = QL$. Consider rays parallel to the axis, starting from a plane P perpendicular to the axis. Path $= PQ + QS = PQ + QL$, which is the same for every ray (the distance from plane P to the directrix). All parallel rays have equal optical paths to S. So by Fermat's principle they all meet at S.

8PYQ N22 Q1 · adapted

What is the paraxial approximation? What does it allow?

Know firstT6M1

In the paraxial approximation we consider only rays that pass close to the axis and make small angles with it. This allows $\sin\phi \approx \tan\phi\approx\phi$ and $\cos\phi\approx1$ (or $1-\phi^2/2$). We can also measure distances from the pole instead of from the foot of the perpendicular from the point of incidence. Under it, all rays from an object point meet at a single image point.

9

State the Cartesian sign convention.

Know firstP4T6

With light incident from left to right:

  1. All distances are measured from the pole (or the optical centre).
  2. Distances measured in the direction of the incident light (to the right) are positive; those against it are negative.
  3. Heights above the axis are positive, below are negative.
  4. The radius of curvature is positive if the centre of curvature lies to the right of the surface, negative if to the left.
10

Meridional and skew rays.

Know firstT6

A meridional ray lies in a plane containing the optical axis. A skew ray does not lie in any such plane. For a single spherical surface the axis is the line joining the object point O and the centre of curvature C. Any ray from O, together with this line, defines a plane through the axis, so every such ray is meridional and, after refraction, crosses the axis again.

Air to glass ($n=1.5$), incidence 30°. Angle of refraction?

Know firstT4P2
$n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2$Snell's law.
$\sin\theta_2 = \dfrac{1\times\sin30^\circ}{1.5} = \dfrac{0.5}{1.5} = 0.333$Substitute.
$\theta_2 = \sin^{-1}(0.333) = 19.5^\circ$Smaller than 30°: the ray bends towards the normal on entering the denser medium.
$\theta_2 \approx 19.5^\circ$
12

What is a virtual object?

Know firstT9P4

When the rays falling on a surface are converging towards a point that lies beyond the surface, that point acts as a virtual object: the rays never actually start from it. In a system of two surfaces, if the image formed by the first surface lies beyond the second surface, the rays reach the second surface before they meet. That image is then a virtual object for the second surface, with $u > 0$ in the Cartesian convention.

13

Focal lengths of a single refracting surface; show $f_2/f_1=-n_2/n_1$.

Know firstT9T7

From $\frac{n_2}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{n_2-n_1}{R}$:

First focus: $v\to\infty$, $u = f_1$:   $f_1 = -\dfrac{n_1 R}{n_2-n_1}$Object position whose image is at infinity.
Second focus: $u\to-\infty$, $v = f_2$:   $f_2 = \dfrac{n_2R}{n_2-n_1}$Image of an object at infinity.
Dividing: $\dfrac{f_2}{f_1} = -\dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$
14

Mirror equation from the refraction formula with $n_2=-n_1$.

Know firstT10T7

Reflection can be treated as refraction into a medium of index $n_2 = -n_1$, because then Snell's law $n_1\sin\phi_1 = -n_1\sin\phi_2$ gives $\phi_2 = -\phi_1$: the ray returns with an equal angle. Substituting in $\frac{n_2}{v}-\frac{n_1}{u}=\frac{n_2-n_1}{R}$:

$$-\frac{n_1}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = -\frac{2n_1}{R}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;\frac1u+\frac1v = \frac2R$$

which is the mirror equation.

15PYQ N24 Q1

Define a thin lens.

Know firstT11

A lens is a portion of a transparent medium bounded by two spherical (or one spherical and one plane) refracting surfaces. It is called a thin lens when its thickness is negligible compared with the object distance, the image distance and the radii of curvature of its surfaces. All distances can then be measured from the centre of the lens.

17PYQ N24 Q2

Define first principal focus of a lens.

Know firstT12T13

The first principal focus $F_1$ is the point on the axis such that rays diverging from it (for a convex lens) or converging towards it (for a concave lens) emerge from the lens parallel to the axis. Its image is at infinity. Its distance from the lens is the first focal length $f_1$ (equal to $-f$ for a thin lens in air).

18PYQ N25 Q1

Glass ($n=1.5$) biconvex lens in water ($n=1.33$): converging?

Know firstT12T11P1

Yes, it still converges. By the lens-maker's formula $\frac1f = (n-1)\left(\frac1{R_1}-\frac1{R_2}\right)$, where $n = n_{glass}/n_{water} = 1.5/1.33 = 1.128$.

Since $n - 1 = 0.128 > 0$, and $\left(\frac1{R_1}-\frac1{R_2}\right)>0$ for a biconvex lens, $f$ is positive: converging. But it is much weaker: $\frac{f_{water}}{f_{air}} = \frac{0.5}{0.128}\approx3.9$, so the focal length is about four times longer.

Still converging; focal length ≈ 3.9 times larger
19

Three rules of ray construction.

Know firstT13T12
  1. A ray parallel to the axis passes, after refraction, through the second focus $F_2$ (or appears to come from it, for a concave lens).
  2. A ray through the optical centre goes on undeviated.
  3. A ray through the first focus $F_1$ (or directed towards it) emerges parallel to the axis.

The point where any two of these meet after the lens is the image of the point they started from.

20PYQ N24 Q3

Define lateral magnification.

Know firstT15M2

Lateral (transverse) magnification is the ratio of the height of the image to the height of the object, both measured perpendicular to the axis with their signs: $m = h'/h$. For a thin lens $m = v/u$. A positive $m$ means an erect image and a negative $m$ an inverted one. $|m|>1$ means the image is enlarged.

21

Newton's formula and its signs.

Know firstT14P4

$x_1 x_2 = f_1f_2 = -f^2$ for a thin lens in air. Here $x_1$ is the distance of the object measured from the first focus $F_1$, and $x_2$ is the distance of the image measured from the second focus $F_2$, both with Cartesian signs. Since the product is negative, $x_1$ and $x_2$ have opposite signs: an object to the left of $F_1$ gives an image to the right of $F_2$. (In magnitudes: $|x_1||x_2| = f^2$.)

22

Show $m=-v/u$ for a spherical mirror.

Know firstT15T10

For a refracting surface $m = \dfrac{n_1v}{n_2u}$. A mirror behaves like a surface with $n_2 = -n_1$ (Q14). Substituting:

$$m = \frac{n_1 v}{-n_1 u} = -\frac vu$$
23

Longitudinal magnification $= m^2$.

Know firstT15M3
$\dfrac1v - \dfrac1u = \dfrac1f$Thin lens formula ($f$ fixed).
$-\dfrac{dv}{v^2} + \dfrac{du}{u^2} = 0$Differentiate. $d(1/x) = -dx/x^2$.
$\dfrac{dv}{du} = \dfrac{v^2}{u^2} = m^2$Since $m = v/u$ for a thin lens.

$dv/du$ is always positive, so the image moves in the same direction as the object.

24

Power of a lens; concave lens, $f=25$ cm.

Know firstT12P3

The power of a lens is the reciprocal of its focal length in metres: $P = 1/f$. Its unit is the dioptre (D). It measures how strongly the lens converges ($P>0$) or diverges ($P<0$).

Concave lens: $f = -25$ cm $= -0.25$ m, so $P = \dfrac{1}{-0.25} = -4$ D.

$P = -4$ dioptre
