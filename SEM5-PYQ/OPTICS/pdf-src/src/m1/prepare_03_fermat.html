Fermat's principle

One rule about time explains straight lines, mirrors and refraction.

T1

Rays and optical path

When can we draw light as lines? And how do we measure a path in "time units"?

The idea · what is a ray?

Shine a lamp through a 1 cm hole: you get a sharp patch of light. Make the hole tiny (about 0.1 mm) and the patch spreads out with fuzzy edges. That spreading is diffraction, and it happens because light is a wave.

When everything (holes, lenses, mirrors) is much bigger than the wavelength (about $5\times10^{-5}$ cm), the spreading is too small to notice. Then light goes in sharp lines called rays. Studying light with rays is geometrical optics. It is the limit $\lambda \to 0$.

Ghatak §3.1. In the model paper: Q5.

Optical path = refractive index × length

Speed in a medium: $v = \dfrac{c}{n}$From P1.
Time for a small piece of path $ds$: $d\tau = \dfrac{ds}{v} = \dfrac{n\,ds}{c}$time = distance ÷ speed.
Add up all pieces from A to B: $\tau = \dfrac1c\displaystyle\int_A^B n\,ds$The sign ∫ is a stretched S, for "Sum". The integral just adds up all the tiny pieces (Ghatak eq. 3.1).
Name the integral: $L_{op} = \displaystyle\int_A^B n\,ds = c\,\tau$So optical path = $c$ × travel time. It is the distance light would cover in vacuum in the same time.
Result · optical path

Optical path length = (refractive index) × (geometrical path length). For several media: $L_{op} = n_1 l_1 + n_2 l_2 + \cdots$

Comparing optical paths is the same as comparing travel times.

Exam trapA shorter route through glass can have a longer optical path than a longer route through air. Always multiply by $n$ before you compare.
Check yourself
Light crosses 3 cm of air and then 2 cm of glass ($n=1.5$). Optical path?
$1\times3 + 1.5\times2 = 6$ cm. Light takes as long as it would to cross 6 cm of vacuum.

T2

Fermat's principle: stationary, not "least"

The one rule behind the whole module, stated the way examiners want.

{{fig:lifeguard}}
The idea A lifeguard runs fast on sand and swims slowly in water. The quickest route to the swimmer is not the straight line. It is a bent line: run a bit further on sand, swim a bit less. Light behaves like the lifeguard. Each medium has its own speed, and the light's path bends where the medium changes.
Result · Fermat's principle (exam statement)

The actual path of a ray between two points is the path for which the optical path length (equivalently, the time taken) is stationary: it is a minimum, a maximum, or stays unchanged for small changes of the path.

$$\delta\int_A^B n\,ds = 0\qquad\text{(Ghatak 3.3)}$$

Read δ as "the change when the path is wiggled a little". The equation says: wiggle the path, and the optical path does not change (to first order).

"Stationary" means: move the path a tiny bit, and the optical path changes only by a second-order amount. That is the "flat spot" of M3.

Why not just "least time"?

Fermat first stated it as the principle of least time. Ghatak calls that form "incomplete and slightly incorrect", because some real rays take a path of maximum time, and some take a path whose time does not change at all.

{{fig:mirror_max}}
Minimumstraight line in one medium; plane-mirror reflection; ordinary refraction Constantelliptical mirror, focus to focus (T5) Maximuma mirror curving more sharply than the ellipse touching it (above); also points beyond the image of a curved surface (T8)
Exam trapWriting "light takes the shortest path" loses marks twice. The rule is about optical path (time), not length, and it is stationary, not least. Say "extremum or stationary" (PYQ N22 Q11 uses the word "extremum").
Check yourself
1. State Fermat's principle in one line.
A ray travels between two points along the path whose optical path length ∫n ds is stationary (minimum, maximum or constant) compared with nearby paths.
2. Give one case where the time is a maximum.
Reflection inside a concave mirror that curves more sharply than the ellipse (with foci at the two points) touching it at the point of reflection.

T3

Result 1 and 2: straight lines and the law of reflection

The first two of the "three results obtained from Fermat's principle" (PYQ N20 Q11).

Result 1: in one medium, light goes straight

$n$ is the same everywhere, so $L_{op} = \displaystyle\int n\,ds = n\times(\text{length})$$n$ comes out of the integral because it is constant.
Stationary optical path = stationary lengthMultiplying by a constant $n$ does not move the flat spot.
The shortest line between two points is straight, so the ray is a straight line (a minimum)This is rectilinear propagation.

Result 2: law of reflection

{{fig:fermat_reflection}}

Light goes from A to B by touching the mirror at some point P. Which P?

Draw A′, the mirror image of A: as far below the mirror as A is above itA trick to "unfold" the bent path.
For every point P on the mirror, $AP = A'P$The mirror is the perpendicular bisector of AA′, so every point on it is equally far from A and A′.
So path $AP + PB = A'P + PB$Same length, now written as a path from A′ to B.
Shortest $A'P + PB$: when A′, P, B lie on one straight lineResult 1. Any other point Q gives a longer path (grey dotted).
That straight line lies in the plane containing A, A′ and B, which also contains the normal at PAA′ is parallel to the normal. So incident ray, normal and reflected ray are in one plane.
(angle of PB with the upward normal) = (angle of PA′ with the downward normal)A′PB is one straight line crossing the normal line at P, so these are vertically opposite angles. The left side is $r$.
(angle of PA′ with the downward normal) = (angle of PA with the upward normal) $= i$A′ is the mirror copy of A, so the picture below the mirror is a flipped copy of the picture above.
Therefore $i = r$Both are equal to the same angle.
Result · law of reflectionThe incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal lie in one plane, and $i = r$. Here the path is a minimum.

T4

Result 3: Snell's law from Fermat

The lifeguard problem done with maths. A favourite derivation.

{{fig:fermat_refraction}}

A is at height $h_1$ above the surface in medium $n_1$. B is at depth $h_2$ in medium $n_2$. Their horizontal separation is $d$ (fixed). The ray crosses the surface at P, a horizontal distance $x$ from A. Only $x$ can change.

$AP = \sqrt{x^2 + h_1^2}$,   $PB = \sqrt{(d-x)^2 + h_2^2}$Pythagoras (M2).
$L(x) = n_1\sqrt{x^2+h_1^2} + n_2\sqrt{(d-x)^2+h_2^2}$Optical path = $n$ × length for each part (T1). Ghatak eq. 3.4.
$\dfrac{dL}{dx} = \dfrac{n_1 x}{\sqrt{x^2+h_1^2}} - \dfrac{n_2 (d-x)}{\sqrt{(d-x)^2+h_2^2}} = 0$Fermat: slope zero (M3). Note the minus from the chain rule.
$\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+h_1^2}} = \sin\theta_1$,   $\dfrac{d-x}{\sqrt{(d-x)^2+h_2^2}} = \sin\theta_2$Read off the two triangles: opposite ÷ hypotenuse, with the angles measured from the normal.
$n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2$Put these into step 3. This is Snell's law (Ghatak 3.6).
Also note

• The coplanarity part follows as for reflection. If P moved out of the plane containing A, B and the normal, both AP and PB would get longer.

• The second derivative is positive, so this ray takes the minimum time.

• Fermat (1657) predicted that light bends towards the normal because it is slower in the denser medium. The particle model of that time predicted the opposite.

Result · the three results from Fermat (PYQ N20 Q11)
  1. In a homogeneous medium light travels in straight lines (rectilinear propagation).
  2. Law of reflection: coplanar, $i = r$.
  3. Law of refraction: coplanar, $n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2$.
Exam trapIn step 3, students drop the minus sign. Then they get $n_1\sin\theta_1 = -n_2\sin\theta_2$ and get stuck. Check: when $x$ grows, $PB$ gets shorter, so its derivative must be negative.
Check yourself
If both points are in the same medium ($n_1=n_2$) and P is on a mirror instead, what does the same calculation give?
$\sin\theta_1 = \sin\theta_2$, so $i = r$: the law of reflection again.

In the model paper: Q29, Q11.

T5

Special mirrors and perfect images

Ellipse, parabola, and why "equal optical paths" means a sharp image.

{{fig:ellipse}}
Elliptical reflector (Ghatak Ex. 3.2, PYQ N19 Q11)

Put a source at focus $F_1$ of a mirror shaped like an ellipse. For every point P on the mirror, $F_1P + PF_2 = 2a$ (M5). So every path from $F_1$ via the mirror to $F_2$ has the same optical path.

Then nearby paths are not longer and not shorter. The path is stationary: neither a minimum nor a maximum, but constant. Every ray from $F_1$ reaches $F_2$. This shows clearly why Fermat says "stationary" and not "least".

{{fig:parabola}}
Paraboloidal mirror (Ghatak Ex. 3.1) For rays coming in parallel to the axis, start every path at the same line P, perpendicular to the axis. Path = $PQ + QS = PQ + QL$, because a parabola has $QS = QL$ (M5). Now PQ and QL lie on one horizontal line, so $PQ + QL$ is just the distance from line P to the directrix. That is the same for every ray. So all parallel rays meet at the focus S, perfectly. This is why car headlights and dish antennas are paraboloids.
Result · perfect imaging If every ray from a point O reaches a point I with the same optical path, they all arrive in step and I is a perfect (sharp) image of O. Fermat's principle is then satisfied for every ray at once.
{{fig:surface_optical_path}}

Why a sphere is not perfect: spherical aberration

{{fig:spherical_aberration}}

A spherical mirror or surface makes the optical paths equal only for rays close to the axis. In T8 we will see the equality holds only up to terms in $\theta^2$. Rays far from the axis cross it at slightly different points, so the image is blurred. This defect is spherical aberration. That is why every formula in this module needs the paraxial approximation (T6). A paraboloid has no such defect for parallel light.

Check yourself
1. In an elliptical reflector, is the path from focus to focus a minimum?
No. All such paths are equal, so the path is stationary (constant), neither a minimum nor a maximum.
2. Why do big telescopes use paraboloidal mirrors instead of spherical ones?
A paraboloid brings all parallel rays to one focus: equal optical paths and no spherical aberration.
