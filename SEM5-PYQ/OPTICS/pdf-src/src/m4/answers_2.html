26

Brewster's law; reflected ⟂ refracted.

Know firstT4P3M2
{{fig:brewster}}

At the polarising angle the reflection coefficient for the component vibrating in the plane of incidence vanishes (Ghatak §24.5):

$r_\parallel = \dfrac{n_2\cos i - n_1\cos r}{n_2\cos i + n_1\cos r} = 0 \Rightarrow n_2\cos i = n_1\cos r$Fresnel's formula for the parallel component.
Snell: $n_1\sin i = n_2\sin r$
Multiply the two: $n_1 n_2\sin i\cos i = n_1 n_2\sin r\cos r \Rightarrow \sin 2i = \sin 2r$
Since $i \ne r$: $2i = 180^\circ - 2r$, so $i + r = 90^\circ$Reflected and refracted rays perpendicular: angle between them $= 180^\circ - i - r = 90^\circ$.
Then $\sin r = \cos i$, and Snell gives $n_1\sin i_B = n_2\cos i_B$, so $\tan i_B = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1} = n$

Physical picture: the refracted wave's electrons oscillate (for the parallel component) along the direction of the would-be reflected ray, and an oscillating dipole does not radiate along its axis, so the reflected light lacks that component and is plane polarised perpendicular to the plane of incidence.

$\tan i_B = n$ and $i_B + r = 90^\circ$
27

Pile of plates.

Know firstT4

A pile of plates is 10–15 thin glass plates stacked parallel, with unpolarised light incident at the polarising angle ($\tan i_B = n$).

At every surface, the reflected light is plane polarised perpendicular to the plane of incidence (Brewster)
Only about 15% of that perpendicular component is reflected per surface ($n = 1.5$); the parallel component is fully transmitted
After many surfaces almost all the perpendicular vibration has been reflected away, so the transmitted beam is nearly plane polarised, vibrating in the plane of incidence

Why the transmitted light is used: the reflected light from one surface is polarised but very weak (about 15% of one component); the transmitted beam is much more intense and, after many plates, nearly completely polarised.

30

E-ray obeys Snell's law when the optic axis ⟂ plane of incidence.

Know firstT6P3
Plane wavefront AB meets the crystal surface obliquely at angle $i$. While the wave travels BC $= c\,t$ in air, A sends wavelets into the crystal
The optic axis is perpendicular to the plane of incidence (the page). The E-ellipsoid is a figure of revolution about that axis, so its section by the page is a circle, of radius $v_\perp t = (c/\mu_e)\,t$
The E-wavefront is the tangent from C to this circle, exactly as for an ordinary isotropic medium of speed $c/\mu_e$
From the triangles: $\sin i = \dfrac{ct}{AC}$, $\sin r_e = \dfrac{(c/\mu_e)t}{AC}$, so $\dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r_e} = \mu_e$The E-ray behaves like an ordinary ray of index $\mu_e$; the O-ray obeys $\sin i/\sin r_o = \mu_o$.

This is how $\mu_e$ is measured: with a prism cut so that the optic axis is perpendicular to the plane of incidence.

31

Construction and working of a Nicol prism.

Know firstT7T5P3
{{fig:nicol}}

Construction: a calcite crystal whose length is about three times its breadth. Its end faces are ground so that their angle with the long edges changes from 71° to 68°. It is cut into two along a plane perpendicular to the principal section and to the new end faces, and the two halves are cemented with Canada balsam ($\mu = 1.55$). The sides are blackened.

Working:

Unpolarised light entering parallel to the long edge splits into the O-ray ($\mu_o = 1.658$) and the E-ray ($\mu_e = 1.486$)
For the O-ray, balsam is rarer (1.55 < 1.658). Critical angle $C = \sin^{-1}(1.55/1.658) \approx 69^\circ$. Its angle of incidence exceeds C, so it is totally reflected and absorbed at the blackened side
For the E-ray, balsam is denser (1.55 > 1.486), so no total reflection; it emerges plane polarised, vibrating in the principal section

Use: as a polariser and an analyser. Between two Nicols, rotating the second gives maximum light when parallel and extinction when crossed.

Plate between crossed polarisers.

Know firstT10T9M1
After P₁: amplitude $A$, intensity $I_1 = I_0/2$. The plate's axis is at φ: components $A\cos\phi$ (E) and $A\sin\phi$ (O)
After the plate the O component has an extra phase δ. The analyser P₂ (⟂ P₁) takes $A\cos\phi\sin\phi$ from one and $-A\sin\phi\cos\phi\,e^{i\delta}$ from the other
Resultant amplitude $A\sin\phi\cos\phi\,(1 - e^{i\delta})$; $|1 - e^{i\delta}|^2 = 2 - 2\cos\delta = 4\sin^2(\delta/2)$
$I = I_1\cdot 4\sin^2\phi\cos^2\phi\sin^2\tfrac\delta2 = I_1\sin^2 2\phi\,\sin^2\tfrac\delta2$
φ = 45°: QWP (δ = π/2): $I = I_1/2 = I_0/4$. HWP (δ = π): $I = I_1 = I_0/2$
QWP: $I_0/4$; HWP: $I_0/2$

Plane = right + left circular; Fresnel's theory; rotation in 1 mm quartz.

Know firstT12T8M2
Right circular: $E_x = E_0\cos\omega t$, $E_y = E_0\sin\omega t$. Left circular: $E_x = E_0\cos\omega t$, $E_y = -E_0\sin\omega t$
Sum: $E_x = 2E_0\cos\omega t$, $E_y = 0$: plane polarised along xThe y parts cancel at every instant.
In an active medium the two circular waves travel with indices $n_R \ne n_L$. After length $z$, their phases differ: one is ahead by $\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(n_L-n_R)z$
Adding them again gives plane polarised light whose direction has turned by half that phase difference: $\phi = \dfrac{\pi}{\lambda}(n_L-n_R)z$
Quartz: $\phi = \dfrac{\pi\times7\times10^{-5}\times10^{-3}}{6\times10^{-7}} = 0.367$ rad $= 21^\circ$
Rotation ≈ 21° per mm of quartz
35PYQ N20 Q23

Producing plane, circular and elliptical light.

Know firstT10T9T7T8

Plane polarised light: pass monochromatic light through a Nicol prism (or Polaroid). The emergent light vibrates in the principal section of the Nicol.

Circularly polarised light: let this plane polarised light fall normally on a quarter-wave plate with its optic axis at 45° to the vibration. Components $E_0\cos45^\circ$ (E) and $E_0\sin45^\circ$ (O) are equal; the plate introduces δ = π/2. With $E_x = a\cos\omega t$, $E_y = a\cos(\omega t - \pi/2) = a\sin\omega t$: $E_x^2 + E_y^2 = a^2$, a circle.

Elliptically polarised light: as above, but with the optic axis at an angle φ ≠ 0°, 45°, 90°. Amplitudes $a = E_0\cos\phi$, $b = E_0\sin\phi$ are unequal; δ = π/2 gives $\dfrac{E_x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{E_y^2}{b^2}=1$, an ellipse with axes along the plate's axes. (Any plate with δ ≠ 0, π/2, π also gives elliptical light.)

Marks: plane 1 · circular with reason 2 · elliptical with reason 2.

36PYQ N24 Q18

Double refraction; Laurent's half-shade polarimeter.

Know firstT5T12T9

Double refraction: when unpolarised light enters an anisotropic crystal such as calcite, it splits into two refracted rays, the ordinary ray (obeys Snell's law, same speed in all directions) and the extraordinary ray (speed depends on direction). Both are plane polarised at right angles to each other.

{{fig:laurent}}

Laurent's half-shade polarimeter measures the rotation of the plane of polarisation by a solution.

Parts: sodium lamp, collimating lens, polarising Nicol, half-shade plate, tube for the solution, analysing Nicol with telescope and circular scale
Half-shade plate: one semicircle is a quartz half-wave plate (optic axis parallel to the dividing line); the other is glass absorbing equally. If the polariser's vibration OA makes a small angle ε with the axis, light through the HWP vibrates along OB, at −ε
When the analyser is perpendicular to the bisector of AOB, both halves are equally dim. A slight turn makes one half brighter and the other darker, so this setting is very sensitive
Procedure: with the tube full of water, set equal dimness; read the scale. Fill with solution: the planes rotate by θ and the balance is lost. Turn the analyser until equal dimness returns. The difference of readings is θ
Specific rotation $[\alpha] = \dfrac{\theta}{l\,c}$ ($l$ in dm, $c$ in g/cm³); or concentration $c = \dfrac{\theta}{[\alpha]l}$

38

Partially polarised light; I_max = 3 I_min.

Know firstT2T1

Partially polarised light is a mixture of unpolarised and plane polarised light. Through a rotating analyser its intensity varies between $I_{max}$ and $I_{min}$ without ever becoming zero.

$P = \dfrac{I_{max}-I_{min}}{I_{max}+I_{min}} = \dfrac{3-1}{3+1} = 0.5$
Unpolarised part $I_u$ gives $I_u/2$ at every angle; polarised part $I_p$ adds $I_p\cos^2\theta$. So $I_{min} = I_u/2$, $I_{max} = I_u/2 + I_p$
$I_{max} = 3I_{min}$ gives $I_p = 2I_{min} = I_u$Equal amounts of polarised and unpolarised light.
Degree of polarisation 50%: half polarised, half unpolarised
