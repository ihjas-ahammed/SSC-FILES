The whole module in one story

The idea

1. Light is a wave whose electric field shakes sideways, at 90° to its direction of travel. "Polarisation" is about which sideways direction (T1).

2. A polariser lets through only the part of the field along its axis. Two polarisers give Malus' law (T2).

3. We can make polarised light by absorption, scattering, reflection at Brewster's angle, or by double refraction in a crystal (T3, T4, T7).

4. In calcite, light splits into two rays with different speeds and perpendicular vibrations. Huygens explained it with two kinds of wavelets (T5, T6).

5. Two perpendicular vibrations with a phase difference make a line, a circle or an ellipse (T8). A crystal plate creates exactly that phase difference: wave plates (T9).

6. So we can produce and identify every kind of polarised light (T10, T11). Some materials even rotate the vibration: optical activity (T12).

P1

Waves: the words you need

Amplitude, phase, intensity, and the difference between transverse and longitudinal.

Remind me · Plus One Ch 14 (Waves)
  • A wave: $y = a\cos(kz - \omega t)$. $a$ = amplitude (biggest shake), $\lambda$ = wavelength, $k = 2\pi/\lambda$, $\omega = 2\pi\nu$.
  • The angle inside the cosine is the phase. Two waves whose phases differ by $\delta$ are "out of step by $\delta$".
  • A path difference $\Delta$ gives a phase difference $\delta = \dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta$. So $\lambda/4$ of path = $\pi/2$ (90°) of phase, and $\lambda/2$ of path = $\pi$ (180°).
  • Intensity ∝ (amplitude)². Double the amplitude, four times the brightness.
{{fig:transverse}}
Transverse vs longitudinal Transverse: the shaking is at 90° to the direction of travel (a rope wave, light). Longitudinal: the shaking is along the direction of travel (sound in air). A transverse wave can shake up–down, left–right or anything in between, so it has a "direction of shaking" to choose. That choice is polarisation. A longitudinal wave has no such choice, so it cannot be polarised.
Check yourself
A path difference of 3λ/4. Phase difference?
$\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{3\lambda}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{2}$ (270°).
P2

Light is an electromagnetic wave

Which arrow is "the light"? The electric field.

Remind me · Plus Two Ch 8
  • Light consists of an electric field $\vec E$ and a magnetic field $\vec B$, both oscillating.
  • $\vec E \perp \vec B$, and both are perpendicular to the direction of travel. So light is transverse.
  • Speed in vacuum $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = 3\times10^8$ m/s; in a medium $v = c/n$.
The idea When we talk about the "vibration" of light we mean the electric field $\vec E$, because it is $\vec E$ that pushes the electrons in our eyes, films and detectors. The polarisation of light = the direction in which $\vec E$ vibrates.
Check yourself
Light travels along $z$ with $\vec E$ along $x$. Along which axis is $\vec B$?
Along $y$: perpendicular to both $\vec E$ and the direction of travel.
P3

Refraction, total internal reflection, Huygens' principle

Three Plus Two ideas that this module reuses.

Remind me · Class 10 / Plus Two Ch 9, 10
  • Snell's law: $n_1\sin i = n_2\sin r$. Angles from the normal. Refractive index $n = c/v$.
  • Total internal reflection: going from a denser to a rarer medium ($n_1 > n_2$), if the angle of incidence is more than the critical angle $C$, where $\sin C = n_2/n_1$, no light goes out: all of it reflects. (Needed for the Nicol prism, T7.)
  • Huygens' principle: every point on a wavefront acts as a source of small "secondary wavelets". After a time $t$, the new wavefront is the surface touching all the wavelets (their envelope). (Needed for T6.)
Check yourself
Critical angle for light going from glass of $n = 1.658$ into a layer of $n = 1.55$?
$\sin C = 1.55/1.658 = 0.935$, so $C \approx 69.2^\circ$.
P4

What Plus Two already told you about polarisation

NCERT XII §10.7 in one box. This module goes deeper into each line.

Remind me · Plus Two Ch 10
  • Ordinary light (sun, bulb) is unpolarised: its $\vec E$ points in random sideways directions that change very fast.
  • A polaroid lets through only the vibrations along its "pass axis". The light that comes out is plane (linearly) polarised, with half the intensity.
  • Two polaroids at angle θ: $I = I_0\cos^2\theta$ (Malus' law).
  • Sunlight scattered by the sky at 90° is polarised.
  • Light reflected at Brewster's angle ($\tan i_B = n$) is polarised.

Good news: all of this is still true. Now you will learn why, and meet new tools: crystals that split light (calcite), plates that turn a line of vibration into a circle, and liquids that rotate the vibration.
