T8

The diffraction grating

Many equally spaced slits: a tool that spreads light into its colours.

{{fig:grating}}
Result · grating (PYQ N20 Q15) A diffraction grating is a plate with a very large number of equally spaced parallel slits (lines ruled on glass, thousands per cm). The distance $d$ from one line to the next is the grating element. Light of wavelength λ gives bright principal maxima where $$d\sin\theta = m\lambda\qquad(m = 0, 1, 2, \ldots)$$ $m = 0$ is at θ = 0 for every colour (white). Each higher order spreads white light into a spectrum, violet nearest the centre.
Highest order: $\sin\theta \le 1$, so $m \le d/\lambda$. Example: 5000 lines/cm, λ = 5000 Å: $d = 2\times10^{-4}$ cm, $d/\lambda = 4$m = 4 would need θ = 90° (grazing), which cannot be observed, so the highest visible order is 3.

Dispersion

Differentiate $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ at fixed m: $d\cos\theta\,\Delta\theta = m\,\Delta\lambda$
$\dfrac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta\lambda} = \dfrac{m}{d\cos\theta}$Ghatak 18.65: angular dispersion. Bigger for higher order, and for smaller d (finer grating).
PYQ N20 Q27: 15000 lines/inch, $d = 1.693\times10^{-4}$ cm; sodium λ = 5890, 5896 Å; f = 10 cm. First order: $\sin\theta = \dfrac{5.89\times10^{-5}}{1.693\times10^{-4}} = 0.348$, $\cos\theta = 0.938$
$\Delta\theta = \dfrac{1\times6\times10^{-8}}{1.693\times10^{-4}\times0.938} = 3.78\times10^{-4}$ rad
On the screen: $f\Delta\theta = 10\times3.78\times10^{-4} = 3.8\times10^{-3}$ cm ≈ 0.038 mm
Overlapping orders (PYQ N22 Q15)Order m of $\lambda_2$ lands on order m+1 of $\lambda_1$ when $m\lambda_2 = (m+1)\lambda_1$. For visible light (400–700 nm) the 1st and 2nd orders never overlap (2 × 400 = 800 > 700), but the 2nd and 3rd do (3 × 400 = 1200 < 2 × 700 = 1400).
Grating vs prism (PYQ N20 Q7)Grating spectra are purer and sharper, and nearly linear (Δθ ∝ Δλ for small θ). A prism's spread depends on how n changes with λ, which is uneven. So "grating spectra are purer than prism spectra" is true.
Exam trap"Larger the grating element, larger the dispersion": false (N19 Q9). Dispersion ∝ 1/d.

In the model paper: Q12 (PYQ N20 Q15), Q13 (PYQ N19 Q9), Q14 (PYQ N22 Q15), Q15 (PYQ N20 Q7), Q18, Q28 (PYQ N20 Q27).

T9

Resolving power of a grating

Can the grating separate two very close wavelengths? Asked in 2022, 2024, 2025.

{{fig:rayleigh}}
m-th order of λ: $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$. Of $\lambda + \Delta\lambda$: $d\sin\theta' = m(\lambda+\Delta\lambda)$
Rayleigh: just resolved when the maximum of $\lambda+\Delta\lambda$ falls on the first minimum next to the maximum of λ, which is at $d\sin\theta = m\lambda + \dfrac{\lambda}{N}$The first zero beside a principal maximum (T6, T7).
$m(\lambda + \Delta\lambda) = m\lambda + \dfrac{\lambda}{N} \;\Rightarrow\; m\Delta\lambda = \dfrac{\lambda}{N}$
$R = \dfrac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = mN$Ghatak 18.69. N = total number of lines illuminated.
Result$R = \dfrac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = mN$. It depends on the order and the total number of lines, not on $d$.
PYQ N22 Q29: 0.15 m ruled with $6\times10^5$ lines/m: $N = 0.15\times6\times10^5 = 90000$; first order $R = 90000$
PYQ N25 Q15: 1 inch, 15000 lines, first order: $R = 15000$; $\Delta\lambda = \dfrac{500}{15000} = 0.033$ nm. Next resolvable wavelength ≈ 500.033 nm
Compare a prismA prism's resolving power is $t\,\dfrac{dn}{d\lambda}$ ($t$ = base length). A 2.5 cm crown-glass prism gives only about 1000.
Exam trapResolving power is $\lambda/\Delta\lambda$ (a big number), not $\Delta\lambda/\lambda$.

T10

Fresnel's half-period zones

Cut the wavefront into rings that arrive alternately in and out of step. PYQ N19 Q20, N24 Q19, N25 Q6.

{{fig:half_period_zones}}
Plane wavefront WW′; point P at distance $d$ from it (O is the foot of the perpendicular). Draw spheres about P of radii $d + \frac\lambda2, d + \frac{2\lambda}{2}, d + \frac{3\lambda}{2}, \ldots$. They cut the wavefront in circles: the zones
Radius of the n-th circle: $r_n^2 = \left(d + \dfrac{n\lambda}{2}\right)^2 - d^2 = n\lambda d + \dfrac{n^2\lambda^2}{4} \approx n\lambda d$Pythagoras; λ ≪ d so drop the λ² term. $r_n = \sqrt{n\lambda d}$ (Ghatak 20.1).
Area of the n-th zone: $\pi r_n^2 - \pi r_{n-1}^2 = \pi\lambda d$Independent of n: all zones have (almost) equal area (20.2).
Exact area difference (PYQ N25 Q6)Keeping the λ² term: area of zone n $= \pi\lambda d + \dfrac{\pi\lambda^2(2n-1)}{4}$. So (area of zone n) − (area of zone n−1) $= \dfrac{\pi\lambda^2}{2}$, which is negligibly small: the areas are practically equal.
Light from neighbouring zones arrives with path difference λ/2, i.e. phase π: their effects at P have opposite signs. Amplitude $u = u_1 - u_2 + u_3 - u_4 + \cdots$"Half-period": half a period apart in time.
Equal areas, but each zone is a little further from P and more tilted, so $u_1 > u_2 > u_3 > \cdots$, decreasing slowly
Group as $\dfrac{u_1}{2} + \left(\dfrac{u_1}{2} - u_2 + \dfrac{u_3}{2}\right) + \left(\dfrac{u_3}{2} - u_4 + \dfrac{u_5}{2}\right) + \cdots$. Each bracket ≈ 0, because each $u_n$ is close to the average of its neighbours
$u \approx \dfrac{u_1}{2}$The whole wavefront gives half the amplitude of the first zone alone (Ghatak 20.12). Intensity is ¼ of the first zone's.
Exam trapZone radii grow as $\sqrt n$ (not equally spaced); zone areas are equal.

In the model paper: Q19 (PYQ N25 Q6), Q20, Q32 (PYQ N19 Q20, N24 Q19).

T11

Straight-line travel, holes and discs

PYQ N22 Q34: rectilinear propagation from half-period zones.

Rectilinear propagation

The light at P is $u_1/2$: it effectively comes only from the first zone (and its neighbours), a tiny disc of radius $\sqrt{\lambda d}$ ≈ 0.5 mm for $d$ = 50 cm, λ = 5000 Å. The outer zones cancel each other in pairs.

So an obstacle much larger than this tiny region throws a sharp shadow, and light seems to travel in straight lines. Only openings or obstacles comparable to $\sqrt{\lambda d}$ show noticeable diffraction.

Result · small circular hole (radius a), on the axis The hole lets through $p = \dfrac{a^2}{\lambda d}$ zones. Odd p: bright (about $u_1$, i.e. 4 times the unobstructed intensity). Even p: nearly dark. Brightest at $d = a^2/\lambda$ (one zone); darkest at $d = a^2/2\lambda$ (two zones).
a = 0.5 mm, λ = $5\times10^{-5}$ cm: brightest at $d = \dfrac{0.0025}{5\times10^{-5}} = 50$ cm; darkest at 25 cm
Opaque disc: Poisson's bright spotA small disc blocks the first p zones. The rest give $u_{p+1} - u_{p+2} + \cdots \approx u_{p+1}/2$, which is never zero. So the centre of the shadow is bright. Poisson (1818) used this to mock Fresnel's theory; Arago then saw the spot, a famous win for the wave theory.

In the model paper: Q21, Q33, Q41 (PYQ N22 Q34).

T12

The zone plate

Block every other zone and you get a lens made of rings. PYQ N22 Q21, N19 Q29, N20 Q30.

{{fig:zone_plate}}
Result · principle and construction Draw circles of radii proportional to $\sqrt1, \sqrt2, \sqrt3, \ldots$ on white paper and blacken alternate rings; photograph it small on glass. For a point P at distance $f = r_1^2/\lambda$, the rings are exactly its half-period zones. The open (odd) zones all send light in step: $u = u_1 + u_3 + u_5 + \cdots$, so P is very bright.
Lens formula: source S at $a$, image at $b$. Edge of zone 1 at height $r_1$: path S→edge→P is λ/2 longer than the straight path: $\dfrac{r_1^2}{2}\left(\dfrac1a + \dfrac1b\right) = \dfrac\lambda2$$\sqrt{a^2+r^2}\approx a + r^2/2a$ (binomial).
$\dfrac1a + \dfrac1b = \dfrac{\lambda}{r_1^2} = \dfrac1f$, with $f = \dfrac{r_1^2}{\lambda}$Same form as a lens (Ghatak 20.20).
More fociAt $f/3$ each open ring holds three zones, so it still adds up; also at $f/5, f/7, \ldots$ (weaker). Between them (at $f/2, f/4$) there are dark points.
PYQ N19 Q29: $r_n = 0.001\sqrt n$ m, λ = $4.5\times10^{-7}$ m: $f = \dfrac{(10^{-3})^2}{4.5\times10^{-7}} = 2.22$ m. Other foci: 0.741, 0.444, 0.317 m
PYQ N20 Q30: $f = 60$ cm, λ = 6000 Å: $r_1 = \sqrt{f\lambda} = \sqrt{60\times6\times10^{-5}} = 0.06$ cm = 0.6 mm
Result · zone plate vs convex lens
Workingdiffraction (zone plate) vs refraction (lens) Focimany ($f, f/3, f/5, \ldots$) vs one Colour$f \propto 1/\lambda$: red focuses nearer than violet; for a glass lens violet focuses nearer Brightnesshalf the area is blocked, so less bright

T13

The straight edge and the Cornu spiral

Why a shadow is never perfectly sharp (Ghatak §20.6).

{{fig:straight_edge}}
The ideaA straight edge blocks half the wavefront. For a point P on the screen, we add up the light from the open half, strip by strip. Strips far from P's line contribute arrows that turn faster and faster, so the arrows curl into a double spiral: the Cornu spiral.
Result · Fresnel integrals $$C(v) = \int_0^v\cos\frac{\pi u^2}{2}\,du,\qquad S(v) = \int_0^v\sin\frac{\pi u^2}{2}\,du$$ Both are 0 at $v = 0$ and tend to $\pm\frac12$ as $v\to\pm\infty$. The curve $(C(v), S(v))$ is the Cornu spiral, winding into the points $Z^\pm = (\pm\frac12, \pm\frac12)$.
{{fig:cornu}}
For a screen point P at height $y$ (shadow edge at $y = 0$), set $v_0 = -\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{\lambda d}}\;y$ ($d$ = distance edge → screen)
Amplitude ∝ the chord from the spiral point $v_0$ to $Z^+$: $\;I = \dfrac{I_0}{2}\left\{\left[\tfrac12 - C(v_0)\right]^2 + \left[\tfrac12 - S(v_0)\right]^2\right\}$Ghatak 20.58. $I_0$ = intensity without the edge (chord $Z^-Z^+$).
At the shadow edge, $v_0 = 0$: $I = \dfrac{I_0}{2}\left(\dfrac14 + \dfrac14\right) = \dfrac{I_0}{4}$The chord is half the full one, so a quarter of the intensity.
Result · the pattern
Deep shadowfalls smoothly to 0 (no fringes) Shadow edge$I_0/4$ Bright sidefringes: maxima at $|v_0|$ = 1.22, 2.34, 3.08 ($1.37I_0$, $1.20I_0$, $1.15I_0$), minima at 1.87, 2.74, …; they crowd together and settle to $I_0$ Positions$y = |v_0|\sqrt{\lambda d/2}$
λ = $6\times10^{-5}$ cm, $d$ = 120 cm: $\sqrt{\lambda d/2} = 0.06$ cm. First two maxima at $1.22\times0.06 = 0.073$ cm and $2.34\times0.06 = 0.140$ cm from the edge of the shadow
