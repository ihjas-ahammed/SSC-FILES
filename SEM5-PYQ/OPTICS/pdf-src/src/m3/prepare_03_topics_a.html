UNIT 12 · GHATAK §18.1–18.2

Light bending round corners

T1

Diffraction; Fresnel and Fraunhofer

What diffraction is, and its two classes (PYQ N25 Q5, N20 Q4).

The idea

When a wave passes an obstacle or an opening, it spreads into the region of the geometrical shadow. This is diffraction. The spreading angle is about $\lambda/b$, where $b$ is the size of the opening.

Sound (λ ≈ 1 m) bends round buildings easily. Light (λ ≈ $5\times10^{-5}$ cm) bends only noticeably at openings a fraction of a millimetre wide. That is why we usually think light goes straight.

{{fig:fresnel_vs_fraunhofer}}
Result · two classes

Fresnel diffraction: the source, or the screen, or both are at finite distances from the aperture. The wavefronts are curved. (Zones, zone plate, straight edge.)

Fraunhofer diffraction: source and screen are effectively at infinite distance: plane waves fall on the aperture and the pattern is seen in the focal plane of a lens. (Single slit, double slit, grating.)

Result · Huygens–Fresnel principleEach point on a wavefront is a source of secondary wavelets, and the wavelets from different points interfere with one another. The disturbance at any point is the sum (with phases) of all the wavelets reaching it.
Interference vs diffractionThere is no basic physical difference: both are adding waves. By custom, "interference" means a few separate sources (two slits), and "diffraction" means a continuous spread of sources across one opening. In a double slit you see both: fringes (interference) inside an envelope (diffraction).
Check yourself
Fill in: In the ____ class of diffraction, the source or screen or both are at finite distances from the aperture.
Fresnel.

T2

The single slit: intensity formula

The derivation asked as a 10-mark essay in 2019, 2020, 2022 and 2024.

{{fig:single_slit_geom}}

A plane wave falls normally on a slit of width $b$. We look at the light leaving at angle θ, collected by a lens at one point of its focal plane.

Divide the slit into $n$ tiny equal strips, spacing Δ, so $b \approx n\Delta$. Each strip sends a wavelet of amplitude $a$Huygens–Fresnel (T1).
Neighbouring strips differ in path by $\Delta\sin\theta$, i.e. in phase by $\phi = \dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta\sin\theta$Path → phase (P1).
Add the $n$ arrows, each turned φ from the last: $E_\theta = a\,\dfrac{\sin(n\phi/2)}{\sin(\phi/2)}$Phasor chain (M1). Ghatak eq. 18.6.
Let $n\to\infty$ with $na = A$ fixed. Then $\dfrac{n\phi}{2} = \dfrac{\pi\,n\Delta\sin\theta}{\lambda} \to \dfrac{\pi b\sin\theta}{\lambda} \equiv \beta$, and $\sin\dfrac{\phi}2 \approx \dfrac{\phi}{2} = \dfrac{\beta}{n}$φ is tiny, so the small-angle rule (M2) applies.
$E_\theta = a\,\dfrac{\sin\beta}{\beta/n} = A\,\dfrac{\sin\beta}{\beta}$Ghatak 18.7. The smooth-arc picture of M1.
$I = I_0\,\dfrac{\sin^2\beta}{\beta^2}$, with $\beta = \dfrac{\pi b\sin\theta}{\lambda}$Square the amplitude. At θ = 0, $\sin\beta/\beta \to 1$, so $I_0$ is the central intensity (Ghatak 18.10).
Result · single-slit Fraunhofer pattern $$I(\theta) = I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2,\qquad \beta = \frac{\pi b\sin\theta}{\lambda}$$
{{fig:single_slit_pattern}}
Same result with an integral (if your teacher used it)Adding infinitely many strips is an integral: $E \propto \int_{-b/2}^{b/2} e^{ikx\sin\theta}dx = b\,\dfrac{\sin\beta}{\beta}$. Both routes give the same answer; Ghatak uses the sum.

T3

Single slit: dark bands, weak bright bands, width

"Find the positions of maximum and minimum intensity."

Minima

$I = 0$ when $\sin\beta = 0$ but $\beta \ne 0$: $\beta = m\pi$, $m = \pm1, \pm2, \ldots$
$\dfrac{\pi b\sin\theta}{\lambda} = m\pi \;\Rightarrow\; b\sin\theta = m\lambda$Ghatak 18.12. m = 0 is NOT a minimum: it is the central maximum.
Why, in words: at $b\sin\theta = \lambda$, split the slit into two halves. Each strip in the top half has a partner in the bottom half $b/2$ lower, whose path is longer by exactly λ/2. Every pair cancelsThe "arc closes into a circle" picture of M1.

Secondary maxima

$\dfrac{dI}{d\beta} = 0$ gives $\sin\beta\,(\beta - \tan\beta) = 0$Differentiate $\sin^2\beta/\beta^2$ (M4).
$\sin\beta = 0$ gives the minima; $\tan\beta = \beta$ gives the maxima: $\beta = 0, 1.43\pi, 2.46\pi, 3.47\pi, \ldots$
Their intensities: $I_0$, $0.047I_0$, $0.016I_0$, $0.008I_0$The first side band is only about 1/21 of the centre.
{{fig:tan_beta}}

Width of the central maximum

First zeros at $\sin\theta = \pm\lambda/b$, so the central band spans $-\lambda/b \le \theta \le \lambda/b$Angular half-width λ/b.
On a screen in the focal plane of a lens of focal length $f$: total width $= \dfrac{2f\lambda}{b}$
Result
Minima$b\sin\theta = m\lambda$, $m = \pm1, \pm2, \ldots$ Secondary maxima$\tan\beta = \beta$; $\beta \approx \pm1.43\pi, \pm2.46\pi$ Central width$2\lambda/b$ (angle), $2f\lambda/b$ (on screen)
Narrower slit or longer wavelength → wider pattern.
Example: $b = 0.2$ mm, λ = 5000 Å, $f = 20$ cm: $\lambda/b = \dfrac{5\times10^{-5}}{0.02} = 2.5\times10^{-3}$ rad
First minimum $20\times2.5\times10^{-3} = 0.05$ cm from the centre; central band 0.1 cm wide; second minimum at 0.1 cm
Exam trapThe secondary maxima are not exactly halfway between minima (not at $1.5\pi$); they are at $1.43\pi$, $2.46\pi$, …

T4

A round hole, and the limit of resolution

Asked in 2019, 2020, 2022 though not in the listed sections (Ghatak §18.3, 18.5).

{{fig:airy}}
Result · Fraunhofer diffraction at a circular aperture

Pattern: concentric rings round a bright central disc (Airy pattern). $I = I_0\left[\dfrac{2J_1(v)}{v}\right]^2$, $v = \dfrac{2\pi a}{\lambda}\sin\theta$ ($a$ = radius).

Dark rings at $v = 3.832, 7.016, \ldots$, i.e. first dark ring at $\sin\theta = \dfrac{0.61\lambda}{a} = \dfrac{1.22\lambda}{D}$. About 84% of the light is in the central disc. Smaller hole → bigger disc.

Result · Rayleigh criterion and resolution Two point sources are just resolved when the central maximum of one falls on the first minimum of the other. For a lens or eye of diameter D, the smallest resolvable angle is $$\Delta\theta_{min} = \frac{1.22\lambda}{D}$$
Eye (PYQ N19 Q28): D = 2 mm, λ = $6\times10^{-7}$ m: $\Delta\theta = \dfrac{1.22\times6\times10^{-7}}{2\times10^{-3}} = 3.66\times10^{-4}$ rad
At 30 m: separation $= 30\times3.66\times10^{-4} = 0.011$ m ≈ 1.1 cm
Microscope and oil immersion (PYQ N19 Q19) A microscope can separate two points a distance $d_{min} = \dfrac{0.61\lambda}{n\sin\alpha}$ apart, where α is the half-angle of the cone of light entering the objective and $n$ is the refractive index between object and lens. $n\sin\alpha$ is the numerical aperture. Filling that space with oil ($n \approx 1.5$) instead of air raises $n\sin\alpha$, so $d_{min}$ falls: finer detail is seen. (Shorter wavelength helps too.)
Loudspeaker (PYQ N19 Q12)The sound spreads over an angle of about $1.22\lambda/D$. To make the sound more directional (a narrow beam), use a loudspeaker of larger diameter compared with the wavelength.
Exam trapFor a round hole use $1.22\lambda/D$, not $\lambda/D$ (that is for a slit).

In the model paper: Q7 (PYQ N19 Q7), Q8 (PYQ N19 Q12), Q29 (PYQ N19 Q19), Q30 (PYQ N19 Q28), Q31 (PYQ N20 Q21, N22 Q22).

T5

Two slits: fringes inside an envelope

Young's fringes and single-slit diffraction at the same time.

Each slit (width $b$) gives amplitude $A\dfrac{\sin\beta}{\beta}$ at angle θ
The second slit is $d$ further on (centre to centre), so its wave is behind by phase $2\gamma$, with $\gamma = \dfrac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}$
Two equal arrows at angle $2\gamma$: total $2A\dfrac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\cos\gamma$Isosceles triangle: resultant $= 2\times$side$\times\cos(\text{half the angle})$.
$I = 4I_0\,\dfrac{\sin^2\beta}{\beta^2}\cos^2\gamma$Ghatak 18.45: single-slit envelope × Young fringes.
{{fig:two_slit_pattern}}
Result · two slits
Bright fringes$d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ Dark fringes$d\sin\theta = (m+\frac12)\lambda$ Envelope zeros$b\sin\theta = p\lambda$
Result · missing orders A bright fringe is missing when it falls exactly on an envelope zero, i.e. when $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ and $b\sin\theta = p\lambda$ at the same θ: $$\frac{d}{b} = \frac{m}{p}$$ If $d = 3b$: orders 3, 6, 9, … are missing, and the central envelope holds $2(d/b) - 1 = 5$ fringes.
Exam trapThe fringe spacing depends on the slit separation $d$; the slit width $b$ only sets the envelope.

In the model paper: Q9, Q26.

T6

N slits: the intensity distribution

PYQ N25 Q21 (10 marks). The same arrow chain, now with N slits.

Each of the $N$ slits gives an arrow of length $A\dfrac{\sin\beta}{\beta}$; neighbours differ in phase by $2\gamma$, $\gamma = \dfrac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}$
Chain of N arrows, each turned $2\gamma$: total $A\dfrac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\cdot\dfrac{\sin N\gamma}{\sin\gamma}$M1 with $\phi = 2\gamma$. Ghatak 18.49.
$I = I_0\,\dfrac{\sin^2\beta}{\beta^2}\cdot\dfrac{\sin^2 N\gamma}{\sin^2\gamma}$Ghatak 18.50. N = 1 gives the single slit; N = 2 gives T5 (since $\sin2\gamma/\sin\gamma = 2\cos\gamma$).
{{fig:nslit_pattern}}
Result · features of the N-slit pattern
Principal maxima$\gamma = m\pi$, i.e. $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$. There $\frac{\sin N\gamma}{\sin\gamma}\to N$, so $I = N^2I_0\frac{\sin^2\beta}{\beta^2}$: all slits in step Minima$N\gamma = p\pi$, $p \ne 0, N, 2N,\ldots$: $d\sin\theta = \frac{\lambda}{N}, \frac{2\lambda}{N}, \ldots, \frac{(N-1)\lambda}{N}, \ldots$ Between two principal maxima$N - 1$ minima and $N - 2$ weak secondary maxima Missing ordersas for two slits, when $b\sin\theta = p\lambda$ coincides
Why principal maxima get sharpHeight grows as $N^2$ but the first zero beside each peak moves in to $\lambda/N$. So with many slits the bright lines become very tall and very thin (T7).
Exam trap"Principal maximum becomes sharper as N increases": true (N22 Q10). "… as N decreases": false (N19 Q6).

T7

Width of a principal maximum

PYQ N24 Q17: "Derive the equation for width of principal maxima of an N slit Fraunhofer diffraction."

The m-th principal maximum is at $d\sin\theta_m = m\lambda$, i.e. $N\gamma = mN\pi$Ghatak 18.60.
The nearest zeros on either side are at $N\gamma = (mN \pm 1)\pi$, i.e. $d\sin(\theta_m \pm \Delta\theta_m) = m\lambda \pm \dfrac{\lambda}{N}$Ghatak 18.61.
$\sin(\theta_m \pm \Delta\theta_m) \approx \sin\theta_m \pm \Delta\theta_m\cos\theta_m$Δθ is tiny: $\sin(A+x)\approx\sin A + x\cos A$ (18.62).
Subtract step 1: $d\,\Delta\theta_m\cos\theta_m = \dfrac{\lambda}{N}$
$\Delta\theta_m = \dfrac{\lambda}{Nd\cos\theta_m}$Ghatak 18.63: angular half-width. Full width $2\Delta\theta_m$.
Result$\Delta\theta_m = \dfrac{\lambda}{Nd\cos\theta_m}$. $Nd$ is the total width of the grating, so a wider grating (more lines) gives sharper lines.

In the model paper: Q25 (PYQ N24 Q17).
