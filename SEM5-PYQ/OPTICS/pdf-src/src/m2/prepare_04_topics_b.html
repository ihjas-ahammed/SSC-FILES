UNIT 8 · GHATAK §14.7–14.8

Two sources from one: mirrors and prisms

T7

Fresnel's two mirrors

Division of wavefront with two mirrors (in the syllabus; not yet asked).

{{fig:two_mirror}}
The ideaTwo plane mirrors M₁, M₂ meet at a line M, tilted by a very small angle θ. A slit S sends light onto both. Each mirror forms an image of S: S₁ and S₂. The two reflected beams look as if they come from S₁ and S₂, two copies of one source: coherent. They overlap and give Young-type fringes.
$MS_1 = MS = MS_2 = b$A plane-mirror image is as far behind the mirror as the object is in front; the meeting line M lies on both mirrors, so M is the same distance from S and from each image.
So S, S₁ and S₂ lie on a circle with centre M and radius b
$\angle S_1MS_2 = 2\theta$Turning the mirror by θ turns the image about M by 2θ (P5).
$d = S_1S_2 = b\cdot2\theta = 2b\theta$Arc ≈ chord for a small angle: radius × angle (M3).
With the screen at distance c from M, $D = b + c$ and $\beta = \dfrac{\lambda D}{d} = \dfrac{\lambda(b + c)}{2b\theta}$
Result $$d = 2b\theta,\qquad \beta = \frac{\lambda(b+c)}{2b\theta}$$
Exam trap$S_1S_2 = 2b\theta$, not $b\theta$; and $D = b + c$, not just c.
Check yourself
Why are S₁ and S₂ coherent?
They are images of the same slit S, so every phase jump of S happens in both at once.

T8

Fresnel's biprism

How it works and how it measures λ (PYQ N20 Q19, N19 Q26).

{{fig:biprism}}
The ideaA biprism is two thin prisms joined base to base (obtuse angle nearly 180°; each refracting angle α is tiny, about 30′). Light from a slit S falling on the upper half is bent down; on the lower half, bent up. Seen from the right, the light seems to come from two virtual sources S₁ and S₂, copies of S: coherent.
Each half bends rays by $\delta = (n-1)\alpha$Thin prism (P5).
With the slit at distance a from the biprism, each virtual source is shifted sideways by $a\delta = a(n-1)\alpha$Small angle: sideways shift = distance × angle (M3).
So $d = S_1S_2 = 2a(n-1)\alpha$
Screen (eyepiece) at distance b beyond the biprism: $D = a + b$, so $\beta = \dfrac{\lambda(a+b)}{2a(n-1)\alpha}$
Result $$d = 2a(n-1)\alpha,\quad \beta = \frac{\lambda D}{d},\quad \lambda = \frac{\beta\,d}{D}$$
Example (PYQ N19 Q26): $n = 1.5$, α = 1° $= 0.01745$ rad, $a = 10$ cm, $b = 100$ cm, λ = 5000 Å
$d = 2\times10\times0.5\times0.01745 = 0.1745$ cm; $D = 110$ cm
$\beta = \dfrac{5\times10^{-5}\times110}{0.1745} = 0.0315$ cm $\approx 0.32$ mm

Measuring λ with a biprism

The set-upSlit, biprism and micrometer eyepiece stand on an optical bench. The slit is lit by sodium light and made parallel to the edge of the biprism, so fringes appear in the eyepiece.
β: move the eyepiece cross-wire across, say, 20 fringes and read the micrometer; β = distance ÷ 20
D: read from the bench scale (slit to eyepiece)
d: lens displacement method (below)
$\lambda = \beta d/D$
{{fig:lens_displacement}}

Why d = √(d₁d₂)

Keep slit and eyepiece fixed, distance D apart. A convex lens of focal length f placed between them forms a sharp image when $u + v = D$ and the lens formula holdsLens formula (P5), with distances as magnitudes.
This gives a quadratic, $u^2 - Du + Df = 0$, which has two real roots only if $D^2 \ge 4Df$, i.e. $D \ge 4f$So choose a lens with $f < D/4$.
The two positions swap u and v. Image separations: $d_1 = d\,\dfrac{v}{u}$, $\;d_2 = d\,\dfrac{u}{v}$Magnification = v/u.
Multiply: $d_1d_2 = d^2$ ⟹ $d = \sqrt{d_1d_2}$
Exam trap$d = \sqrt{d_1d_2}$ (geometric mean), not $(d_1 + d_2)/2$. In $d = 2a(n-1)\alpha$, a is the slit-to-biprism distance, and α must be in radians.
Check yourself
d₁ = 0.6 cm, d₂ = 0.15 cm. d?
$\sqrt{0.09} = 0.3$ cm.

White light, Lloyd, and a half-wave jump

T9

Interference with white light

Why you see only a few coloured fringes.

{{fig:white_fringes}}
At the centre O, Δ = 0 for every wavelength: all colours are bright there ⟹ the central fringe is white
Moving out, Δ grows. At Δ ≈ $2\times10^{-5}$ cm (half the violet wavelength) violet is cancelled: the edge looks reddish
At Δ ≈ $3.5\times10^{-5}$ cm (half the red wavelength) red is cancelled: the next region looks bluish. So the first few fringes are coloured, violet on the inside, red on the outside
Far out (say Δ = $30\times10^{-5}$ cm) many different wavelengths are bright (Δ = mλ) and many dark at the same point. The mixture looks white again: uniform illuminationThe fringes of different colours overlap and wash out.
ResultWhite light gives a white central fringe, then a few coloured fringes, then uniform white. Use: the white central fringe marks exactly where Δ = 0 (to locate the zero-order fringe, for example when measuring a fringe shift, T6).
Check yourself
At a point where Δ = 5000 Å, which visible colour is brightest?
Only λ = 5000 Å (green) has Δ = mλ in the visible; the point looks greenish.

T10

Lloyd's mirror

One real source, its mirror image as the second (PYQ N22 Q26).

{{fig:lloyd}}
The ideaA slit S is placed a small height h above a long flat glass plate (the mirror). Light grazing the mirror reflects and seems to come from the image S′, a distance h below the mirror. S and S′ are coherent, $d = 2h$ apart. Direct and reflected light overlap near the mirror and give fringes.
Fringe width as in Young: $\beta = \dfrac{\lambda D}{2h}$d = 2h; D = source-to-screen distance.
Expected: where the screen touches the mirror (O), the paths are equal, so a bright fringe
Observed: the fringe at O is dark. Every bright and dark fringe is swappedSeen by moving the screen up to the end of the mirror.
Conclusion: reflection at the glass (denser) surface adds a phase change of π (half a wave) to the reflected wave
Result · Lloyd's mirror
Dark$S'P - SP = m\lambda$ Bright$S'P - SP = (m+\frac12)\lambda$ Width$\beta = \lambda D/2h$
Central fringe dark. The fringes appear only on one side of the mirror plane.
Example (PYQ N22 Q26): h = 2 mm, so d = 4 mm; λ = 589 nm; D = 1.5 m
$\beta = \dfrac{589\times10^{-9}\times1.5}{4\times10^{-3}} = 2.21\times10^{-4}$ m $= 0.221$ mm
Exam trapd = 2h, not h. Lloyd's centre is dark, opposite to Young.

T11

Phase change on reflection: Stokes relations

Why only one of the two reflections gets the half-wave jump.

{{fig:stokes}}
The idea · reversibilityIf you reverse a ray (send it back along its path) it retraces its path exactly. Stokes applied this to a ray that splits into a reflected and a transmitted part.

Let $r, t$ be the amplitude ratios for reflection and transmission from medium 1, and $r', t'$ from medium 2.

A wave of amplitude a in medium 1 gives a reflected wave $ar$ and a transmitted wave $at$
Reverse both. The reversed $ar$ splits into $ar\cdot r$ (back along the original incident line) and $ar\cdot t$ (into medium 2). The reversed $at$ splits into $at\cdot t'$ (into medium 1) and $at\cdot r'$ (into medium 2)Each reversed wave hits the surface and divides again.
Reversibility says the result must be just the original wave a, travelling backwards in medium 1
Medium 1: $ar^2 + att' = a$ ⟹ $tt' = 1 - r^2$
Medium 2 (nothing should emerge): $art + atr' = 0$ ⟹ $r' = -r$
Result · Stokes relations $$r' = -r,\qquad tt' = 1 - r^2$$ The minus sign = a phase change of π. So the reflections from the two sides of a surface differ by π. Lloyd's mirror shows it is the reflection from the denser medium that gets the π; the reflection from the rarer side gets none.
Same result from Fresnel's formulas (normal incidence)

$r = \dfrac{n_1 - n_2}{n_1 + n_2}$, $\;t = \dfrac{2n_1}{n_1 + n_2}$. Swapping $n_1, n_2$: $r' = \dfrac{n_2 - n_1}{n_1 + n_2} = -r$, $t' = \dfrac{2n_2}{n_1 + n_2}$.

Check: $1 - tt' = 1 - \dfrac{4n_1n_2}{(n_1+n_2)^2} = \dfrac{(n_1 - n_2)^2}{(n_1 + n_2)^2} = r^2$ ✓. And $r < 0$ when $n_2 > n_1$: the π change on reflection from a denser medium.

Result · the rule used in all thin films
Rare → densereflected wave gets π (adds λ/2) Dense → rareno phase change Transmittednever a phase change
Check yourself
Light in air reflects from glass (n = 1.5) at normal incidence. Fraction of intensity reflected?
$r = -0.5/2.5 = -0.2$; $r^2 = 0.04$: 4%, with a phase change of π.
