1. Homomorphisms

An isomorphism keeps the operation and is one-to-one and onto. A homomorphism keeps the operation and need not be either. So every isomorphism is a homomorphism, but not the other way round.

Definition 8.1 Let $\phi : G \to G'$ be a map between groups. $\phi$ is a homomorphism if $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$ for all $a, b \in G$.

Example 8.2. The map $\phi : \mathbb{R} \to U$ given by $\phi(x) = \cos(2\pi x) + i\sin(2\pi x) = e^{2\pi ix}$ is a homomorphism. The identity formulas for sines and cosines give $\phi(a + b) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$. It maps onto $U$ but is not one-to-one, so it is not an isomorphism. Also $\phi(-x) = (\phi(x))^{-1}$.

Example 8.3. $\phi : U_{28} \to U_4$ given by $\phi(z) = z^7$ is well defined and a homomorphism: $(z_1z_2)^7 = z_1^7z_2^7$, and $z^{28} = 1$ gives $(z^7)^4 = 1$.

Recall. For a map $\phi : X \to Y$, a subset $A$ of $X$ has an image $\phi[A]$ and a subset $B$ of $Y$ has an inverse image $\phi^{-1}[B]$. The inverse image can be nonempty even when $\phi$ is not onto.

  1. Take $\phi(x) = x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$, $B = \{4\}$. Then $\phi^{-1}[B] = \{2, -2\}$, although $4$ is in the image.
  2. Take $A = \{-1, 1\}$. Then $\phi[A] = \{1\}$.

Definition 8.4 Let $\phi : X \to Y$, $A \subseteq X$, $B \subseteq Y$. The image of $A$ is $\phi[A] = \{\phi(a) \mid a \in A\}$. The inverse image of $B$ is $\phi^{-1}[B] = \{a \in X \mid \phi(a) \in B\}$.

The book gives proofs of the four properties in Theorem 8.5 because they are not obvious for maps that are neither one-to-one nor onto. For an isomorphism they are obvious: an isomorphism only relabels.

Theorem 8.5 Let $\phi : G \to G'$ be a homomorphism.
  1. $\phi(e) = e'$, the identity of $G'$.
  2. $\phi(a^{-1}) = \phi(a)^{-1}$ for every $a \in G$.
  3. If $H \le G$, then $\phi[H] \le G'$.
  4. If $K' \le G'$, then $\phi^{-1}[K'] \le G$.

Proof in brief. (1) $\phi(e) = \phi(ee) = \phi(e)\phi(e)$; cancel. (2) $e' = \phi(aa^{-1}) = \phi(a)\phi(a^{-1})$. (3) $\phi(a)\phi(b) = \phi(ab)$ keeps $\phi[H]$ closed; (1) and (2) give the identity and inverses. (4) If $a, b \in \phi^{-1}[K']$ then $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b) \in K'$, and $\phi(a^{-1}) = \phi(a)^{-1} \in K'$; also $\phi(e) = e' \in K'$. ◆

With $\{e'\}$ in place of $K'$, Theorem 8.5 (4) says $\phi^{-1}[\{e'\}]$ is a subgroup of $G$.

Definition 8.6 For a homomorphism $\phi : G \to G'$ the subgroup $\phi^{-1}[\{e'\}] = \{x \in G \mid \phi(x) = e'\}$ is the kernel of $\phi$, written $\operatorname{Ker}(\phi)$.

The kernel is used later in the book to define the alternating group, and the case $H = G$ of Theorem 8.5 (3) says $\phi[G]$ is a subgroup of $G'$. That case is used in Cayley’s Theorem.

Example 8.7. In Example 8.2 the kernel of $\phi$ is the set of integers, since $e^{2\pi ix} = 1$ exactly when $x$ is an integer. For $n \ge 1$, $\phi[\langle \frac{1}{n} \rangle] = U_n$.

Example 8.8. $\phi : \mathbb{Z}_n \to D_n$ given by $\phi(k) = \rho^k$ is a homomorphism. The two cases $a + b < n$ and $a + b \ge n$ are checked separately, using $\rho^n = \iota$. Its image is $\langle \rho \rangle$.

2. Cayley’s Theorem

Many groups we have met are isomorphic to groups of permutations. $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is isomorphic to $\langle (1, 2, \dots, n) \rangle \le S_n$. $D_n$ is a group of permutations of $\mathbb{Z}_n$. $GL(n, \mathbb{R})$ permutes the vectors of $\mathbb{R}^n$. A subgroup of a permutation group is called a group of permutations. Cayley’s Theorem says every group is isomorphic to one.

The idea comes from the group table. Each row contains every element once. So each row defines a permutation of the group: put the table head on top and the row for $a$ below. For $D_3$ (Table 8.9) the row for $\mu\rho$ gives

$$\begin{pmatrix} \iota & \rho & \rho^2 & \mu & \mu\rho & \mu\rho^2 \\ \mu\rho & \mu\rho^2 & \mu & \rho^2 & \iota & \rho \end{pmatrix}.$$

Table 8.9. The group table of $D_3$. Rows and columns are headed $\iota, \rho, \rho^2, \mu, \mu\rho, \mu\rho^2$ in that order:

$D_3$$\iota$$\rho$$\rho^2$$\mu$$\mu\rho$$\mu\rho^2$
$\iota$$\iota$$\rho$$\rho^2$$\mu$$\mu\rho$$\mu\rho^2$
$\rho$$\rho$$\rho^2$$\iota$$\mu\rho^2$$\mu$$\mu\rho$
$\rho^2$$\rho^2$$\iota$$\rho$$\mu\rho$$\mu\rho^2$$\mu$
$\mu$$\mu$$\mu\rho$$\mu\rho^2$$\iota$$\rho^2$$\rho$
$\mu\rho$$\mu\rho$$\mu\rho^2$$\mu$$\rho^2$$\iota$$\rho$
$\mu\rho^2$$\mu\rho^2$$\mu$$\mu\rho$$\rho$$\rho^2$$\iota$

Let $\lambda_x$ be the permutation given by row $x$: $\lambda_x(g)$ is the entry in row $x$, column $g$, that is $xg$. This makes sense for infinite groups too.

Definition 8.10 Let $G$ be a group. The map $\phi : G \to S_G$ given by $\phi(x) = \lambda_x$, where $\lambda_x(g) = xg$ for all $g \in G$, is the left regular representation of $G$.

Each $\lambda_x$ is a permutation. It is one-to-one, because $xa = xb$ gives $a = b$ by cancellation. It is onto, because $\lambda_x(x^{-1}b) = b$.

Theorem 8.11 (Cayley’s Theorem) Every group is isomorphic to a group of permutations.

Proof in brief. $\phi$ is one-to-one: if $\lambda_a = \lambda_b$ then $ae = be$, so $a = b$. $\phi$ is a homomorphism: $\lambda_{ab}(g) = (ab)g = a(bg) = (\lambda_a\lambda_b)(g)$. By Theorem 8.5 (3) with $H = G$, $\phi[G]$ is a subgroup of $S_G$, and $\phi : G \to \phi[G]$ is an isomorphism. ◆

Example 8.12. The proof gives a subgroup of $S_G$ for any $G$, but not always the smallest one. $D_n$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $S_{\mathbb{Z}_n}$, although the proof gives a subgroup of $S_{D_n}$ (which has $2n$ elements, not $n$). Also $\mathbb{Z}_6$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $S_5$, since $(1, 2, 3)(4, 5)$ has order $6$.

The right regular representation is defined next.

Historical note Arthur Cayley (1821–1895) gave an abstract-sounding definition of a group in 1854, and in 1878 stated what is now Theorem 8.11 in a paper that argued from the group table. Walther von Dyck’s axiomatic definition of 1882 gave the abstract theory its start. The book’s full note is kept in the excerpt.
Definition 8.13 Let $G$ be a group. Put $\sigma_x(g) = gx$. The map $\tau : G \to S_G$ given by $\tau(x) = \sigma_{x^{-1}}$ is the right regular representation of $G$.

The right regular representation also proves Cayley’s Theorem. The book leaves the details to Exercise 54, which is printed right after this paragraph.

Exercise 54, Section 8 (named after Definition 8.13) Prove Cayley’s Theorem using the right regular representation rather than the left regular representation.

3. Transpositions and orbits

Definition 8.14 A cycle of length $2$ is a transposition.

A transposition fixes every element except two, which it swaps. A computation shows

$$\begin{aligned}(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n) &= (a_1, a_n)(a_1, a_{n-1}) \cdots \\ &\quad (a_1, a_3)(a_1, a_2).\end{aligned}$$

So every cycle of length $n$ is a product of $n - 1$ transpositions.

Theorem 8.15 Every permutation of a finite set with at least two elements is a product of transpositions.

In plain terms: any rearrangement of $n$ objects can be produced by swapping pairs.

Example 8.16. $(1, 6)(2, 5, 3) = (1, 6)(2, 3)(2, 5)$.

Example 8.17. In $S_n$ with $n \ge 2$, the identity is $(1, 2)(1, 2)$.

A product of transpositions for a given permutation is not unique (we can always insert $(1, 2)(1, 2)$). Later in the book it is shown that the number of transpositions is always even or always odd. That proof uses orbits, which are defined here.

For $\sigma \in S_A$ and $a \in A$, the orbit of $a$ is $\{\sigma^k(a) \mid k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$. For a permutation in disjoint cycle form, the orbit of $a$ is the set of entries in the cycle that contains $a$.

Example 8.18. Let $\sigma = (1, 2, 6)(3, 5) \in S_6$. The orbit of $1$ is $\{1, 2, 6\}$, and the same set is the orbit of $2$ and of $6$. The orbit of $3$ is $\{3, 5\}$. The orbit of $4$ is $\{4\}$, since $\sigma = (1, 2, 6)(3, 5)(4)$ when $1$-cycles are included.
