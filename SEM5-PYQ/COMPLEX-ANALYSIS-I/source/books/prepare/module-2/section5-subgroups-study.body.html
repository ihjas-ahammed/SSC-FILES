Before you start

  • The four group properties: closure, associativity, identity and inverses (Section 2 of the book, Definition 2.1).
  • Matrices and determinants: $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$, and a matrix is invertible exactly when its determinant is not $0$ (Example 5.14 uses this).
  • Complex numbers: modulus and the unit circle (Examples 5.5 and 5.26).

1. What a subgroup is

Subgroups inside groups (Definition 5.1)

Some groups sit inside bigger groups. The integers under addition sit inside the real numbers under addition. The positive rationals under multiplication do not sit inside the real numbers under addition, even though every positive rational is a real number. The reason is that the operation changes: $2 \cdot 3 = 6$ in the first group, but $2 + 3 = 5$ in the second. A subgroup must keep the same operation.

Definition 5.1 If a subset $H$ of a group $G$ is closed under the operation of $G$, and $H$ with that induced operation is itself a group, then $H$ is a subgroup of $G$. Write $H \le G$ for "$H$ is a subgroup of $G$", and $H < G$ when $H \le G$ and $H \ne G$.

In short: $H$ is a subgroup when the group operation of $G$ keeps you inside $H$, and $H$ satisfies the group axioms by itself.

$\langle \mathbb{Z}, + \rangle < \langle \mathbb{R}, + \rangle$. But $\langle \mathbb{Q}^+, \cdot \rangle$ is not a subgroup of $\langle \mathbb{R}, + \rangle$, because the operation is different, even though $\mathbb{Q}^+ \subset \mathbb{R}$ as sets. Every group $G$ has $G$ itself and $\{e\}$ as subgroups.

Definition 5.2 The subgroup $G$ of $G$ itself is the improper subgroup. All other subgroups are proper. The subgroup $\{e\}$ is the trivial subgroup. All other subgroups are nontrivial.

Examples of subgroups

Example 5.3. In the additive group $\mathbb{R}^n$ of $n$-component row vectors, the vectors whose first component is $0$ form a subgroup.

Example 5.4. $\mathbb{Q}^+$ under multiplication is a proper subgroup of $\mathbb{R}^+$ under multiplication.

Example 5.5. The $n$th roots of unity $U_n$ form a subgroup of $U$, the complex numbers of absolute value $1$. And $U$ is in turn a subgroup of $\mathbb{C}^*$, the nonzero complex numbers under multiplication.

Example 5.6. $D_n$ is the set of one-to-one maps $\phi$ of $\mathbb{Z}_n$ onto itself that keep the edges of the regular $n$-gon $P_n$. $D_n$ is a subset of $S_{\mathbb{Z}_n}$, and both are groups under composition, so $D_n \le S_{\mathbb{Z}_n}$.

Example 5.7. There are two different groups of order $4$ (Exercise 20 of Section 2, printed right after this example). The first is $\mathbb{Z}_4$. The second is the Klein 4-group $V$. Their tables are Tables 5.8 and 5.9.

The only nontrivial proper subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}_4$ is $\{0, 2\}$. The set $\{0, 3\}$ is not a subgroup, since $3 + 3 = 2$ and $2 \notin \{0, 3\}$. The group $V$ has three nontrivial proper subgroups: $\{e, a\}$, $\{e, b\}$ and $\{e, c\}$. The set $\{e, a, b\}$ is not a subgroup, because $ab = c$ and $c$ is not in the set.

Subgroup diagrams

A subgroup diagram shows which subgroups sit inside which. A line going down from $G$ to $H$ means $H$ is a subgroup of $G$. The bigger group goes higher.

Figure 5.10 shows two diagrams. In (a), $\mathbb{Z}_4$ is at the top, then $\{0, 2\}$, then $\{0\}$ at the bottom: a single chain. In (b), $V$ is at the top, the three subgroups $\{e, a\}$, $\{e, b\}$, $\{e, c\}$ sit in the middle row, and $\{e\}$ is at the bottom.

Notice that if $H \le G$ and $a \in H$, then the equation $ax = a$ has one solution in $H$, the identity of $H$. It also has one solution in $G$. These must be the same element, so the identity of $H$ is the identity of $G$. The same kind of argument shows that the inverse of $a$ in $H$ is the inverse of $a$ in $G$.

Checking whether a subset is a subgroup

Example 5.11. The continuous real-valued functions form a subgroup of the additive group of all real-valued functions. The sum of two continuous functions is continuous, the zero function is continuous, and the negative of a continuous function is continuous.

We want a routine that works for every example. The next theorem gives it.

Theorem 5.12 A subset $H$ of a group $G$ is a subgroup of $G$ if and only if:
  1. $H$ is closed under the operation of $G$;
  2. the identity $e$ of $G$ is in $H$; and
  3. for every $a \in H$, $a^{-1} \in H$.

Proof in brief. If $H$ is a subgroup, the three conditions hold by definition. In the other direction, conditions 2 and 3 give the identity and inverse axioms in $H$. Associativity is inherited from $G$, because the equation $(ab)c = a(bc)$ is already true in $G$.

Example 5.13. The differentiable functions form a subgroup of the additive group of all real-valued functions, by the same argument as Example 5.11.

Example 5.14. In the multiplicative group $G$ of invertible $n \times n$ complex matrices, the matrices of determinant $1$ form a subgroup $T$. The reason: $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$ keeps $T$ closed under multiplication, $I_n$ has determinant $1$, and if $\det(A) = 1$ then $\det(A^{-1}) = 1$. Theorem 5.12 then applies.

Recall. For square matrices $A$ and $B$ of the same size, $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$. A square matrix $A$ is invertible exactly when $\det(A) \ne 0$.

Re-derive.

  1. Take $B = A^{-1}$. Then $\det(A)\det(A^{-1}) = \det(AA^{-1}) = \det(I_n) = 1$.
  2. So $\det(A) = 1$ forces $\det(A^{-1}) = 1$.

Saved. Example 5.14 (the matrices of determinant $1$ form a subgroup).

Theorem 5.15 gives another test for a nonempty set. It says: a nonempty subset $H$ of $G$ is a subgroup if and only if $ab^{-1}$ lies in the set whenever $a$ and $b$ do. The book states the theorem with $G$ in both places, as printed; the condition that is meant is "$a, b \in H$ and $ab^{-1} \in H$". Its proof is Exercise 51. In practice Theorem 5.12 is about as quick.

Exercise 51, Section 5 (the proof of Theorem 5.15) Prove Theorem 5.15.

Theorem 5.16 is the fastest test when the set is finite. A finite nonempty subset of $G$ is a subgroup if and only if it is closed under the operation of $G$. Its proof is Exercise 57.

Exercise 57, Section 5 (the proof of Theorem 5.16) Prove Theorem 5.16.

Example 5.17. $U_n$ is a finite nonempty subset of $\mathbb{C}^*$, and if $z_1, z_2 \in U_n$ then $(z_1z_2)^n = 1$. So $U_n$ is closed, and by Theorem 5.16 it is a subgroup.

Example 5.18. The set $H = \{\iota, \rho, \rho^2, \dots, \rho^{n-1}\}$ is a subgroup of $D_n$, because $\rho^k\rho^r = \rho^{k +_n r}$ stays in $H$.

2. Cyclic subgroups

Building a subgroup from one element

Suppose a subgroup $H$ of $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ contains $3$. Then it contains $3 + 3 = 6$, then $6 + 3 = 9$, and the inverses $9$ and $6$. The set $\{0, 3, 6, 9\}$ is a subgroup, and it is the smallest subgroup that contains $3$.

The same idea works in any group. If $a \in G$ and $H$ is a subgroup containing $a$, then $H$ must contain every power $a^n$ with $n \in \mathbb{Z}$: the positive powers by closure, $a^0 = e$ as the identity, and the negative powers as inverses. The powers need not all be different. In $V$, $a^2 = e$, $a^3 = a$, $a^4 = e$, and $a^{-1} = a$.

Theorem 5.19. Let $a \in G$. Then $H = \{a^n \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a subgroup of $G$. It is the smallest subgroup of $G$ that contains $a$: every subgroup containing $a$ contains $H$.

Proof in brief. $a^ra^s = a^{r+s}$, so $H$ is closed. $a^0 = e \in H$. And $a^{-r}$ is in $H$ with $a^{-r}a^r = e$. By Theorem 5.12, $H \le G$. Any subgroup containing $a$ contains all of $H$, so $H$ is the smallest.

The footnote in the book makes a careful distinction. A subset is minimal with a property if no smaller subset has it. A subset is the smallest if it is contained in every subset with the property. $\{e, a\}$, $\{e, b\}$ and $\{e, c\}$ are all minimal nontrivial subgroups of $V$, but $V$ has no smallest nontrivial subgroup.

Definition 5.20 Let $a \in G$. The subgroup $\{a^n \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ of Theorem 5.19 is the cyclic subgroup of $G$ generated by $a$, written $\langle a \rangle$.

Example 5.21. In $D_{10}$, $(\mu\rho^k)^2 = \iota$ and $(\mu\rho^k)^{-1} = \mu\rho^k$. So $\langle \mu\rho^k \rangle = \{\iota, \mu\rho^k\}$. Since $\rho^{10} = \iota$ and $\rho^{-1} = \rho^9$, we get $\langle \rho \rangle = \{\iota, \rho, \rho^2, \dots, \rho^9\}$.

Definition 5.22 An element $a$ generates $G$ and is a generator for $G$ if $\langle a \rangle = G$. A group $G$ is cyclic if some element of $G$ generates it.

Example 5.23. $\mathbb{Z}_4$ is cyclic. Both $1$ and $3$ generate it: $\langle 1 \rangle = \langle 3 \rangle = \mathbb{Z}_4$. The group $V$ is not cyclic, because each of $\langle a \rangle$, $\langle b \rangle$, $\langle c \rangle$ has only two elements.

Example 5.24. $\mathbb{Z}$ under addition is cyclic. Its only generators are $1$ and $-1$. For $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$, $\mathbb{Z}_n$ under addition modulo $n$ is cyclic, and $1$ and $n - 1$ are generators (there may be others).

Example 5.25. In $\mathbb{Z}$ under addition, $\langle 3 \rangle$ is the set of all multiples of $3$. We write it $3\mathbb{Z}$. In the same way $n\mathbb{Z} = \langle n \rangle$. For example $6\mathbb{Z} < 3\mathbb{Z}$.

Example 5.26. $U_n$ is the set of $n$th roots of unity. They are equally spaced on the unit circle (Figure 5.27). The point $\zeta = \cos\frac{2\pi}{n} + i\sin\frac{2\pi}{n}$ generates $U_n$: raising $\zeta$ to successive powers moves counterclockwise and lands on every root in turn. So $U_n = \langle \zeta \rangle$ is cyclic.

Figure 5.27 is the picture described in Example 5.26. The points $1, \zeta, \zeta^2, \zeta^3$ and so on sit around the circle, with $i$ marked at one point. The figure is not redrawn here.
