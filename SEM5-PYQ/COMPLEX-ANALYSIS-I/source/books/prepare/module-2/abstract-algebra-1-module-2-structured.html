1. Subgroups

Definition 5.1

Definition 5.1 Let $G$ be a group. A subset $H$ of $G$ that is closed under the binary operation of $G$, and that is a group under the induced operation, is a subgroup of $G$. Write $H \le G$; write $H < G$ if $H \le G$ and $H \ne G$.

Remark after Definition 5.1. $\langle \mathbb{Z}, + \rangle < \langle \mathbb{R}, + \rangle$. The set $\mathbb{Q}^+ \subset \mathbb{R}$, but $\langle \mathbb{Q}^+, \cdot \rangle \not\le \langle \mathbb{R}, + \rangle$, because the operation differs.

Every group $G$ has $G$ and $\{e\}$ as subgroups.

Definition 5.2 The subgroup $G$ is the improper subgroup of $G$; every other subgroup is proper. The subgroup $\{e\}$ is the trivial subgroup; every other subgroup is nontrivial.

Examples 5.3 to 5.7

Example 5.3. In the additive group $\mathbb{R}^n$, the set of row vectors with first component $0$ is a subgroup.

Example 5.4. $\langle \mathbb{Q}^+, \cdot \rangle$ is a proper subgroup of $\langle \mathbb{R}^+, \cdot \rangle$.

Example 5.5. $U_n \le U \le \mathbb{C}^*$, where $U_n$ is the set of $n$th roots of unity, $U$ is the set of complex numbers of absolute value $1$, and $\mathbb{C}^*$ is the group of nonzero complex numbers under multiplication.

Example 5.6. $D_n \subseteq S_{\mathbb{Z}_n}$, and both are groups under composition; hence $D_n \le S_{\mathbb{Z}_n}$.

Example 5.7. Up to isomorphism there are two groups of order $4$ (see Exercise 20 of Section 2, reproduced right after this example). They are $\mathbb{Z}_4$ (Table 5.8) and the Klein $4$-group $V$ (Table 5.9). In $\mathbb{Z}_4$ the only nontrivial proper subgroup is $\{0, 2\}$; $\{0, 3\}$ is not a subgroup because it is not closed. In $V$ the nontrivial proper subgroups are $\{e, a\}$, $\{e, b\}$, $\{e, c\}$; $\{e, a, b\}$ is not a subgroup because $ab = c$.

Subgroup diagrams and two remarks

A subgroup diagram places the larger group higher, with a line from $G$ down to $H$ whenever $H \le G$. Figure 5.10. (a) The subgroup diagram of $\mathbb{Z}_4$: $\mathbb{Z}_4 - \{0, 2\} - \{0\}$. (b) The subgroup diagram of $V$: $V$ above the three subgroups $\{e, a\}$, $\{e, b\}$, $\{e, c\}$, above $\{e\}$.

Let $H \le G$ and $a \in H$. The equation $ax = a$ has a unique solution in $H$, namely the identity of $H$; it has a unique solution in $G$, namely the identity $e$ of $G$. Hence the identity of $H$ is $e$. Applying the same argument to $ax = e$ shows that the inverse of $a$ in $H$ is the inverse of $a$ in $G$.

Subgroup tests

Example 5.11. The continuous real-valued functions form a subgroup of the additive group $F$ of all real-valued functions on $\mathbb{R}$.

Theorem 5.12 A subset $H$ of a group $G$ is a subgroup if and only if (1) $H$ is closed under the operation of $G$, (2) $e \in H$, and (3) $a \in H$ implies $a^{-1} \in H$.

Proof. Necessity follows from Definition 5.1. For sufficiency, (2) and (3) give $\mathcal{G}_2$ and $\mathcal{G}_3$ in $H$, and $\mathcal{G}_1$ holds in $H$ because it holds in $G$. ◆

Example 5.13. The differentiable functions form a subgroup of $F$, by Theorem 5.12.

Example 5.14. Let $G$ be the multiplicative group of invertible $n \times n$ complex matrices and $T = \{A \in G \mid \det(A) = 1\}$. The identity $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$ shows that $T$ is closed; $\det(I_n) = 1$; and $\det(A) = 1$ gives $\det(A^{-1}) = 1$. Hence $T \le G$ by Theorem 5.12.

2. Cyclic subgroups

In $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$, any subgroup containing $3$ contains $3$, $6$, $9$, $0$; the set $\{0, 3, 6, 9\}$ is a subgroup and is the smallest one containing $3$.

In a group $G$, a subgroup containing $a$ contains $a^n$ for every $n \in \mathbb{Z}$: positive powers by closure, $a^0 = e$, and negative powers as inverses. Powers need not be distinct: in $V$, $a^2 = e$, $a^3 = a$, $a^4 = e$, $a^{-1} = a$.

Theorem 5.19 Let $a \in G$. Then $H = \{a^n \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a subgroup of $G$, and it is the smallest subgroup of $G$ containing $a$: every subgroup containing $a$ contains $H$.

Proof. $a^ra^s = a^{r+s}$, $a^0 = e$, and $(a^r)^{-1} = a^{-r} \in H$; by Theorem 5.12, $H \le G$. Any subgroup containing $a$ contains $H$ by the remark above. ◆

Footnote on the book’s terms: a subset is minimal with a property if no proper subset has it; it is the smallest if it is contained in every subset with the property. $\{e, a\}$, $\{e, b\}$, $\{e, c\}$ are minimal nontrivial subgroups of $V$; $V$ has no smallest nontrivial subgroup.

Definition 5.20 For $a \in G$, the subgroup $\{a^n \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ of Theorem 5.19 is the cyclic subgroup generated by $a$, denoted $\langle a \rangle$.

Example 5.21. In $D_{10}$: $\langle \mu\rho^k \rangle = \{\iota, \mu\rho^k\}$, since $(\mu\rho^k)^2 = \iota$; and $\langle \rho \rangle = \{\iota, \rho, \dots, \rho^9\}$, since $\rho^{-1} = \rho^9$ and $\rho^{10} = \iota$.

Definition 5.22 $a$ generates $G$, and is a generator for $G$, if $\langle a \rangle = G$. $G$ is cyclic if it has a generator.

Example 5.23. $\mathbb{Z}_4$ is cyclic with generators $1$ and $3$. $V$ is not cyclic: $\langle a \rangle$, $\langle b \rangle$, $\langle c \rangle$ are proper of order $2$.

Example 5.24. $\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic with generators $1$ and $-1$ only. $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is cyclic; for $n > 1$, $1$ and $n - 1$ are generators.

Example 5.25. $\langle 3 \rangle = 3\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{Z}$; in general $n\mathbb{Z} = \langle n \rangle$, and $6\mathbb{Z} < 3\mathbb{Z}$.

Example 5.26. $U_n = \langle \zeta \rangle$ with $\zeta = \cos\frac{2\pi}{n} + i\sin\frac{2\pi}{n}$. Powers of $\zeta$ move counterclockwise and reach each element of $U_n$ in turn (Figure 5.27). $U_n$ is cyclic, and $\zeta$ is a generator.

1. Elementary properties

Theorem 6.1 Every cyclic group is abelian.

Proof. Let $G = \langle a \rangle$ and $g_1 = a^r$, $g_2 = a^s$. Then $g_1g_2 = a^{r+s} = a^{s+r} = g_2g_1$. ◆

Multiplicative notation is used throughout this section, whether or not the group is abelian.

2. The division algorithm and the gcd

Theorem 6.2 (Division Algorithm for $\mathbb{Z}$) If $m \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ and $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, there exist unique $q, r \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $n = mq + r$ and $0 \le r < m$.

Proof. The book argues from the number line (Figure 6.3): either $n = qm$, or $n$ lies strictly between $qm$ and $(q+1)m$ for a unique $q$, and $r = n - qm$. ◆

Figure 6.3. Number-line diagrams for the cases $n \ge 0, q \ge 0$ and $n < 0, q < 0$, with the remainder $r$ marked. Labels as printed; not redrawn.

Example 6.4. $38 = 7(5) + 3$: quotient $5$, remainder $3$.

Example 6.5. $-38 = 7(-6) + 4$: quotient $-6$, remainder $4$.

Theorem 6.6 A subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic.

Proof. Let $G = \langle a \rangle$ and $H \le G$. If $H = \{e\}$, then $H = \langle e \rangle$. Otherwise let $m \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ be least with $a^m \in H$, and put $c = a^m$. For $b = a^n \in H$, write $n = mq + r$, $0 \le r < m$. Then $a^r = c^{-q}b \in H$, so $r = 0$ by minimality, and $b = c^q$. Thus $H = \langle c \rangle$. ◆

Corollary 6.7 The subgroups of $\langle \mathbb{Z}, + \rangle$ are precisely the groups $n\mathbb{Z}$, $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
Exercise 54, Section 6 (named in the remark after Corollary 6.7) Let $r$ and $s$ be integers. Show that $\{nr + ms \mid n, m \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$.
Definition 6.8 Let $r \in \mathbb{Z}^+$, $s \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$. The positive generator $d$ of $H = \{nr + ms \mid n, m \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is the greatest common divisor $\gcd(r, s)$.

Since $r, s \in d\mathbb{Z}$, $d$ is a common divisor; since $d = nr + ms$, every common divisor of $r$ and $s$ divides $d$. Hence $d$ is the greatest common divisor. The identity $d = nr + ms$ is Bézout’s identity.

Example 6.9. $\gcd(42, 72) = 6 = (3)(72) + (-5)(42)$. Integers $r, s$ are relatively prime if $\gcd(r, s) = 1$.

$$\text{If } \gcd(r,s) = 1 \text{ and } r \mid sm, \text{ then } r \mid m. \qquad (1)$$

Proof of (1). $1 = ar + bs$ gives $m = arm + bsm$; $r$ divides both terms. ◆

3. Structure of cyclic groups

Theorem 6.10 Let $G = \langle a \rangle$. If $|G| = \infty$, then $G \cong \langle \mathbb{Z}, + \rangle$. If $|G| = n$, then $G \cong \langle \mathbb{Z}_n, +_n \rangle$.

Proof. Case I: $a^m \ne e$ for all $m > 0$. Then $a^h = a^k$ forces $h = k$, and $\phi(a^i) = i$ is a well-defined bijection $G \to \mathbb{Z}$ with $\phi(a^ia^j) = \phi(a^i) + \phi(a^j)$. Case II: let $n$ be least positive with $a^n = e$. Then $a^0, \dots, a^{n-1}$ are distinct and exhaust $G$; $\psi(a^i) = i$ is a bijection $G \to \mathbb{Z}_n$ with $\psi(a^ia^j) = \psi(a^i) +_n \psi(a^j)$. ◆

Figures 6.11 and 6.12. The elements of a cyclic group of order $n$ placed evenly on a circle; labels are $a^0, a, \dots, a^{n-1}$ in 6.11 and $0, 1, \dots, n-1$ in 6.12. Not redrawn.

Example 6.13. Multiplication is addition of positions around the circle: $a^ha^k = a^r$, where $h + k = nq + r$ and $0 \le r < n$. This is the isomorphism $\langle a \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}_n$.

Consequently, the order of $a \in G$ is the least $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ with $a^n = e$.

Example 6.14. The order of a $k$-cycle is $k$.

4. Subgroups of finite cyclic groups

Theorem 6.15 Let $G$ be cyclic of order $n$ with generator $a$, and let $b = a^s$. Then $b$ generates a cyclic subgroup of order $n/d$, where $d = \gcd(n, s)$. Moreover $\langle a^s \rangle = \langle a^t \rangle$ if and only if $\gcd(s, n) = \gcd(t, n)$.

Proof. $|\langle b \rangle|$ is the least $m > 0$ with $n \mid ms$. Write $d = un + vs$; then $\gcd(n/d, s/d) = 1$, and by (1) the least such $m$ is $n/d$. The second statement follows from the uniqueness of the subgroup of each order $n/d$ in $\mathbb{Z}_n$. ◆

Example 6.16. In $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$: $\langle 3 \rangle$ has $4$ elements, $\langle 8 \rangle$ has $3$, and $5$ generates the whole group.

Corollary 6.17 If $a$ generates a finite cyclic group of order $n$, then its generators are the $a^r$ with $\gcd(r, n) = 1$.

Example 6.18. The subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}_{18}$ are $\langle 1 \rangle = \mathbb{Z}_{18}$, $\langle 2 \rangle$ (order $9$), $\langle 3 \rangle$ (order $6$), $\langle 6 \rangle = \{0, 6, 12\}$, $\langle 9 \rangle = \{0, 9\}$ and $\langle 0 \rangle$. Figure 6.19 gives the subgroup diagram: $\langle 1 \rangle$ above $\langle 2 \rangle$ and $\langle 3 \rangle$; $\langle 2 \rangle$ above $\langle 6 \rangle$; $\langle 3 \rangle$ above $\langle 6 \rangle$ and $\langle 9 \rangle$; $\langle 6 \rangle$ and $\langle 9 \rangle$ above $\langle 0 \rangle$. Labels as printed; not redrawn.

Corollary 6.20 If $G$ is finite and cyclic and $H \le G$, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$.

Proof. By Theorem 6.6 and Theorem 6.15, $|H| = n/\gcd(n, s)$ for some $s$, which divides $n$. ◆

Example 6.21. The subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}_{28}$ have orders $1, 2, 4, 7, 14, 28$, and each order occurs.

Corollary 6.20 holds without cyclicity; Lagrange’s Theorem (Section 10) gives this for all finite groups.

1. Homomorphisms

Definition 8.1 A map $\phi : G \to G'$ of groups is a homomorphism if $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$ for all $a, b \in G$.

Example 8.2. $\phi : \mathbb{R} \to U$, $\phi(x) = \cos(2\pi x) + i\sin(2\pi x) = e^{2\pi ix}$, is a homomorphism. It maps onto $U$ and is not one-to-one, so it is not an isomorphism. Moreover $\phi(-x) = (\phi(x))^{-1}$.

Example 8.3. $\phi : U_{28} \to U_4$, $\phi(z) = z^7$, is a well-defined homomorphism: $z^{28} = 1$ gives $(z^7)^4 = 1$, and $(z_1z_2)^7 = z_1^7z_2^7$.

Definition 8.4 For $\phi : X \to Y$, $A \subseteq X$, $B \subseteq Y$: $\phi[A] = \{\phi(a) \mid a \in A\}$ is the image of $A$; $\phi^{-1}[B] = \{a \in X \mid \phi(a) \in B\}$ is the inverse image of $B$.
Theorem 8.5 Let $\phi : G \to G'$ be a homomorphism.
  1. $\phi(e) = e'$.
  2. $\phi(a^{-1}) = \phi(a)^{-1}$ for all $a \in G$.
  3. $H \le G$ implies $\phi[H] \le G'$.
  4. $K' \le G'$ implies $\phi^{-1}[K'] \le G$.

Proof. (1) $\phi(e) = \phi(e)\phi(e)$, so $\phi(e) = e'$. (2) $e' = \phi(a)\phi(a^{-1})$. (3) $\phi(a)\phi(b) = \phi(ab) \in \phi[H]$; (1) and (2) supply the identity and inverses. (4) If $a, b \in \phi^{-1}[K']$, then $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b) \in K'$, and $\phi(a^{-1}) = \phi(a)^{-1} \in K'$; also $e' \in K'$. ◆

Definition 8.6 For a homomorphism $\phi : G \to G'$, the subgroup $\operatorname{Ker}(\phi) = \phi^{-1}[\{e'\}] = \{x \in G \mid \phi(x) = e'\}$ is the kernel of $\phi$.

The kernel is used in the book to define the alternating group. The case $H = G$ of Theorem 8.5 (3) gives that $\phi[G]$ is a subgroup of $G'$, which is used in Cayley’s Theorem.

Example 8.7. In Example 8.2, $\operatorname{Ker}(\phi) = \mathbb{Z}$. For $n \ge 1$, $\phi[\langle \frac{1}{n} \rangle] = U_n$.

Example 8.8. $\phi : \mathbb{Z}_n \to D_n$, $\phi(k) = \rho^k$, is a homomorphism (cases $a + b < n$ and $a + b \ge n$, using $\rho^n = \iota$), with image $\langle \rho \rangle$.

2. Cayley’s Theorem

Several standard groups are groups of permutations: $\mathbb{Z}_n \cong \langle (1, 2, \dots, n) \rangle \le S_n$; $D_n$ acts on $\mathbb{Z}_n$; $GL(n, \mathbb{R})$ acts on $\mathbb{R}^n$. Cayley’s Theorem says that every group is isomorphic to a group of permutations. The proof uses the group table: the row of $a$ defines a permutation of $G$. For $D_3$ (Table 8.9), the row for $\mu\rho$ gives $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} \iota & \rho & \rho^2 & \mu & \mu\rho & \mu\rho^2 \\ \mu\rho & \mu\rho^2 & \mu & \rho^2 & \iota & \rho \end{smallmatrix}\right)$.

Write $\lambda_x(g) = xg$. This is well defined for infinite groups as well as finite ones.

Definition 8.10 The map $\phi : G \to S_G$, $\phi(x) = \lambda_x$, is the left regular representation of $G$.

Each $\lambda_x$ is a permutation: it is one-to-one by cancellation ($xa = xb \Rightarrow a = b$), and onto since $\lambda_x(x^{-1}b) = b$.

Theorem 8.11 (Cayley’s Theorem) Every group is isomorphic to a group of permutations.

Proof. If $\lambda_a = \lambda_b$, then $ae = be$, so $a = b$: $\phi$ is one-to-one. $\lambda_{ab}(g) = (ab)g = a(bg) = (\lambda_a\lambda_b)(g)$, so $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$. By Theorem 8.5 (3) with $H = G$, $\phi[G] \le S_G$, and $\phi : G \to \phi[G]$ is an isomorphism. ◆

Example 8.12. The subgroup from the proof is not always the smallest. $D_n$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $S_{\mathbb{Z}_n}$, while the proof gives a subgroup of $S_{D_n}$. And $\mathbb{Z}_6 \cong \langle (1, 2, 3)(4, 5) \rangle \le S_5$.

The right regular representation is defined in Definition 8.13.

Historical note Arthur Cayley (1821–1895) gave an abstract definition of a group in 1854. In 1878 he stated Theorem 8.11, with a proof based on the group table. Walther von Dyck’s axiomatic definition of 1882 led to the development of abstract group theory. The book’s full note is kept in the excerpt.
Definition 8.13 With $\sigma_x(g) = gx$, the map $\tau : G \to S_G$, $\tau(x) = \sigma_{x^{-1}}$, is the right regular representation of $G$.

It can also be used to prove Cayley’s Theorem. See Exercise 54, printed next.

3. Transpositions, orbits

Definition 8.14 A cycle of length $2$ is a transposition.
$$\begin{aligned}(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n) &= (a_1, a_n)(a_1, a_{n-1}) \cdots \\ &\quad (a_1, a_3)(a_1, a_2).\end{aligned}$$

Hence every $n$-cycle is a product of $n - 1$ transpositions.

Theorem 8.15 Every permutation of a finite set with at least two elements is a product of transpositions.

Example 8.16. $(1, 6)(2, 5, 3) = (1, 6)(2, 3)(2, 5)$.

Example 8.17. In $S_n$ ($n \ge 2$), $\iota = (1, 2)(1, 2)$.

The representation of a permutation as a product of transpositions is not unique. The even or odd parity of the number of transpositions is studied in the book from 8.19 onward (Module III, not in these notes), which uses the orbits defined below.

For $\sigma \in S_A$ and $a \in A$, the orbit of $a$ is $\{\sigma^k(a) \mid k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$. For $\sigma \in S_n$, the orbit of $a$ is the set of entries of the cycle containing $a$ in the disjoint-cycle form of $\sigma$.

Example 8.18. For $\sigma = (1, 2, 6)(3, 5) \in S_6$: the orbits of $1$, $2$, $6$ are $\{1, 2, 6\}$; the orbits of $3$, $5$ are $\{3, 5\}$; the orbit of $4$ is $\{4\}$, since $\sigma = (1, 2, 6)(3, 5)(4)$ when $1$-cycles are included.
