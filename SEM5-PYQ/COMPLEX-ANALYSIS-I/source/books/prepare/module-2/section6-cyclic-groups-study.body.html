Before you start

  • Cyclic subgroups $\langle a \rangle$ and generators (Section 5, Theorem 5.19 and Definitions 5.20 and 5.22).
  • Integers: dividing one integer by another with a remainder. Every integer $n$ has the form $mq + r$. This is the division algorithm that opens the section.
  • Isomorphisms: a one-to-one, onto map that keeps the operation (Section 2).

1. Cyclic groups are abelian

In Section 5 a cyclic group was defined as a group with a generator. This section studies cyclic groups in detail. The first fact is that they are always abelian.

Theorem 6.1 Every cyclic group is abelian.

Why. Let $G = \langle a \rangle$. Any two elements are $a^r$ and $a^s$. Then $a^ra^s = a^{r+s} = a^{s+r} = a^sa^r$. So $G$ is abelian. ◆

From here on we keep multiplicative notation, even though these groups are abelian.

The division algorithm is school arithmetic. Divide $n$ by $m$ and you get a quotient $q$ and remainder $r$ with $0 \le r < m$. Multiplying by $m$ gives the form we need.

2. The division algorithm and the greatest common divisor

Theorem 6.2 (Division Algorithm for $\mathbb{Z}$) If $m$ is a positive integer and $n$ is any integer, there are unique integers $q$ and $r$ with $n = mq + r$ and $0 \le r < m$.

Idea of the proof. Mark the multiples of $m$ on a number line and place $n$. Either $n$ is a multiple $qm$ (take $r = 0$), or $n$ lies between two multiples of $m$; let $qm$ be the first multiple to the left of $n$ and let $r$ be the distance from $qm$ to $n$. Uniqueness: a multiple of $m$ to the left of $n$ at distance less than $m$ is unique. ◆

Figure 6.3. The number-line picture for the proof. Two cases are drawn: $n \ge 0$ with $q \ge 0$, and $n < 0$ with $q < 0$. In each the remainder $r$ is the distance from $qm$ to $n$. Labels as printed; not redrawn.

In the notation of Theorem 6.2, $q$ is the quotient and $r$ is the nonnegative remainder.

Example 6.4. For $38$ divided by $7$: $38 = 35 + 3 = 7(5) + 3$. So $q = 5$ and $r = 3$.

Example 6.5. For $-38$ divided by $7$: $-38 = -42 + 4 = 7(-6) + 4$. So $q = -6$ and $r = 4$.

We shall use the division algorithm to show that a subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic. The natural generator to try is a suitable power of the generator $a$.

Theorem 6.6 A subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic.

Idea of the proof. Let $G = \langle a \rangle$ and $H \le G$. If $H = \{e\}$, it is cyclic. Otherwise let $m$ be the smallest positive integer with $a^m \in H$. Put $c = a^m$. For any $b = a^n \in H$, write $n = mq + r$ with $0 \le r < m$. Then $a^r = (a^m)^{-q}a^n \in H$. Minimality of $m$ forces $r = 0$, so $b = c^q$. Hence $H = \langle c \rangle$. ◆

Examples 5.24 and 5.25 showed that $\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic and that $n\mathbb{Z}$ is the cyclic subgroup generated by $n$. Theorem 6.6 says these are the only subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}$.

Corollary 6.7 The subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}$ under addition are precisely the groups $n\mathbb{Z}$, for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

This gives a clean definition of the greatest common divisor. The set $H = \{nr + ms \mid n, m \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ (Exercise 54), so it is cyclic with a positive generator $d$.

Exercise 54, Section 6 (named after Corollary 6.7) Let $r$ and $s$ be integers. Show that $\{nr + ms \mid n, m \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$.
Definition 6.8 Let $r$ be a positive integer and $s \ge 0$ an integer. The positive generator $d$ of $H = \{nr + ms \mid n, m \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is the greatest common divisor of $r$ and $s$, written $d = \gcd(r, s)$.

Why is $d$ the greatest common divisor? Since $r = 1r + 0s$ and $s = 0r + 1s$ lie in $H = d\mathbb{Z}$, the number $d$ divides both $r$ and $s$. And since $d \in H$, we can write $d = nr + ms$. Any common divisor of $r$ and $s$ divides the right-hand side, so it divides $d$. So $d$ is the largest common divisor.

The identity $d = nr + ms$ is called Bézout's identity.

Example 6.9. The positive divisors of $42$ are $1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 14, 21, 42$; those of $72$ are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12, 18, 24, 36, 72$. So $\gcd(42, 72) = 6$, and $6 = (3)(72) + (-5)(42)$. Two integers are relatively prime when their gcd is $1$.

The next fact is needed for subgroups of cyclic groups. It is labelled (1) in the book:

$$\text{If } r \text{ and } s \text{ are relatively prime and } r \mid sm, \text{ then } r \mid m. \qquad (1)$$

Proof of (1): write $1 = ar + bs$, multiply by $m$ to get $m = arm + bsm$. Both terms on the right are divisible by $r$, since $r \mid sm$. So $r \mid m$.

3. The structure of cyclic groups

Theorem 6.10 Let $G$ be cyclic with generator $a$. If $G$ is infinite, then $G \cong \langle \mathbb{Z}, + \rangle$. If $G$ has finite order $n$, then $G \cong \langle \mathbb{Z}_n, +_n \rangle$.

Proof in brief. Case I: no positive power of $a$ is $e$. Then every element is $a^m$ for exactly one $m \in \mathbb{Z}$, and $\phi(a^i) = i$ is an isomorphism onto $\mathbb{Z}$, since $\phi(a^ia^j) = i + j$. Case II: $a^m = e$ for some $m > 0$. Let $n$ be the least such $m$. Then $a^0, a, \dots, a^{n-1}$ are distinct and exhaust $G$, and $\psi(a^i) = i$ is an isomorphism onto $\mathbb{Z}_n$, since $a^ia^j = a^k$ with $k = i +_n j$. ◆

Figures 6.11 and 6.12. Each shows the elements of a cyclic group of order $n$ spaced evenly around a circle. In 6.11 the points are labelled $a^0 = e, a, a^2, a^3, \dots, a^{n-1}$; in 6.12 the same points carry the exponents $0, 1, 2, 3, \dots, n-1$. Labels as printed; not redrawn.

Example 6.13. Think of $a^h$ as the point $h$ units counterclockwise from $e$. To multiply $a^h$ by $a^k$, start at $a^h$ and move $k$ more units. Write $h + k = nq + r$ with $0 \le r < n$. Going around the circle $q$ full times brings you back to the start, so you finish at $a^r$. This is the isomorphism between $\langle a \rangle$ and $\mathbb{Z}_n$.

So the order of an element $a$ of any group is the least positive $n$ with $a^n = e$, as promised earlier.

Example 6.14. The order of a $k$-cycle $\sigma = (a_1, a_2, \dots, a_k)$ is $k$. Applying $\sigma$ moves each entry to the next. Each entry returns to itself only after $k$ applications, and not sooner.

4. Subgroups of finite cyclic groups

Theorem 6.15 Let $G$ be cyclic with $n$ elements, generated by $a$, and let $b = a^s$. Then $b$ generates a cyclic subgroup $H$ of $G$ with $n/d$ elements, where $d = \gcd(n, s)$. Also, $\langle a^s \rangle = \langle a^t \rangle$ if and only if $\gcd(s, n) = \gcd(t, n)$.

Proof in brief. $H$ has as many elements as the least $m > 0$ with $b^m = e$, that is with $n \mid ms$. Write $d = un + vs$; then $n/d$ and $s/d$ are relatively prime. The least $m$ with $\frac{m(s/d)}{n/d} \in \mathbb{Z}$ is $n/d$, by (1). Thus $|H| = n/d$. Since $\mathbb{Z}_n$ has only one subgroup of each order $n/d$, the second statement follows. ◆

Example 6.16. In $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$: $\gcd(3, 12) = 3$, so $\langle 3 \rangle = \{0, 3, 6, 9\}$ has $12/3 = 4$ elements. $\gcd(8, 12) = 4$, so $\langle 8 \rangle = \{0, 4, 8\}$ has $3$ elements. $\gcd(5, 12) = 1$, so $5$ generates all of $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$.

Corollary 6.17 If $a$ generates a finite cyclic group $G$ of order $n$, the other generators of $G$ are the elements $a^r$ with $\gcd(r, n) = 1$.

Example 6.18. Find the subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}_{18}$. By Corollary 6.17 the generators are $1, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17$. Next, $\langle 2 \rangle = \{0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16\}$ has order $9$, and its generators are $h2$ with $h$ relatively prime to $9$, namely $2, 4, 8, 10, 14, 16$. The element $6$ of $\langle 2 \rangle$ generates $\{0, 6, 12\}$, and $12$ also generates it. That accounts for $0, 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 16, 17$. The rest are $3, 9, 15$: $\langle 3 \rangle = \{0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15\}$, which $15$ also generates, and $\langle 9 \rangle = \{0, 9\}$.

Figure 6.19. The subgroup diagram of $\mathbb{Z}_{18}$. At the top is $\langle 1 \rangle = \mathbb{Z}_{18}$. Below it are $\langle 2 \rangle$ and $\langle 3 \rangle$. Below these are $\langle 6 \rangle$ and $\langle 9 \rangle$; $\langle 2 \rangle$ joins $\langle 6 \rangle$, $\langle 3 \rangle$ joins both $\langle 6 \rangle$ and $\langle 9 \rangle$. At the bottom is $\langle 0 \rangle$, joined to $\langle 6 \rangle$ and $\langle 9 \rangle$. Labels as printed; not redrawn.

The example is long because every step is shown. The exercises give practice with the same method.

Corollary 6.20 If $G$ is a finite cyclic group and $H \le G$, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$.

Proof. $H$ is cyclic by Theorem 6.6, generated by some $h = g^s$, where $g$ generates $G$. By Theorem 6.15, $|H| = n/\gcd(n, s)$, which divides $n$. ◆

Example 6.21. The possible orders of subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}_{28}$ are the divisors of $28 = 2^2 \cdot 7$: $1, 2, 4, 7, 14, 28$. Each occurs: $\langle 0 \rangle$ has $1$, $\langle 14 \rangle$ has $2$, $\langle 7 \rangle$ has $4$, $\langle 4 \rangle$ has $7$, $\langle 2 \rangle$ has $14$, and $\langle 1 \rangle = \mathbb{Z}_{28}$ has $28$.

Corollary 6.20 holds for every finite group, not only cyclic ones. Lagrange’s Theorem (Section 10 of the book, outside this module) gives that in general.
