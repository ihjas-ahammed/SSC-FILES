Section 16: Isomorphism Theorems (16.1–16.10)
Printed pp. 145–148 (16.1–16.10; Figures 16.1, 16.9; Exercise 10 is named in 16.7 and printed on page 149)

Prerequisites

  • Normal subgroups and factor groups $G/N$ (Module V material; used here only as stated).
  • Homomorphisms, kernels and kernel cosets (Module 2, Section 8; Module 3, Theorem 10.17).

1. First isomorphism theorem

Figure 16.1. Triangle diagram: $\phi : G \to \phi[G]$, $\gamma_K : G \to G/K$, and a dashed isomorphism $\mu : G/K \to \phi[G]$. Labels as printed; not redrawn.

Theorem 16.2 (First Isomorphism Theorem) Let $\phi : G \to G'$ be a homomorphism with kernel $K$, and $\gamma_K : G \to G/K$ canonical. There is a unique isomorphism $\mu : G/K \to \phi[G]$ with $\phi(x) = \mu(\gamma_K(x))$ for all $x \in G$.

2. Joins and a lemma

For $H, N \le G$ put $HN = \{hn \mid h \in H, n \in N\}$. The join $H \vee N$ is the smallest subgroup containing $HN$.

Lemma 16.3 If $N \trianglelefteq G$ and $\gamma : G \to G/N$ is canonical, then $L \mapsto \gamma[L]$ is a bijection from the normal subgroups of $G$ containing $N$ onto the normal subgroups of $G/N$.

Proof. By Theorem 13.18 and Theorem 10.17, $\gamma^{-1}[\gamma[L]] = L$ for every $L \supseteq N$, which gives injectivity. For surjectivity, $\gamma[\gamma^{-1}[H]] = H$ for every normal $H \le G/N$, and $\gamma^{-1}[H]$ contains $N$. ■

Lemma 16.4 If $N \trianglelefteq G$ and $H \le G$, then $H \vee N = HN = NH$. If $H \trianglelefteq G$ also, then $HN \trianglelefteq G$.

Proof. For $h_1, h_2 \in H$, $n_1, n_2 \in N$, write $n_1h_2 = h_2n_3$ with $n_3 \in N$; then $(h_1n_1)(h_2n_2) = (h_1h_2)(n_3n_2)$, and $(hn)^{-1} = h^{-1}n_4$ with $n_4 \in N$. So $HN \le G$; the same argument gives $NH \le G$. If $H$ is normal, $ghng^{-1} = (ghg^{-1})(gng^{-1}) \in HN$. ◆

3. Second isomorphism theorem

Theorem 16.5 If $H \le G$ and $N \trianglelefteq G$, then $(HN)/N \simeq H/(H \cap N)$.

Proof. $\phi(h) = hN$ is a homomorphism $H \to HN/N$, onto, with kernel $H \cap N$. Apply Theorem 16.2. ◆

Example 16.6. In $\mathbb{Z}^3$ with $H = \mathbb{Z}^2 \times \{0\}$ and $N = \{0\} \times \mathbb{Z}^2$: $HN = \mathbb{Z}^3$ and $H \cap N = \{0\} \times \mathbb{Z} \times \{0\}$; both quotients are $\simeq \mathbb{Z}$.

Example 16.7. In $\mathbb{Z}$ with $N = \langle n \rangle$, $H = \langle h \rangle$: $N + H = \langle \gcd(n, h) \rangle$ and $N \cap H = \langle \operatorname{lcm}(n, h) \rangle$. The second isomorphism theorem gives $\langle \gcd(n,h) \rangle/\langle n \rangle \simeq \langle h \rangle/\langle \operatorname{lcm}(n,h) \rangle$, and both are cyclic of order $n/\gcd(n,h)$. Hence $nh = \gcd(n,h)\operatorname{lcm}(n,h)$.

Exercise 10 (named in Example 16.7). Use one of the Isomorphism Theorems to prove that if $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ and $a \mid b$, then $|a\mathbb{Z}/b\mathbb{Z}| = b/a$.

4. Third isomorphism theorem

Theorem 16.8 If $H, K \trianglelefteq G$ and $K \le H$, then $G/H \simeq (G/K)/(H/K)$.

Proof. $\phi(gK) = gH$ is well defined since $gK \subseteq gH$; it is a homomorphism, onto, with kernel $H/K$. Apply Theorem 16.2: $\mu((gK)(H/K)) = gH$. ◆

Figure 16.9 (printed on page 148): an ellipse for $G$ with the cosets of $H$ and of $K$ drawn inside it. No labels; not redrawn.

Example 16.10. In $\mathbb{Z}_8$ with $H = \{0, 2, 4, 6\}$ and $K = \{0, 4\}$: $G/K = \{\{0,4\},\{1,5\},\{2,6\},\{3,7\}\}$, $G/H = \{\{0,2,4,6\},\{1,3,5,7\}\}$, $H/K = \{\{0,4\},\{2,6\}\}$, and $(G/K)/(H/K) = \{\{\{0,4\},\{2,6\}\},\{\{1,5\},\{3,7\}\}\}$. The isomorphism sends $\{\{0,4\},\{2,6\}\} \mapsto \{0,4,2,6\}$ and $\{\{1,5\},\{3,7\}\} \mapsto \{1,5,3,7\}$.

Section 22: Rings and Fields (22.1–22.18)
Printed pp. 185–191 (22.1–22.18; Exercises 30, 36, 37, 38, 45, 50 named in the text)

Prerequisites

  • Abelian groups; the additive group of a ring.
  • Division algorithm and remainders (Module 2, Theorem 6.2).
  • Plus Two recall: distributive law in school algebra (status OPEN, check against the Kerala text).

1. Rings

Definition 22.1 A ring $\langle R, +, \cdot \rangle$: (R1) $\langle R, + \rangle$ is an abelian group; (R2) multiplication is associative; (R3) for all $a, b, c \in R$, $a(b + c) = ab + ac$ and $(a + b)c = ac + bc$.

Example 22.2. $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$ are rings.

Example 22.3. $M_n(R)$ is a ring for any ring $R$; it is noncommutative for $n \ge 2$.

Example 22.4. $F = \{f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\}$ with pointwise operations is a ring.

Exercise 36 (named in Example 22.4). Show that the multiplication defined on the set $F$ of functions in Example 22.4 satisfies axioms $\mathcal{R}_2$ and $\mathcal{R}_3$ for a ring.

Example 22.5. $n\mathbb{Z}$ is a ring.

Example 22.6. $\mathbb{Z}_n$ with multiplication modulo $n$ is a ring.

Example 22.7. Direct products of rings, by components, are rings.

2. Elementary properties

Theorem 22.8 In any ring: (1) $0a = a0 = 0$; (2) $a(-b) = (-a)b = -(ab)$; (3) $(-a)(-b) = ab$.

Proof. (1) $a0 + a0 = a(0+0) = a0$ gives $a0 = 0$ by cancellation in $\langle R, + \rangle$. (2) $a(-b) + ab = a(-b + b) = 0$. (3) By (2) twice, $(-a)(-b) = -(-(ab)) = ab$. ◆

Exercise 30 (named after Theorem 22.8). What is wrong with the following attempt at a proof of Property 2 in Theorem 22.8? $(-a)b = ((-1)a)b = (-1)(ab) = -(ab)$.

3. Homomorphisms and isomorphisms

Definition 22.9 A map $\phi : R \to R'$ of rings is a homomorphism if $\phi(a + b) = \phi(a) + \phi(b)$ and $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$ for all $a, b$. Its kernel is $\{a \in R \mid \phi(a) = 0'\}$.

$\phi$ is a group homomorphism of the additive groups, so one-to-one iff the kernel is $\{0\}$.

Example 22.10. The evaluation $\phi_a(f) = f(a)$ on $F$ is a ring homomorphism.

Exercise 37 (named in Example 22.10). Show that the evaluation map $\phi_a$ of Example 22.10 is a ring homomorphism.

Example 22.11. $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}_n$, $a \mapsto a \bmod n$, is a ring homomorphism.

Definition 22.12 A ring isomorphism is a bijective ring homomorphism.

Exercise 38 (named after Definition 22.12). Complete the argument outlined after Definitions 22.12 to show that isomorphism gives an equivalence relation on a collection of rings.

Example 22.13. $x \mapsto 2x$ is a group isomorphism $\mathbb{Z} \to 2\mathbb{Z}$ but not a ring homomorphism.

4. Units and fields

Definition 22.14 A commutative ring has commutative multiplication; a ring with unity has a multiplicative identity $1$ (unity).

Example 22.15. If $\gcd(r, s) = 1$, $\mathbb{Z}_{rs} \simeq \mathbb{Z}_r \times \mathbb{Z}_s$ as rings.

Exercise 45 (named in Example 22.15). Show that the multiplicative inverse of a unit in a ring with unity is unique.

Definition 22.16 A unit of a ring with unity $1 \ne 0$ has a multiplicative inverse. A division ring has every nonzero element a unit. A field is a commutative division ring; a noncommutative one is a strictly skew field.

Example 22.17. The units of $\mathbb{Z}_{14}$ are $1, 3, 5, 9, 11, 13$.

Example 22.18. $\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field; $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$ are.

Exercise 50 (named after Example 22.18). Show that a subset $S$ of a ring $R$ gives a subring of $R$ if and only if $0 \in S$, $a - b \in S$ for all $a, b \in S$, and $ab \in S$ for all $a, b \in S$.

Section 23: Integral Domains (23.1–23.14)
Printed pp. 194–196 (23.1–23.14; Figure 23.10; Exercises 26 and 35 named in the text)

Prerequisites

  • Rings, units and fields (Section 22 notes).
  • gcd and relative primeness (Module 2, Section 6).
  • Plus Two recall: factorising a quadratic (status OPEN, check against the Kerala text).

1. Divisors of zero and cancellation

Example 23.1. In $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$, $x^2 - 5x + 6 = (x - 2)(x - 3)$ and $(2)(6) = (3)(4) = 0$, so the equation has the roots $2, 3, 6, 11$.

Definition 23.2 Nonzero $a, b$ in a ring $R$ with $ab = 0$ are divisors of $0$ (or $0$ divisors).

In $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ the divisors of $0$ are $2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10$. A unit is never a divisor of $0$.

Theorem 23.3 For $m \in \mathbb{Z}_n$, either $m = 0$; or $\gcd(m, n) = 1$ and $m$ is a unit; or $\gcd(m, n) \ne 1$ and $m$ is a $0$ divisor.

Proof. If $d = \gcd(m, n) \ne 1$ and $m \ne 0$, then $m \cdot (n/d) = (m/d)n = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}_n$, with $m$ and $n/d$ nonzero. If $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, write $an + bm = 1$, $b = nq + r$, $0 \le r \le n - 1$; then $rm = 1 - n(a + qm) = 1$ in $\mathbb{Z}_n$. ◆

Example 23.4. In $\mathbb{Z}_{20}$ the units are $1, 3, 7, 9, 11, 13, 17, 19$ and the $0$ divisors are $2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18$.

Corollary 23.5 If $p$ is prime, every nonzero element of $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is a unit; $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is a field with no divisors of $0$.

Proof. Apply Theorem 23.3 with $\gcd(m, p) = 1$ for $0 < m \le p - 1$. ■

Theorem 23.6 Cancellation holds in a ring $R$ if and only if $R$ has no divisors of $0$.

Proof. If $ab = 0$ with $a \ne 0$, cancellation applied to $ab = a0$ gives $b = 0$. Conversely, if $ab = ac$ with $a \ne 0$ and no divisors of $0$, then $a(b - c) = 0$ gives $b = c$. ◆

In a ring with no divisors of $0$, $ax = b$ with $a \ne 0$ has at most one solution. In a commutative ring $b/a$ denotes $a^{-1}b = ba^{-1}$.

2. Integral domains

Definition 23.7 An integral domain is a commutative ring with unity $1 \ne 0$ and no divisors of $0$.

Example 23.8. $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}_p$ ($p$ prime) are integral domains; $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is not if $n$ is composite; a direct product $R \times S$ of nonzero rings is not, since $(r, 0)(0, s) = (0, 0)$.

Example 23.9. $\mathbb{Z}_2$ is an integral domain, but $M_2(\mathbb{Z}_2)$ has divisors of $0$: $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix} = 0$. Every field is an integral domain.

Figure 23.10. A collection of rings as overlapping regions, numbered 1 to 6, with the labels Commutative rings, Rings with unity, Integral Domains and Fields as printed. Not redrawn.

Exercise 26 (named in the text introducing Figure 23.10). Redraw Fig. 23.10 to include a subset corresponding to strictly skew fields.

Theorem 23.11 Every finite integral domain is a field.

Proof. For $a \ne 0$ the map $x \mapsto ax$ is one-to-one by cancellation, hence onto since $R$ is finite; so $ab = 1$ for some $b$. ■

3. Characteristic

Definition 23.12 The least positive $n$ with $n \cdot a = 0$ for all $a \in R$ is the characteristic of $R$; if none exists, $R$ has characteristic $0$.

Exercise 35 (named in the text after Definition 23.12). Show that the characteristic of an integral domain $D$ must be either $0$ or a prime $p$. [Hint: If the characteristic of $D$ is $mn$, consider $(m \cdot 1)(n \cdot 1)$ in $D$.]

Example 23.13. $\mathbb{Z}_n$ has characteristic $n$; $\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}$ have characteristic $0$.

Theorem 23.14 In a ring with unity, the characteristic is $0$ if $n \cdot 1 \ne 0$ for all $n$, and otherwise the least $n$ with $n \cdot 1 = 0$.

Proof. If $n \cdot 1 = 0$, then $n \cdot a = a(n \cdot 1) = 0$ for all $a$. ◆
