1. The first isomorphism theorem

The isomorphism theorems say that certain factor groups are isomorphic to groups we already know. The first is the most important. Figure 16.1 shows the idea: a homomorphism $\phi$ from $G$ to $G'$ factors through the factor group $G/K$, where $K$ is the kernel.

Figure 16.1. A triangle: $\phi$ runs from $G$ to $\phi[G]$ across the top; $\gamma_K$ runs from $G$ down to $G/K$; the dashed arrow $\mu$, labelled "isomorphism", runs from $G/K$ up to $\phi[G]$. Labels as printed; not redrawn.

Theorem 16.2 (First Isomorphism Theorem) Let $\phi : G \to G'$ be a homomorphism with kernel $K$, and let $\gamma_K : G \to G/K$ be the canonical homomorphism. There is a unique isomorphism $\mu : G/K \to \phi[G]$ such that $\phi(x) = \mu(\gamma_K(x))$ for each $x \in G$.

2. Joins and a lemma on normal subgroups

For subgroups $H$ and $N$ of $G$, let $HN = \{hn \mid h \in H, n \in N\}$. In general $HN$ need not be a subgroup. The join $H \vee N$ is the smallest subgroup of $G$ that contains $HN$ (equivalently, both $H$ and $N$).

Lemma 16.3 Let $N$ be a normal subgroup of a group $G$ and $\gamma : G \to G/N$ the canonical homomorphism. Then $\phi(L) = \gamma[L]$ is a one-to-one and onto map from the normal subgroups of $G$ containing $N$ to the normal subgroups of $G/N$.

Idea of the proof. If $L$ is normal and contains $N$, then $\gamma^{-1}[\gamma[L]] = L$ because $L$ contains every coset $xN$ with $x \in L$. Conversely, if $H$ is normal in $G/N$, then $\gamma^{-1}[H]$ is normal in $G$ and contains $N$. ◆

Lemma 16.4 If $N$ is normal in $G$ and $H$ is any subgroup, then $H \vee N = HN = NH$. If $H$ is also normal, then $HN$ is normal in $G$.

Proof. Let $h_1, h_2 \in H$ and $n_1, n_2 \in N$. Normality of $N$ gives $n_1h_2 = h_2n_3$ for some $n_3 \in N$. Then $(h_1n_1)(h_2n_2) = (h_1h_2)(n_3n_2) \in HN$. Also $(hn)^{-1} = n^{-1}h^{-1} = h^{-1}n_4 \in HN$. So $HN$ is a subgroup, and by the same argument $NH$ is one; hence $NH = H \vee N = HN$. If $H$ is normal, $ghng^{-1} = (ghg^{-1})(gng^{-1}) \in HN$. ◆

3. The second isomorphism theorem

Theorem 16.5 (Second Isomorphism Theorem) Let $H$ be a subgroup of $G$ and $N$ a normal subgroup of $G$. Then $(HN)/N \simeq H/(H \cap N)$.

Proof. $N$ is normal in $HN$. Define $\phi : H \to HN/N$ by $\phi(h) = hN$. It is a homomorphism, it is onto (every element of $HN/N$ is $hnN = hN$), and its kernel is $\{h \in H \mid hN = N\} = H \cap N$. Apply Theorem 16.2. ◆

Example 16.6. Let $G = \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$, $H = \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z} \times \{0\}$, and $N = \{0\} \times \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$. Then $HN = \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$ and $H \cap N = \{0\} \times \mathbb{Z} \times \{0\}$. So $(HN)/N \simeq \mathbb{Z}$ and $H/(H \cap N) \simeq \mathbb{Z}$.

Example 16.7. Let $G = \mathbb{Z}$, $N = \langle n \rangle$ and $H = \langle h \rangle$ with $n, h$ positive. Write $N + H$ for $NH$. Then $N + H = \langle \gcd(n, h) \rangle$, since $\gcd(n, h) = xn + yh$ for some integers $x, y$. Also $N \cap H = \langle \operatorname{lcm}(n, h) \rangle$. The second isomorphism theorem gives $\langle \gcd(n,h) \rangle / \langle n \rangle \simeq \langle h \rangle / \langle \operatorname{lcm}(n,h) \rangle$. Since a factor group of a cyclic group is cyclic, both sides are isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_{n/\gcd(n,h)}$. Comparing orders gives $nh = \gcd(n,h)\operatorname{lcm}(n,h)$.

Exercise 10 (named in Example 16.7). Use one of the Isomorphism Theorems to prove that if $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ and $a$ divides $b$, then $|a\mathbb{Z}/b\mathbb{Z}| = b/a$.

4. The third isomorphism theorem

If $H$ and $K$ are normal subgroups of $G$ with $K \le H$, then $H/K$ is a normal subgroup of $G/K$.

Theorem 16.8 (Third Isomorphism Theorem) Let $H$ and $K$ be normal subgroups of $G$ with $K \le H$. Then $G/H \simeq (G/K)/(H/K)$.

Proof. Define $\phi : G/K \to G/H$ by $\phi(gK) = gH$. It is well defined because $gK \subseteq gH$. It is a homomorphism, it is onto, and its kernel is $H/K$. Apply Theorem 16.2: $\mu((gK)(H/K)) = gH$ is the isomorphism. ◆

Collapsing $K$ and then the $H/K$ cosets gives the same group as collapsing $H$ directly. Figure 16.9 (printed on page 148) shows this as an ellipse for $G$ with the cosets of $H$ drawn as thick-line regions and the cosets of $K$ inside them. The figure carries no labels; it is not redrawn.

Example 16.10. Let $G = \mathbb{Z}_8$, $H = \langle 2 \rangle = \{0, 2, 4, 6\}$, and $K = \langle 4 \rangle = \{0, 4\}$. Then

$$G/K = \{\{0, 4\}, \{1, 5\}, \{2, 6\}, \{3, 7\}\},$$ $$G/H = \{\{0, 2, 4, 6\}, \{1, 3, 5, 7\}\},$$ $$H/K = \{\{0, 4\}, \{2, 6\}\}.$$

Each element of $(G/K)/(H/K)$ is a coset of $H/K$, so it is a set of sets:

$$(G/K)/(H/K) = \{\{\{0, 4\}, \{2, 6\}\}, \{\{1, 5\}, \{3, 7\}\}\}.$$

The isomorphism of Theorem 16.8 sends $\{\{0, 4\}, \{2, 6\}\}$ to $\{0, 4, 2, 6\}$ and $\{\{1, 5\}, \{3, 7\}\}$ to $\{1, 5, 3, 7\}$.
