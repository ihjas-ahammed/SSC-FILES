1. Divisors of zero and cancellation

In the integers a product is $0$ only when a factor is $0$. We use this when we solve $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$ by factoring it as $(x - 2)(x - 3)$. This property can fail in other rings.

Example 23.1. In $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ the factorisation $x^2 - 5x + 6 = (x - 2)(x - 3)$ still holds, but many products are $0$: for instance $(2)(6) = 0$ and $(3)(4) = 0$. So the equation has the solutions $2, 3, 6, 11$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$, not just $2$ and $3$, since $(6 - 2)(6 - 3) = 0$ and $(11 - 2)(11 - 3) = 0$.

Definition 23.2 Two nonzero elements $a, b$ of a ring $R$ with $ab = 0$ are divisors of $0$ (or $0$ divisors).

In $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ the elements $2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10$ are divisors of $0$. These are exactly the numbers whose gcd with $12$ is not $1$. A unit cannot be a divisor of $0$: if $ab = 0$ and $a$ is a unit, then $b = a^{-1}ab = 0$.

Theorem 23.3 Let $m \in \mathbb{Z}_n$. Either $m = 0$; or $\gcd(m,n) = 1$, and $m$ is a unit of $\mathbb{Z}_n$; or $\gcd(m,n) \ne 1$, and $m$ is a $0$ divisor of $\mathbb{Z}_n$.

Proof. If $d = \gcd(m, n) \ne 1$ and $m \ne 0$, then $m(n/d) = (m/d)n$ is a multiple of $n$, so $m \cdot (n/d) = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}_n$ with $m, n/d \ne 0$. If $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, write $an + bm = 1$ and reduce $b$ modulo $n$: $b = nq + r$ with $0 \le r \le n - 1$. Then $rm = 1 - n(a + qm)$, so $rm = 1$ in $\mathbb{Z}_n$. ◆

Example 23.4. In $\mathbb{Z}_{20}$ the units are $1, 3, 7, 9, 11, 13, 17, 19$ (since $1 \cdot 1 = 3 \cdot 7 = 9 \cdot 9 = \cdots = 1$), and the $0$ divisors are $2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18$ (for example $2 \cdot 10 = 0$).

Corollary 23.5 If $p$ is prime, every nonzero element of $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is a unit; so $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is a field with no divisors of $0$.

Proof. For $0 < m \le p - 1$, $\gcd(m, p) = 1$; apply Theorem 23.3. ■

The book adds that linear algebra over a finite field such as $\mathbb{Z}_p$ uses only field properties, since there is no natural idea of magnitude in $\mathbb{Z}_p$ ($p \cdot 1 = 0$).

Theorem 23.6 The cancellation laws hold in a ring $R$ if and only if $R$ has no divisors of $0$.

Proof. If cancellation holds and $ab = 0$ with $a \ne 0$, then $ab = a0$ gives $b = 0$. Conversely, if $R$ has no divisors of $0$ and $ab = ac$ with $a \ne 0$, then $a(b - c) = 0$ gives $b = c$; the case $ba = ca$ is similar. ◆

In a ring with no divisors of $0$, an equation $ax = b$ with $a \ne 0$ has at most one solution. If $a$ is a unit the solution is $a^{-1}b$. In a commutative ring the quotient $b/a$ denotes $a^{-1}b = ba^{-1}$; this notation must not be used in a noncommutative ring.

2. Integral domains

Definition 23.7 An integral domain $D$ is a commutative ring with unity $1 \ne 0$ that has no divisors of $0$.

An integral domain lies between a commutative ring with unity and a field (Example 23.9 and Figure 23.10).

Example 23.8. $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}_p$ ($p$ prime) are integral domains. $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is not an integral domain if $n$ is not prime, and a direct product $R \times S$ of nonzero rings is never one, since $(r, 0)(0, s) = (0, 0)$.

Example 23.9. $\mathbb{Z}_2$ is an integral domain, but $M_2(\mathbb{Z}_2)$ has divisors of $0$: $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix} = 0$.

Every field is an integral domain, since units are not divisors of $0$ and multiplication in a field is commutative.

Theorem 23.11 Every finite integral domain is a field.

Proof. Let $R$ be a finite integral domain and $a \ne 0$. The map $f(x) = ax$ is one-to-one by cancellation (Theorem 23.6). Since $R$ is finite, $f$ is also onto, so $ab = 1$ for some $b \in R$. Thus $a$ is a unit. ■

The finite condition is needed: $\mathbb{Z}$ is an infinite integral domain that is not a field, and multiplication by $2$ is one-to-one but not onto.

3. Characteristic

Definition 23.12 If some positive integer $n$ satisfies $n \cdot a = 0$ for all $a \in R$, the least such $n$ is the characteristic of $R$. If none exists, $R$ has characteristic $0$.

Exercise 35 (named after Definition 23.12). Show that the characteristic of an integral domain $D$ must be either $0$ or a prime $p$. [Hint: If the characteristic of $D$ is $mn$, consider $(m \cdot 1)(n \cdot 1)$ in $D$.]

Example 23.13. $\mathbb{Z}_n$ has characteristic $n$; $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$ have characteristic $0$.

Theorem 23.14 Let $R$ have unity. If $n \cdot 1 \ne 0$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$, then $R$ has characteristic $0$; otherwise the least $n$ with $n \cdot 1 = 0$ is the characteristic.

Proof. If $n \cdot 1 = 0$, then $n \cdot a = a(n \cdot 1) = a0 = 0$ for every $a \in R$. ◆
