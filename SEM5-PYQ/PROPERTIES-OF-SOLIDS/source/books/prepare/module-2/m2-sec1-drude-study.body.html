5.1 Drude–Lorentz classical free electron theory (student notes)

Source: book-sources/bs-3-free-electron-theory.html, Puri and Babbar pp. 151–152. No numbered equations appear in this section except the Wiedemann–Franz ratio, which is shown unnumbered. Figure 5.1 is redrawn.

Before you start

  • Electric current is charge moving per unit time; Ohm's law is $V=IR$ (Class 12).
  • An electron moving under an electric field gains speed, and collisions reduce it (Class 12, drift velocity).
  • Heat conduction and electrical conduction both depend on how freely carriers move.
Plus Two recall: current, drift and Ohm's law

Recall. The current in a wire is $I=neAv_d$, where $n$ is the carrier density, $e$ the charge, $A$ the area and $v_d$ the drift velocity. Ohm's law says $V=IR$.

Re-derive.

  1. Take a wire of length $\ell$ and area $A$ with field $E=V/\ell$.
  2. Current density $J=I/A=nev_d$.
  3. Writing $v_d$ as proportional to $E$ gives $J=\sigma E$ with a constant $\sigma$ (the conductivity). This is Ohm's law in local form.

Saved. $J=\sigma E$. Drude's model gives this $\sigma$ from the collisions.

1. The model

Drude (1900) pictured a metal as positive ion cores with its valence electrons moving freely among them. The electrons are held in the metal by the attraction of the ion cores, but the potential of the cores is taken as constant, and the repulsion between electrons is neglected. The electrons behave like the molecules of a perfect gas, so the model is called the free electron gas model. The book lists three differences between the free electron gas and an ordinary gas: the electrons are negatively charged; their concentration is far higher; and they obey the Pauli exclusion principle.

Because the potential of the ion cores is taken as constant, the potential energy of a conduction electron inside the metal is zero, and its total energy is only kinetic. At the surface the electron faces a potential barrier $V_0$, the difference between its energy inside and just outside the metal. This barrier keeps the electrons inside (Fig. 5.1). In one dimension the metal is therefore a potential box.

2. Lorentz's assumption

Drude's picture makes the electrons behave as a perfect gas. Lorentz (1909) assumed that the electrons obey Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics at equilibrium. The combined theory is the Drude–Lorentz theory. Because it uses classical statistics, it is also called the classical theory.

3. Conduction and Ohm's law

Without a field, the electrons move at random and there is no net current. An applied field accelerates them in one direction. They do not speed up without limit: they collide with the ions and lose their speed. The balance of acceleration and collision gives a steady drift, and the current is proportional to the applied voltage at a fixed temperature. This is Ohm's law. The free motion of the electrons also explains the high electrical and thermal conductivities of metals.

4. Wiedemann–Franz law

Since the electrons move freely whatever the crystal structure, the ratio of electrical to thermal conductivity should be the same for all metals at a given temperature:

$$\frac{\sigma}{\kappa}=\text{constant}$$

The book notes that this law has been verified in practice.

5. Lustre and opacity

Free electrons absorb all incident light and are set into forced oscillation. They re-emit the same energy in all directions, which gives the metallic lustre and the complete opacity of metals.

6. Where the theory fails

  • It predicts the room-temperature resistivity correctly, but predicts that resistivity varies as $T^{1/2}$. Experiment shows that it varies linearly with $T$.
  • It fails for the heat capacity and the paramagnetic susceptibility of the conduction electrons. With classical statistics every electron gains energy, which gives values much too large.
  • It cannot explain the long mean free paths of electrons at low temperatures ($10^8$ to $10^9$ interatomic spacings, more than a centimetre).

The fixes come from Fermi–Dirac statistics: only a fraction of the electrons can gain energy, and this gives the right heat capacity and susceptibility. That is the content of Sommerfeld's theory (Section 5.2).

Result: what the classical theory gives

Ohm's law, the Wiedemann–Franz ratio independent of metal, lustre and opacity. It fails on the temperature dependence of resistivity, on the heat capacity and on the susceptibility.

Summary

  • Metal = fixed ion cores plus a free electron gas inside a potential box of height $V_0$.
  • Drude–Lorentz uses Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics; it explains Ohm's law, the Wiedemann–Franz law and lustre.
  • It fails for the heat capacity, susceptibility and the $T$-dependence of resistivity.
