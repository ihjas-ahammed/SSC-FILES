5.2.2–5.3 Free electron gas in three dimensions and its applications (student notes)

Source: book-sources/bs-3-free-electron-theory.html, Puri and Babbar pp. 159–172 (Examples 5.1 and 5.2 included). Equations 5.19 to 5.53 are cited by their book numbers. Figures 5.6 to 5.9 are redrawn. Section 5.3.1 (electronic specific heat) is included.

Before you start

  • Volume of a sphere $\tfrac43\pi k^3$ (Plus Two recall below).
  • Fermi function: occupation probability of a state at energy $E$ and temperature $T$.
  • Pressure and bulk modulus: $P=-\partial E/\partial V$, $B=-V\,\partial P/\partial V$.
Plus Two recall: volume of a sphere and counting points

Recall. A sphere of radius $k$ has volume $\tfrac43\pi k^3$.

Re-derive.

  1. In $k$-space each allowed state occupies a cube of side $2\pi/L$, volume $(2\pi/L)^3$.
  2. The number of $k$-points inside a sphere of radius $k$ is therefore $\tfrac43\pi k^3/(2\pi/L)^3=\tfrac{L^3}{6\pi^2}k^3$.
  3. Two spin states per point double this to $\tfrac{L^3}{3\pi^2}k^3$.

Saved. The counting in Eq. (5.34).

1. The cube and the wave functions

For a cubical box of edge $L$, the particle-in-a-box solution is a standing wave in each direction (5.20). The book uses the travelling-wave form $\psi_{\mathbf k}=Ae^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}$ (5.21) instead, because it is easier to work with. Periodic boundary conditions over the cube (5.23) fix each component of the wave vector:

$$\psi_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)=Ae^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\tag{5.21}$$
$$k_x=\frac{2\pi n_x}{L},\qquad k_y=\frac{2\pi n_y}{L},\qquad k_z=\frac{2\pi n_z}{L}\tag{5.24}$$

The energy of the state is $E_k=\hbar^2k^2/2m=(\hbar^2/2m)(k_x^2+k_y^2+k_z^2)$ (5.25), and the normalised wave function is (5.27):

$$E_k=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(k_x^2+k_y^2+k_z^2\right)\tag{5.25}$$
$$\psi_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L^3}}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\tag{5.27}$$

Each state is labelled by $k_x$, $k_y$, $k_z$ and the spin $m_s$. Two spin states share each $\mathbf k$, so each energy level holds two electrons.

2. Filling the levels; the Fermi sphere

At 0 K the occupied states form a sphere in $k$-space (Fig. 5.8). Its surface marks the Fermi wave vector $k_F$, and the energy at the surface is the Fermi energy (5.33). Counting the states in the sphere (5.34) gives

$$2\cdot\frac{\frac43\pi k_F^3}{(2\pi/L)^3}=\frac{L^3}{3\pi^2}k_F^3=N\tag{5.34}$$
{{FIG:m2-s3-kspace}}
$$k_F=\left(\frac{3\pi^2N}{V}\right)^{1/3}\tag{5.35}$$

So $k_F$ depends on the density $N/V$ only, not on the mass. Using (5.33) with (5.35):

$$E_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{3\pi^2N}{V}\right)^{2/3}\tag{5.36}$$

Inverting (5.36) gives the total number of electrons (5.37), and the Fermi velocity follows from (5.32) and (5.35) (5.38):

$$N=\frac{V}{3\pi^2}\left(\frac{2mE_F}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\tag{5.37}$$
$$v_F=\frac{\hbar k_F}{m}=\frac{\hbar}{m}\left(\frac{3\pi^2N}{V}\right)^{1/3}\tag{5.38}$$
{{FIG:m2-s3-occ3d}}

3. Density of states in three dimensions

Require $\int_0^{E_F}D(E)\,dE=N$ (5.39). Using (5.37) and differentiating with respect to $E$ gives the parabolic density of states (5.40):

$$D(E)=\frac{V}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}E^{1/2}\tag{5.40}$$
{{FIG:m2-s3-dos3d}}

At the Fermi level, $D(E_F)=dN/dE_F$ (5.41). Since $N\propto E_F^{3/2}$, $dN/N=\tfrac32\,dE_F/E_F$ (5.42), so the density of states at $E_F$ is roughly the number of electrons divided by $E_F$.

$$D(E_F)=\frac{dN}{dE_F}=\frac{V}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}E_F^{1/2}\tag{5.41}$$
$$\frac{dN}{N}=\frac32\frac{dE_F}{E_F}\tag{5.42}$$

4. The Fermi function and the filled states

At $T>0$ some electrons leave the levels below $E_F$, so the occupation is given by the Fermi function (5.28):

$$f(E)=\frac{1}{\exp\!\left[\dfrac{E-E_F}{k_BT}\right]+1}\tag{5.28}$$
{{FIG:m2-s3-fermi}}

At 0 K it is a step: $f=1$ below $E_{F0}$ and $0$ above (5.29). At $E=E_F$, $f=\tfrac12$ for any $T$, and the Fermi level moves with temperature as

$$E_F\cong E_{F_0}\left[1-\frac{\pi^2}{12}\left(\frac{k_BT}{E_{F_0}}\right)^2\right]\tag{5.30}$$

For metals the level spacing is so small that the highest filled level is taken as $E_F$. Only electrons within about $k_BT$ of $E_F$ are affected by the temperature; at room temperature this is roughly 1% of the electrons, for $E_F=3$ eV. The filled-state density is $N(E)=D(E)f(E)$ (5.43), and the number of filled states in $dE$ is $N(E)\,dE$ (5.44).

5. Parameters at absolute zero

(a) Fermi energy. Integrating (5.43) with the step function (5.29) gives $N=\int_0^{E_{F0}}D(E)\,dE$; with $n=N/V$:

$$E_{F0}=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2n)^{2/3}\tag{5.47}$$

(b) Average kinetic energy. $E_0=\dfrac1N\int_0^{E_{F0}}E\,D(E)\,dE$. Evaluating the integral with (5.40) and (5.37) gives $E_0=\tfrac35E_{F0}$ (the average kinetic energy per electron at 0 K). This is the value the classical theory cannot give.

$$E_0=\tfrac35E_{F0}$$

6. Applications (Section 5.3)

The properties of metals divide into static properties (heat capacity, emission, susceptibility, which need only the energy levels) and transport properties (conductivity, Hall effect, which need the response to a field).

6.1 Electronic specific heat

Classical statistics give each electron $\tfrac32k_BT$, so $N$ electrons give $E=\tfrac32Nk_BT$ and $C_V=\tfrac32Nk_B$ (5.49, 5.50). Using one electron per atom, this is about $3\ \text{cal g-atom}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$, half as large again as the lattice value, which contradicts experiment. The electronic contribution is at most 1% of this at room temperature and falls linearly to zero as $T\to0$.

Quantum mechanics explains this: only electrons within $k_BT$ of $E_F$ can be excited, which is a fraction $\sim T/T_F$ of the electrons, where $E_F=k_BT_F$. The printed result is

$$C_V\cong\frac32Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{T_F}\right)\tag{5.51}$$

$C_V$ is therefore proportional to $T$ and goes to zero as $T\to0$. With $T=300$ K and $T_F=3000$ K, $C_V$ is about 0.01 of the classical value, in agreement with experiment.

7. Worked examples (book)

Example 5.1. Sodium: $r=1.86$ Å, bcc, one valence electron, two per unit cell. $V=a^3=(4r/\sqrt3)^3=7.93\times10^{-29}$ m$^3$; $N/V=2.52\times10^{28}$ m$^{-3}$; $E_{F0}=4.98\times10^{-19}$ J $=3.11$ eV (from (5.47)).

Example 5.2. From $P=-N\,\partial E_0/\partial V$ with $E_0=\tfrac35E_{F0}$ and $E_{F0}\propto(N/V)^{2/3}$, the book obtains $P=\tfrac25NE_{F0}/V$ (5.53) and then the pressure–volume law (5.52). The bulk modulus follows from $B=-V\,\partial P/\partial V$ and gives $B=\tfrac53P$, i.e. $B$ in terms of $E_0$ (the book expresses it through the average kinetic energy).

Result: three-dimensional free electron gas

$k_F=(3\pi^2N/V)^{1/3}$; $E_F$ as (5.36); $D(E)$ as (5.40); $E_0=\tfrac35E_F$; $C_V\cong\tfrac32Nk_B(T/T_F)$.

Summary

  • Fermi sphere in $k$-space; $k_F$ depends only on $N/V$ (5.35).
  • $E_F$ (5.36), $D(E)\propto E^{1/2}$ (5.40), $f(E)$ (5.28), $E_F(T)$ (5.30).
  • Average kinetic energy $\tfrac35E_{F0}$; specific heat $C_V\propto T$ (5.51).
