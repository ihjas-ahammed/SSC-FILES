Plus Two recall: magnetic moment and magnetic materials

Recall. A current loop of area $A$ carrying current $I$ has moment $m=IA$. Materials are classed as diamagnetic, paramagnetic or ferromagnetic by their response to a field.

Re-derive.

  1. The magnetisation $\mathbf{M}$ is the moment per unit volume.
  2. For weak fields $M\propto H$, so $\chi=M/H$ is a constant of the material.
  3. Negative and small $\chi$ (diamagnetic), small positive $\chi$ (paramagnetic), and large positive $\chi$ (ferromagnetic) are the classes.

Saved. Eq. (8.1).

8.1 Magnetic terminology

The magnetisation $\mathbf{M}$ is the magnetic moment per unit volume. The susceptibility is

$$\chi=M/H\tag{8.1}$$

$\chi$ is a tensor in general and a scalar in isotropic media. It is dimensionless in SI units. The molar susceptibility $\chi_m$ refers to a gram molecule.

The flux density $\mathbf{B}$ in the medium is

$$\mathbf{B}=\mu_0(\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{M})\tag{8.2}$$
$$\mathbf{B}=\mu_0(1+\chi)\mathbf{H}\tag{8.3}$$
$$\mathbf{B}=\mu\mathbf{H}\tag{8.4}$$
$$\mu=\mu_0\mu_r\tag{8.5}$$
$$\mathbf{B}=\mu_0\mu_r\mathbf{H}\tag{8.6}$$
$$\mu_r=1+\chi\tag{8.7}$$

In free space $\chi=0$, $\mu_r=1$ and $\mathbf{B}=\mu_0\mathbf{H}$ (8.8).

$$\mathbf{B}=\mu_0\mathbf{H}\tag{8.8}$$

8.2 Types of magnetism

Magnetism in solids arises from the orbital and spin motion of electrons, and to a smaller extent from nuclear spins. The main contribution is the spin of unpaired valence electrons, which gives permanent electronic moments. The total moment depends on the number of unpaired electrons in each atom and on the relative orientation of neighbouring moments. Five classes are recognised: diamagnetism (induced, opposing the field, very weak), paramagnetism (permanent moments, aligned with the field, weak), ferromagnetism (parallel alignment of neighbours, very strong), antiferromagnetism (antiparallel and equal, so no net moment) and ferrimagnetism (antiparallel and unequal, so a net moment). Above the critical temperature all materials are paramagnetic.

Plus Two recall: Lenz's law

Recall. An induced current opposes the change in flux that produces it.

Re-derive.

  1. An orbiting electron is a current loop with a moment.
  2. A switched-on field changes the flux through the loop.
  3. The induced moment opposes the field, so $\chi$ is negative (Eq. 8.16).

Saved. Eqs. (8.16) and (8.20).

8.3.1 Langevin's classical theory

For an electron of radius $\rho$ about a nucleus of charge $Ze$:

$$m\omega_0^2\rho=\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\rho^2}\tag{8.9}$$
$$\omega_0=\sqrt{\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0m\rho^3}}\tag{8.10}$$

A field $\mathbf{B}$ perpendicular to the orbit exerts a Lorentz force

$$\mathbf{F}=e(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\tag{8.11}$$

with magnitude

$$F=e\rho\omega B\tag{8.12}$$

The force balance with the field is

$$\begin{aligned}m\omega^2\rho&=\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\rho^2}-e\rho\omega B\\&=m\omega_0^2\rho-e\rho\omega B\end{aligned}\tag{8.13}$$

For $eB/2m\ll\omega_0$ the solution is

$$\omega\cong\pm\omega_0-\frac{eB}{2m}\tag{8.14}$$

This is Larmor's theorem: the frequency changes by $eB/2m$. The additional current and moment are

$$\begin{aligned}I&=\text{charge}\times\text{revolutions per unit time}\\&=(-e)\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{eB}{2m}\right)\\&=-\frac{e^2B}{4\pi m}\end{aligned}\tag{8.15}$$
$$\mu_e=-\frac{e^2B}{4m}\rho^2\tag{8.16}$$
$$\mu_a=-\frac{Ze^2B}{4m}\langle\rho^2\rangle\tag{8.17}$$
$$\langle r^2\rangle=\frac{3}{2}\langle\rho^2\rangle\tag{8.18}$$

Hence

$$\begin{aligned}\mu_a &= -\frac{Ze^2B}{6m}\langle r^2\rangle \\ &= -\frac{\mu_0Ze^2H}{6m}\langle r^2\rangle\end{aligned} \tag{8.19}$$

and for $N$ atoms per unit volume

$$\chi_{dia}=\frac{M}{H}=-\frac{N\mu_0Ze^2}{6m}\langle r^2\rangle\tag{8.20}$$

This is Langevin's classical result, independent of temperature.

8.3.2 Quantum theory (background)

The field adds $\mathcal{H}'$ to the electron Hamiltonian:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathcal{H}' &= -\frac{ie\hbar}{2m}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}+2\mathbf{A}\cdot\nabla) \\ &+\frac{e^2}{2m}A^2\end{aligned} \tag{8.21}$$

For a uniform field,

$$\mathbf{A}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{r}\tag{8.22}$$

and with $\mathbf{B}$ along $z$:

$$A_x=-\frac{1}{2}yB,\quad A_y=\frac{1}{2}xB,\quad A_z=0\tag{8.23}$$
$$\begin{aligned}\mathcal{H}' &= -\frac{ie\hbar B}{2m}\left(x\frac{\partial}{\partial y}-y\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right) \\ &+\frac{e^2B^2}{8m}(x^2+y^2)\end{aligned} \tag{8.24}$$

The angular momentum operator is

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf{L}&=-i\hbar\,\mathbf{r}\times\nabla\\&=-i\hbar\left[\hat{\mathbf{i}}\left(y\frac{\partial}{\partial z}-z\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)\right.\\&\qquad\left.+\hat{\mathbf{j}}\left(z\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-x\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)+\hat{\mathbf{k}}\left(x\frac{\partial}{\partial y}-y\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)\right]\end{aligned}\tag{8.25}$$

The first term of (8.24) is proportional to $L_z$. The second term, summed over $N$ atoms of $Z$ electrons, is

$$\frac{Ne^2B^2}{8m}\sum_{i=1}^{Z}(x_i^2+y_i^2)=\frac{NZe^2B^2}{8m}\langle\rho^2\rangle\tag{8.26}$$

Comparing with $-\tfrac{1}{2\mu_0}\chi_{dia}B^2$:

$$\chi_{dia}=-\frac{N\mu_0Ze^2}{4m}\langle\rho^2\rangle\tag{8.27}$$
$$\chi_{dia}=-\frac{N\mu_0Ze^2}{6m}\langle r^2\rangle\tag{8.28}$$

This agrees with the classical result. $\langle r^2\rangle$ must be found from the electron charge distribution. Agreement is good for light elements and rare gases; for helium $\chi\approx-1.9\times10^{-6}$.

Worked Example 8.1

Ground-state hydrogen, $\psi=(\pi a_0^3)^{-1/2}e^{-r/a_0}$, $a_0=0.46$ Å, gives $\langle r^2\rangle=3a_0^2$. With (8.28), $\chi_{dia}=-(N\mu_0e^2/2m)a_0^2$, and with $N=2.69\times10^{28}$ m$^{-3}$ the book gives $\chi_{dia}=-1.01\times10^{-6}$ (SI).

Plus Two recall: a magnetic dipole in a field

Recall. $U=-\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{B}$ for a dipole in a uniform field.

Re-derive.

  1. At angle $\theta$, $U=-\mu B\cos\theta$.
  2. Lowest energy at $\theta=0$.
  3. Thermal agitation spreads orientations, so only a partial alignment results.

Saved. $U=-\mu B\cos\theta$ is the basis of Eq. (8.29).

8.4.1 Langevin's classical theory

For $N$ independent dipoles of moment $\mu$, the energy at angle $\theta$ is $E=-\mu B\cos\theta$, and the population in $d\omega$ follows the Maxwell–Boltzmann law:

$$dn\propto\exp\left(\frac{\mu B\cos\theta}{kT}\right)d\omega=k\exp\left(\frac{\mu B\cos\theta}{kT}\right)2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\tag{8.29}$$
$$\begin{aligned}M &= N\mu\langle\cos\theta\rangle \\ &= N\frac{\mu\int_0^\pi\cos\theta\sin\theta\exp\left(\frac{\mu B\cos\theta}{kT}\right)d\theta}{\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\exp\left(\frac{\mu B\cos\theta}{kT}\right)d\theta}\end{aligned} \tag{8.30}$$

With $x=\mu B/kT$ and $y=\cos\theta$:

$$\begin{aligned}M &= N\mu\frac{\int_1^{-1}ye^{xy}\,dy}{\int_1^{-1}e^{xy}\,dy} \\ &= N\mu\left[\coth x-\frac{1}{x}\right] \\ &= N\mu\,L(x)\end{aligned} \tag{8.31}$$

For $x\ll1$, $L(x)\cong x/3$:

$$\left.L(x)\right|_{x=0}\cong\frac{x}{3}=\frac{\mu B}{3kT}\tag{8.32}$$

Hence

$$\chi_{para}=\frac{\mu_0M}{B}=\frac{\mu_0N\mu^2}{3kT}\tag{8.33}$$
$$\chi_{para}=\frac{C}{T}\tag{8.34}$$

with $C=\mu_0N\mu^2/3k$ (Curie law, valid for $\mu B\ll kT$). For large $x$, saturation gives

$$M=N\mu=M_s\tag{8.35}$$

8.4.2 Quantum theory

The moment and its component are quantised:

$$\boldsymbol{\mu}=-g\mu_B\mathbf{J}\tag{8.36}$$
$$g=1+\frac{J(J+1)+S(S+1)-L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)}\tag{8.37}$$
$$\mu_z=-g\mu_Bm_J\tag{8.38}$$
$$E=m_Jg\mu_BB\tag{8.39}$$
$$M=N\sum_{m_J=-J}^{m_J=+J}\frac{-m_Jg\mu_Be^{-m_Jg\mu_BB/kT}}{e^{-m_Jg\mu_BB/kT}}\tag{8.40}$$

Weak fields, ordinary temperatures. Expanding the exponentials and using $\sum m_J=0$, $\sum m_J^2=J(J+1)(2J+1)/3$:

$$M=N\frac{g^2\mu_B^2B}{3kT}J(J+1)\tag{8.41}$$
$$\chi_{para}=\frac{\mu_0M}{B}=\frac{\mu_0N\mu_B^2}{3kT}g^2J(J+1)\tag{8.42}$$
$$\chi_{para}=\frac{\mu_0Np_{eff}^2\mu_B^2}{3kT}\tag{8.43}$$
$$p_{eff}=g\sqrt{J(J+1)}\tag{8.44}$$
$$\mu^2=p_{eff}^2\mu_B^2\tag{8.45}$$

Strong fields, low temperatures.

$$M=NgJ\mu_BB_J(x)\tag{8.46}$$
$$\begin{aligned}B_J(x) &= \frac{2J+1}{2J}\coth\left(\frac{2J+1}{2J}x\right) \\ &-\frac{1}{2J}\coth\left(\frac{x}{2J}\right)\end{aligned} \tag{8.47}$$

For $x\ll1$, $B_J=x(J+1)/3J$, so

$$\begin{aligned}\chi_{para} &= \frac{\mu_0M}{B} \\ &= \frac{\mu_0NJ(J+1)g^2\mu_B^2}{3kT} \\ &= \frac{\mu_0Np_{eff}^2\mu_B^2}{3kT}\end{aligned} \tag{8.48}$$

For $x\gg1$, $B_J\cong1$ and

$$M=NgJ\mu_B\tag{8.49}$$

As $J\to\infty$, $B_J\to L(x)$, so the classical result is recovered. When the crystal field quenches the orbital moment, $E=2m_s\mu_BB$ with $g=2$ (8.50), and

$$E=2m_s\mu_BB\tag{8.50}$$
$$\chi=\frac{\mu_0N}{3kT}\left(\frac{e\hbar}{m}\right)^2S(S+1)\tag{8.51}$$

This gives the observed susceptibility of $\mathrm{Mn}^{3+}$ ($S=2$), where (8.48) gives zero.

Worked Examples 8.2 and 8.3

Example 8.2 ($L=0$, $S=\tfrac12$, $J=\tfrac12$, $g=2$): with two levels,

$$M=g\mu_BJN\tanh\left(\frac{g\mu_BB}{2kT}\right)\tag{8.83}$$

For $N=10^{28}$ m$^{-3}$ and $B=2.5$ T, $M=519.1$ A/m at 300 K and $M=3.0\times10^{4}$ A/m at 5 K; the level populations are given in the book.

Example 8.3 ($\mathrm{Dy}^{3+}$, $S=5/2$, $L=5$, $J=15/2$, $g=1.33$): $\chi=5.9\times10^{-4}$ at 300 K for one kilogram-mole.

Plus Two recall: hysteresis of magnetic materials

Recall. Soft materials are easily magnetised and demagnetised; hard materials keep their magnetisation. Their behaviour is a loop in $B$ against $H$.

Re-derive.

  1. Increasing $H$ gives a rising magnetisation to saturation.
  2. Reducing $H$ to zero leaves the remanence $M_r$.
  3. Reversing $H$ to $H_c$ removes it. The closed curve is the hysteresis loop.

Saved. §8.5.3 and Fig. 8.6.

8.5.1 Weiss theory

Spontaneous magnetisation exists below $T_C$. Weiss proposed domains, each spontaneously magnetised, and an exchange field proportional to the magnetisation:

$$B_E=\lambda M\tag{8.52}$$

The effective field is

$$B_{eff}=B+B_E=B+\lambda M\tag{8.53}$$

Quantum form, with $B_J$ as in (8.55):

$$M=NgJ\mu_BB_J(x)\tag{8.54}$$
$$\begin{aligned}B_J(x) &= \frac{2J+1}{2J}\coth\left(\frac{2J+1}{2J}x\right) \\ &-\frac{1}{2J}\coth\left(\frac{x}{2J}\right)\end{aligned} \tag{8.55}$$
$$x=\frac{gJ\mu_BB_{eff}}{kT}=\frac{gJ\mu_B}{kT}(B+\lambda M)\tag{8.56}$$

Spontaneous magnetisation ($B=0$):

$$x=\frac{gJ\mu_B\lambda M}{kT}\tag{8.57}$$
$$M(T)=\frac{xkT}{\lambda gJ\mu_B}\tag{8.58}$$

The saturation value is

$$M_s(0)=NgJ\mu_B\tag{8.59}$$
$$\frac{M(T)}{M_s(0)}=\frac{xkT}{\lambda Ng^2J^2\mu_B^2}\tag{8.60}$$
$$\frac{M(T)}{M_s(0)}=B_J(x)\tag{8.61}$$

The intersection of (8.60) and (8.61) gives $M_s(T)$ for $T\lt T_C$. It vanishes at $T_C$, a second-order transition. Spontaneous magnetisation versus temperature is shown in Fig. 8.4.

The paramagnetic region

For $T>T_C$, $x\ll1$ and $B_J\cong(J+1)x/3J$. Then

$$x=\frac{gJ\mu_B(B+\lambda M)}{kT}\tag{8.62}$$
$$M=\frac{Ng^2\mu_B^2J(J+1)}{3kT}(B+\lambda M)\tag{8.63}$$
$$\chi=\frac{\mu_0M}{B}=\frac{\mu_0T_C/\lambda}{T-T_C}=\frac{C}{T-T_C}\tag{8.64}$$
$$C=\frac{\mu_0T_C}{\lambda}\tag{8.65}$$
$$T_C=\frac{\lambda Ng^2\mu_B^2J(J+1)}{3k}\tag{8.66}$$

(8.64) is the Curie–Weiss law.

8.5.2 Origin of the Weiss field

Dipole–dipole fields are about $10^{3}$ G, far below the Weiss field of about $10^{7}$ G for iron. Heisenberg attributed it to exchange, a non-magnetic electrostatic effect that depends on wave-function overlap and spin orientation:

$$U_{ij}=-2J_e\mathbf{S}_i\cdot\mathbf{S}_j\tag{8.67}$$

Stoner's mean-field treatment of $z$ neighbours gives

$$U_i=-2J_e\sum_j\mathbf{S}_i\cdot\mathbf{S}_j\tag{8.68}$$
$$U_i=-2zJ_e\left(S_{xi}\langle S_{xj}\rangle+S_{yi}\langle S_{yj}\rangle+S_{zi}\langle S_{zj}\rangle\right)\tag{8.69}$$

With $\langle S_{xj}\rangle=\langle S_{yj}\rangle=0$ and

$$\langle S_{zj}\rangle=\frac{M}{g\mu_BN}\tag{8.70}$$

the exchange energy becomes

$$U_i=-\frac{2zJ_eS_{zi}M}{gN\mu_B}\tag{8.71}$$
$$U_i=-gS_{zi}\mu_BB_E\tag{8.72}$$
$$B_E=\frac{2zJ_e}{Ng^2\mu_B^2}M\tag{8.73}$$
$$\lambda=\frac{2zJ_e}{Ng^2\mu_B^2}\tag{8.74}$$

so $B_E=\lambda M$ (8.52).

8.5.3 Domains and hysteresis

Domains have random orientations, so there is no net magnetisation. Field growth of domains (Fig. 8.7b) and their rotation (Fig. 8.7c) produce the magnetisation.

Neel's energy minimisation (magnetic field, anisotropy, domain wall and magnetostrictive energy) fixes the domain size. For $T\lt T_C$ the hysteresis boundary is traced by the intersections of (8.61) with

$$M=\left(\frac{kT}{\mu_Bg\lambda J}\right)x-\frac{1}{\lambda}B\tag{8.75}$$

OA (reversible), AB (large irreversible domain growth), BC (rotation to $M_s$), then remanence $M_r$ and coercivity $H_c$ close the loop CEFKC.

Worked Example 8.4

$J=3/2$, $g=2$, $T_C=125$ K: $B_E=3kT_C/[g\mu_B(J+1)]=111.6$ T. Above $T_C$,

$$M=\frac{Ng^2\mu_B^2J(J+1)B}{3k(T-T_C)}\tag{8.84}$$

giving $M/M_s(0)=6.4\times10^{-6}$ for $B=1$ mT at 300 K.

8.6 Antiferromagnetism

Spins of neighbouring atoms are antiparallel, forming two interpenetrating sublattices A and B with opposite magnetisation (Fig. 8.8). The net moment is zero. MnO was the first observed example. A field gives a small magnetisation that grows with temperature up to the Néel temperature $T_N$, above which the material is paramagnetic. The susceptibility has a weak cusp at $T_N$ (Fig. 8.9).

Molecular-field treatment of Néel

With $\alpha$ (AA, BB) and $\beta$ (AB) as the Weiss field constants, the internal fields are

$$\left.\begin{aligned}B_{mA}&=-\alpha M_A-\beta M_B\\B_{mB}&=-\beta M_A-\alpha M_B\end{aligned}\right.\tag{8.76}$$

and with the applied field $B$:

$$\left.\begin{aligned}B_A&=B-\alpha M_A-\beta M_B\\B_B&=B-\beta M_A-\alpha M_B\end{aligned}\right.\tag{8.77}$$

$T>T_N$. Each sublattice follows the paramagnetic law:

$$\left.\begin{aligned}M_A&=\frac{N\mu^2}{3kT}B_A\\M_B&=\frac{N\mu^2}{3kT}B_B\end{aligned}\right.\tag{8.78}$$

with $\mu^2=\mu_B^2g^2J(J+1)$. Summing with $N_A=N_B=N$:

$$\begin{aligned}M&=M_A+M_B\\&=\frac{N\mu^2}{3kT}(B_A+B_B)\\&=\frac{N\mu^2}{3kT}\left[2B-(\alpha+\beta)(M_A+M_B)\right]\\&=\frac{N\mu^2}{3kT}[2B-(\alpha+\beta)M]\end{aligned}\tag{8.79}$$
$$\begin{aligned}\chi &= \frac{\mu_0M}{B} \\ &= \frac{\dfrac{2\mu_0N\mu^2}{3k}}{T+\dfrac{N\mu^2(\alpha+\beta)}{3k}} \\ &= \frac{C}{T+\theta}\end{aligned} \tag{8.80}$$
$$C=\frac{2\mu_0N\mu^2}{3k}$$
$$\theta=\frac{N\mu^2(\alpha+\beta)}{3k}\tag{8.81}$$
$$\frac{T_N}{\theta}=\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\beta+\alpha}\tag{8.82}$$

For $\alpha=0$, $T_N=\theta$; $T_N\lt \theta$ for positive $\alpha$; $T_N$ rises with $\beta$.

$T\lt T_N$. $\chi_\perp$ is almost constant; $\chi_\parallel$ falls to zero as $T\to0$; both equal $\chi$ at $T_N$ (Fig. 8.11).

8.7 Ferrimagnetism

The sublattice magnetisations differ in size, so there is a net moment. Ferrites such as magnetite, $\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}$, have one $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ on tetrahedral A sites and one $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ and one $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ on octahedral B sites. The net moment of a formula unit is that of one $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ ion (Fig. 8.12), and a spinel cell has eight formula units. Ferrimagnets show spontaneous magnetisation and hysteresis like ferromagnets. Néel's theory explains the antiparallel alignment through the negative AB interaction; above $T_C$ the sublattices obey the Curie–Weiss law, below it Eq. (8.46) applies. The detailed theory is outside the book's scope.

Plus Two recall: a dielectric in a capacitor

Recall. A dielectric of constant $K$ between capacitor plates gives $C=KC_0$.

Re-derive.

  1. Polarisation creates bound charges that oppose the free-charge field.
  2. The resultant field is therefore smaller than the applied field.
  3. For fixed charge, $C$ rises by the factor $K$.

Saved. Eq. (9.1) and Eq. (9.5).

9.1 Polarisation and susceptibility

The polarisation field opposes the applied field, so

$$\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{E}_o+\mathbf{E}_p\tag{9.1}$$
$$\mathbf{P}=\varepsilon_o\chi_e\mathbf{E}\tag{9.2}$$

where $\chi_e$ is the electric susceptibility.

9.2 The local field

The local field at an atom with cubic symmetry is given by the Lorentz relation:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf{E}_{loc} &= \mathbf{E}_o+\mathbf{E}_p+\frac{\mathbf{P}}{3\varepsilon_o} \\ &= \mathbf{E}+\frac{\mathbf{P}}{3\varepsilon_o}\end{aligned} \tag{9.3}$$

The extra term is the field of the other atoms' polarisation. Its form depends on specimen shape.

9.3 Dielectric constant and polarisability

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf{D} &= \varepsilon_o\varepsilon_r\mathbf{E} \\ &= \varepsilon_o\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}\end{aligned} \tag{9.4}$$
$$\begin{aligned}\varepsilon_r &= \frac{\varepsilon_o\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}}{\varepsilon_o\mathbf{E}} \\ &= 1+\chi_e\end{aligned} \tag{9.5}$$

The polarisability of an atom is

$$p=\alpha E_{loc}\tag{9.6}$$

Polarisability is atomic; the dielectric constant is macroscopic. For $N$ identical atoms per unit volume:

$$P=Np=N\alpha E_{loc}\tag{9.7}$$
$$P=\sum_jN_j\alpha_jE_{loc}(j)\tag{9.8}$$

With cubic symmetry, (9.3) and (9.7) give

$$P=N\alpha\left(E+\frac{P}{3\varepsilon_o}\right)\tag{9.9}$$

which yields

$$\begin{aligned}\chi_e &= \frac{P}{\varepsilon_oE} \\ &= \frac{N\alpha/\varepsilon_o}{1-\dfrac{N\alpha}{3\varepsilon_o}}\end{aligned} \tag{9.10}$$
$$\varepsilon_r=\frac{1+\dfrac{2N\alpha}{3\varepsilon_o}}{1-\dfrac{N\alpha}{3\varepsilon_o}}\tag{9.11}$$
$$\frac{\varepsilon_r-1}{\varepsilon_r+2}=\frac{N\alpha}{3\varepsilon_o}\tag{9.12}$$

This is the Clausius–Mossotti relation. In general form:

$$\frac{\varepsilon_r-1}{\varepsilon_r+2}=\frac{1}{3\varepsilon_o}\sum_jN_j\alpha_j\tag{9.13}$$

9.4 Sources of polarisability

Electronic, ionic and dipolar (orientational) polarisability make up the net value. Their relative weight depends on the material and on the frequency.

9.4.1 Electronic polarisability

Electronic polarisation is the displacement of the electron cloud relative to the nucleus (Fig. 9.2). At optical frequencies the dielectric constant is mainly electronic. With $\varepsilon_r=n^2$:

$$\frac{n^2-1}{n^2+2}=\frac{1}{3\varepsilon_o}\sum_jN_j\alpha_j\quad\text{(electronic)}\tag{9.14}$$

Classical resonance and static polarisability:

$$\omega_o=\sqrt{\beta/m}\tag{9.15}$$
$$-eE_{loc}=\beta x=m\omega_o^2x\tag{9.16}$$
$$\alpha_e=\frac{p}{E_{loc}}=-\frac{ex}{E_{loc}}=\frac{e^2}{m\omega_o^2}\tag{9.17}$$
$$m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}+m\omega_o^2x=-eE_{loc}\sin\omega t\tag{9.18}$$
$$m(-\omega^2+\omega_o^2)x_o=-eE_{loc}\tag{9.19}$$
$$\alpha_e=\frac{p_o}{E_{loc}}=\frac{e^2/m}{\omega_o^2-\omega^2}\tag{9.20}$$
$$\alpha_e=\frac{e^2}{m}\sum_j\frac{f_{ij}}{\omega_{ij}^2-\omega^2}\tag{9.21}$$

9.4.2 Ionic polarisability

$$\beta\Delta x\cong eE\tag{9.22}$$
$$\alpha_i=\frac{p}{E}=\frac{e\Delta x}{E}\cong\frac{e^2}{\beta}\tag{9.23}$$

For $\beta\simeq20$ N/m, $\alpha_i\simeq10^{-39}$ F m$^2$. NaCl: $\varepsilon_r\simeq5.6$ at low frequency, 2.25 at optical frequencies.

9.4.3 Dipolar polarisability

A polar molecule has a permanent dipole. The energy at angle $\theta$ (Fig. 9.3) is

$$U=-\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{E}=-pE\cos\theta\tag{9.24}$$
$$\exp\left(-\frac{U}{kT}\right)d\Omega=\exp\left(\frac{pE\cos\theta}{kT}\right)2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\tag{9.25}$$
$$2\pi p\exp\left(\frac{pE\cos\theta}{kT}\right)\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta\tag{9.26}$$
$$\begin{aligned}\bar p &= \frac{P}{N} \\ &= \frac{p\int_0^\pi\exp\left(\frac{pE\cos\theta}{kT}\right)\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta}{\int_0^\pi\exp\left(\frac{pE\cos\theta}{kT}\right)\sin\theta\,d\theta}\end{aligned} \tag{9.27}$$
$$\frac{\bar p}{p}=\coth x-\frac{1}{x}=L(x)\tag{9.28}$$
$$L(x)\simeq\frac{x}{3}=\frac{pE}{3kT}\tag{9.29}$$
$$P=N\bar p=\frac{Np^2E}{3kT}\tag{9.30}$$
$$\alpha_d=\frac{\bar p}{E}=\frac{p^2}{3kT}\tag{9.31}$$

9.5 Frequency dependence of the total polarisability

$$\alpha=\alpha_e+\alpha_i+\alpha_d\tag{9.32}$$

The total falls with frequency. Relaxation times are longest for dipolar, then ionic, then electronic processes, so the dipolar part disappears first.

Worked Example 9.1

NaCl with $n=1.5$, $\varepsilon_r=5.6$. The static and optical Clausius–Mossotti relations are

$$\frac{\varepsilon_r-1}{\varepsilon_r+2}=\frac{N(\alpha_e+\alpha_i)}{3\varepsilon_0}\tag{9.40}$$
$$\frac{n^2-1}{n^2+2}=\frac{N\alpha_e}{3\varepsilon_0}\tag{9.41}$$

Dividing (9.41) by (9.40):

$$\left(\frac{n^2-1}{n^2+2}\right)\left(\frac{\varepsilon_r+2}{\varepsilon_r-1}\right)=\frac{\alpha_e}{\alpha_e+\alpha_i}\tag{9.42}$$

The ionic share is $[1-(9.42)]\times100=51.4\%$.

9.6 Ferroelectricity

Ferroelectricity is spontaneous polarisation, the electrical analogue of spontaneous magnetisation. Centres of positive and negative charge do not coincide in the absence of a field. The polarisation against field forms a hysteresis loop. Above the Curie point $T_C$ the crystal is paraelectric and the dielectric constant falls sharply.

Examples are $\mathrm{BaTiO_3}$ and Rochelle salt. Order–disorder ferroelectrics (for example $\mathrm{KH_2PO_4}$, $\mathrm{RbH_2PO_4}$) transform by ionic ordering, and displacive ones ($\mathrm{BaTiO_3}$, $\mathrm{KNbO_3}$, $\mathrm{LiTaO_3}$) by the displacement of one sublattice.

In $\mathrm{BaTiO_3}$ above about 380 K the cell is cubic, with $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ at the corners, $\mathrm{O^{2-}}$ at the face centres and $\mathrm{Ti^{4+}}$ at the body centre (Fig. 9.5). Below $T_C$ the Ti and Ba ions move up and the O ions move down, the structure becomes tetragonal, and a spontaneous polarisation appears.

$$\varepsilon_r=\frac{1+\dfrac{2}{3\varepsilon_o}\sum_jN_j\alpha_j}{1-\dfrac{1}{3\varepsilon_o}\sum_jN_j\alpha_j}\tag{9.33}$$

The dielectric constant diverges when

$$\sum_jN_j\alpha_j=3\varepsilon_o\tag{9.34}$$

This is the polarisation catastrophe: the local field grows faster than the restoring force, and higher-order forces limit the displacement. Writing $\tfrac{1}{3\varepsilon_o}\sum_jN_j\alpha_j=1-3s$ with $s\ll1$:

$$\frac{1}{3\varepsilon_o}\sum_jN_j\alpha_j=1-3s\tag{9.35}$$
$$\varepsilon_r=\frac{1}{s}-2\cong\frac{1}{s}\tag{9.36}$$
$$s\cong\frac{T-T_C}{\xi}\tag{9.37}$$
$$\varepsilon_r\cong\frac{\xi}{T-T_C}\tag{9.38}$$

This matches the paraelectric state qualitatively.

9.7 Piezoelectricity

A stress induces polarisation in a piezoelectric crystal, and an electric field strains it (quartz, Rochelle salt, tourmaline). In one-dimensional notation:

$$\left.\begin{aligned}P&=Zd+\varepsilon_oE\chi\\e&=Zs+Ed\end{aligned}\right.\tag{9.39}$$

Strains are very small: in quartz about $10^{-8}$ for $10^4$ V/m. Piezoelectricity needs the absence of a centre of inversion (Fig. 9.6). All ferroelectrics are piezoelectric; not all piezoelectrics are ferroelectric ($\mathrm{BaTiO_3}$ and quartz are the examples). The devices are transducers: gramophone pick-ups, microphones, strain gauges and ultrasonic generators.
