3. Maxwell’s equations (§7.3)

3.1 Electrodynamics before Maxwell (§7.3.1)

Up to now we have four laws, one for each divergence and curl:

  • (i) $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0$ (Gauss’s law);
  • (ii) $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$ (no name);
  • (iii) $\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial\mathbf B/\partial t$ (Faraday’s law);
  • (iv) $\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J$ (Ampère’s law).

These are inconsistent. The divergence of a curl is always zero. Take the divergence of (iii): the left side is zero, and the right side is zero by (ii), so it works. Take the divergence of (iv): the left side is zero, but the right side is $\mu_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf J$, which is not zero unless the current is steady (Eq. 7.45). Ampère’s law is wrong for non-steady currents.

A second check on the same point is the charging capacitor (Fig. 7.43). For the flat surface spanning an Ampèrian loop, $I_{\text{enc}}=I$. For a balloon-shaped surface that passes between the plates, $I_{\text{enc}}=0$. The answer depends on the surface, so “the current enclosed” is ill-defined. The fault is that charge piles up on the capacitor plates, which never happens in magnetostatics.

Ampère’s law was derived from the Biot–Savart law, so there was no reason to expect it to hold beyond magnetostatics. The flaw was purely theoretical, and Maxwell fixed it theoretically.

3.2 How Maxwell fixed Ampère’s law (§7.3.2)

The extra divergence in Eq. 7.45 comes from the charge term. By the continuity equation and Gauss’s law,

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=-\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=-\nabla\cdot\left(\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}\right)$$

So adding $\mu_0\epsilon_0\,\partial\mathbf E/\partial t$ to Ampère’s law kills the extra divergence:

Result (Ampère–Maxwell law)
$$\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t} \tag{7.46}$$

Maxwell’s own motive was an ether model, now discredited. The continuity argument is the stronger reason. In magnetostatics the new term is zero, so nothing changes there. Its effects show up in waves (Chapter 9).

The new term is the displacement current:

Result (displacement current density)
$$\mathbf J_d\equiv\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t} \tag{7.47}$$

The name is misleading: it has nothing to do with the motion of charge. It is simply the term that adds to $\mathbf J$ in Ampère’s law. Now take the capacitor with small plate separation. The field between the plates is $E=\sigma/\epsilon_0=Q/\epsilon_0A$, so $\partial E/\partial t=I/\epsilon_0A$. In integral form,

$$\oint\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf l=\mu_0I_{\text{enc}}+\mu_0\epsilon_0\int\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}\cdot d\mathbf a \tag{7.48}$$

With the flat surface, $I_{\text{enc}}=I$ and the displacement term is zero. With the balloon surface, $I_{\text{enc}}=0$ and the displacement term is $I/\epsilon_0\cdot\epsilon_0=I$. Both give the same answer. Only the source of the current differs.

  • Example 7.15. Two concentric spherical shells, with a radial ohmic current $\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E$ between them, carry $\pm Q(t)$. The conduction current $\mathbf J$ and the displacement current $\epsilon_0\partial\mathbf E/\partial t=-\mathbf J$ cancel exactly, so $\mathbf B=\mathbf 0$, even though there is a current. This is because the situation is not static.

The displacement term matters only when $\mathbf E$ changes in time. For steady fields it is zero and Ampère’s law is unchanged.

3.3 Maxwell’s equations (§7.3.3)

The four equations are now complete:

Result (Maxwell’s equations in vacuum)
$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0},\quad\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\quad\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\quad\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t} \tag{7.49}$$

Together with the Lorentz force law $\mathbf F=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$ (Eq. 7.50), these contain all of classical electrodynamics, apart from the properties of matter. The continuity equation $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=-\partial\rho/\partial t$ (Eq. 7.51) follows from them by taking the divergence of (iv).

The book warns that Maxwell’s equations are differential equations and need boundary conditions to be solved. These are usually obvious from context, for example fields tending to zero far from a finite charge distribution.

Written with the fields on the left and the sources on the right, the equations read (Eq. 7.52):

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0},\quad\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\quad\nabla\times\mathbf E+\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}=\mathbf 0,\quad\nabla\times\mathbf B-\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}=\mu_0\mathbf J$$

This shows that the fields are ultimately caused by charges and currents. A changing field is itself produced by charges and currents.

Magnetic charge (§7.3.4, beyond the syllabus). Free space has a symmetry between $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$: replacing $\mathbf E\to\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf B\to-\mu_0\epsilon_0\mathbf E$ swaps the pairs of equations. If magnetic charge $\rho_m$ and current $\mathbf J_m$ existed, the equations would read $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=\mu_0\rho_m$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\mu_0\mathbf J_m-\partial\mathbf B/\partial t$ (Eq. 7.53), and the force on a monopole would follow from Problem 7.40. No magnetic monopole has ever been found. Dirac showed that magnetic charge would explain the quantisation of electric charge (Problem 8.21).

3.4 Maxwell’s equations in matter (§7.3.5)

Inside matter there are bound charges and currents that you cannot control directly. It is better to write the equations in terms of the free charges and currents only.

  • Electric polarization $\mathbf P$ gives a bound charge $\rho_b=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P$ (Eq. 7.56).
  • Magnetization $\mathbf M$ gives a bound current $\mathbf J_b=\nabla\times\mathbf M$ (Eq. 7.57).
  • A changing polarization gives a current too: the polarization current $\mathbf J_p=\partial\mathbf P/\partial t$ (Eq. 7.58). It is the motion of bound charge when $\mathbf P$ changes. Its divergence is $-\partial\rho_b/\partial t$, so it keeps bound charge conserved. A changing magnetization gives no equivalent charge flow.

The total charge and current split as

$$\rho=\rho_f-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P \tag{7.59}$$
$$\mathbf J=\mathbf J_f+\nabla\times\mathbf M+\frac{\partial\mathbf P}{\partial t} \tag{7.60}$$

Defining

$$\mathbf D\equiv\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P \tag{7.62}\qquad\mathbf H\equiv\frac{1}{\mu_0}\mathbf B-\mathbf M \tag{7.64}$$

the equations become (Eq. 7.65):

Result (Maxwell’s equations in terms of free charge and current)
$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f,\quad\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\quad\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\quad\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t} \tag{7.65}$$

These are not more general than Eq. 7.49. They are a division into free and bound parts. They mix $\mathbf E$ with $\mathbf D$ and $\mathbf B$ with $\mathbf H$, so they need constitutive relations. For linear media:

Result (linear media)
$$\mathbf P=\epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E,\qquad\mathbf M=\chi_m\mathbf H \tag{7.66}$$
$$\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E,\qquad\mathbf H=\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf B \tag{7.67}$$

with $\epsilon=\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)$ and $\mu=\mu_0(1+\chi_m)$.

In these materials, $\partial\mathbf D/\partial t$ is the displacement current density (Eq. 7.68), $\mathbf J_d\equiv\partial\mathbf D/\partial t$. It is the term that gives the displacement current its name. The Problem 7.42 sea-water example compares conduction current with displacement current at $4\times10^{8}$ Hz.

3.5 Boundary conditions (§7.3.6)

Fields are discontinuous at a boundary between media, or at a surface that carries charge $\sigma$ or current $\mathbf K$. The integral forms of Maxwell’s equations in free-charge form give the discontinuities:

  • (i) $\oint_{\mathcal S}\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf a=Q_{f,\text{enc}}$ and (ii) $\oint_{\mathcal S}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a=0$, for any closed surface $\mathcal S$;
  • (iii) $\oint_{\mathcal P}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a$ and (iv) $\oint_{\mathcal P}\mathbf H\cdot d\mathbf l=I_{\text{enc}}+\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf a$, for any surface $\mathcal S$ bounded by the loop $\mathcal P$.

A thin pillbox across the surface gives the normal components. An Ampèrian loop straddling the surface gives the tangential components. Results:

Result (normal components)
$$D^\perp_{\text{above}}-D^\perp_{\text{below}}=\sigma_f \tag{7.69}$$
$$B^\perp_{\text{above}}-B^\perp_{\text{below}}=0 \tag{7.70}$$
Result (tangential components)
$$\mathbf E^\parallel_{\text{above}}-\mathbf E^\parallel_{\text{below}}=\mathbf 0 \tag{7.71}$$
$$\mathbf H^\parallel_{\text{above}}-\mathbf H^\parallel_{\text{below}}=\mathbf K_f\times\hat{\mathbf n} \tag{7.72}$$

Here $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is the unit normal pointing from below to above.

In linear media, the general boundary conditions become (Eq. 7.73):

Result (linear media)
$$\epsilon_{\text{above}}E^\perp_{\text{above}}-\epsilon_{\text{below}}E^\perp_{\text{below}}=\sigma_f,\quad B^\perp_{\text{above}}-B^\perp_{\text{below}}=0$$
$$E^\parallel_{\text{above}}-E^\parallel_{\text{below}}=0,\quad\frac{1}{\mu_{\text{above}}}B^\parallel_{\text{above}}-\frac{1}{\mu_{\text{below}}}B^\parallel_{\text{below}}=\mathbf K_f\times\hat{\mathbf n}$$

With no free charge or free current at the interface (Eq. 7.74), the right-hand sides are zero. These conditions are the basis of reflection and refraction (Chapter 9).

4. Background: the field of a rotating magnet (§7.4, beyond the syllabus)

Does a rotating magnet drag its field round with it? Faraday’s experiments with a disk and a magnet (Fig. 7.51) say no for rotation about the symmetry axis. A rotating magnet about a perpendicular axis does drag its field, and in 1912 Barnett confirmed that the Earth’s field associated with its rotation is not dragged.

Field lines are only a way of drawing a field; the field has physical reality, and the labelling of field lines has no physical content (Figs. 7.52). Whether field lines “move” is a choice of picture. The magnetic case is subtle only because the Lorentz force uses the velocity of the charge, so the frame of reference matters. Footnote 34 of the book is wrong: moving field lines do not induce electric fields; changing fields do.
