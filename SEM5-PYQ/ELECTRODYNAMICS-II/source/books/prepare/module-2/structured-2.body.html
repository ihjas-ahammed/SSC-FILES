2. Electromagnetic induction (§7.2)

2.1 Faraday’s law (§7.2.1)

Experiments (Fig. 7.21). (1) A loop pulled through a field produces a current (motional emf, explained by the flux rule). (2) A magnet moved past a stationary loop produces the same current; the loop is at rest, so the emf cannot be magnetic. (3) Loop and magnet at rest, field strength varied: a current flows. In (2) and (3) the driving force on the charges is an electric field induced by $\partial\mathbf B/\partial t$.

Result 2.1 (Faraday’s law, integral form)
$$\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-\int\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}\cdot d\mathbf a \tag{7.24}$$
Result 2.2 (Faraday’s law, differential form; from Stokes’ theorem)
$$\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} \tag{7.25}$$

For constant $\mathbf B$ this reduces to $\nabla\times\mathbf E=\mathbf 0$.

Result 2.3 (universal flux rule)
$$\mathcal E=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \tag{7.26}$$

This holds whatever the cause of the flux change. Eq. 7.26 covers two mechanisms: a magnetic (Lorentz) force when the loop moves, and an induced electric field when $\mathbf B$ changes. The book does not use the name “Faraday’s law” for the first mechanism.

Lenz’s law. The induced current flows so that its own flux opposes the change in flux. The rule gives direction only.

  • Ex. 7.6. Magnet of length $L$ and magnetization $\mathbf M$ passing through a ring. $\Phi$ rises to $\mu_0M\pi a^2$, then falls to zero; $\mathcal E$ has two spikes, of opposite sign (Fig. 7.23).
  • Ex. 7.7 (jumping ring). Before switching on, $\Phi=0$. After switching on, $\Phi$ rises, so the induced current opposes the solenoid current; the ring is repelled.

2.2 The induced electric field (§7.2.2)

For a pure Faraday field ($\rho=0$, due to $\partial\mathbf B/\partial t$ alone):

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0,\qquad\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}$$

This is the magnetostatic system with $\mu_0\mathbf J$ replaced by $-\partial\mathbf B/\partial t$. By analogy with the Biot–Savart law,

$$\mathbf E=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\frac{(\partial\mathbf B/\partial t)\times\hat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}\,d\tau \tag{7.27}$$

In symmetric cases, Faraday’s law on an Ampèrian loop gives

Result 2.4 (symmetric cases)
$$\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \tag{7.28}$$
  • Ex. 7.8. $\mathbf B(t)$ uniform over a disk of radius $R$. Loop of radius $s$: $E\cdot2\pi s=-\pi s^2\,dB/dt$, so $\mathbf E=-\frac{s}{2}\frac{dB}{dt}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$.
  • Ex. 7.9 (wheel). Line charge $\lambda$ on a rim of radius $b$; field $B_0$ inside radius $a$ removed. $\mathbf E=-\frac{a^2}{2b}\frac{dB}{dt}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$. The torque is $N=-b\lambda\pi a^2\,dB/dt$; the angular momentum transferred is $\lambda\pi a^2bB_0$, independent of the rate of switch-off. The work is done by the induced electric field.

2.3 Quasistatic approximation (§7.2.2, continued)

Magnetostatic formulas are used to evaluate $\mathbf B$ in the right-hand side of Faraday’s law. This is valid if $\partial\mathbf B/\partial t$ is slow enough; the regime is quasistatic.

  • Ex. 7.10. Infinite straight wire with $I(t)$: $\mathbf B=\mu_0I/2\pi s$ and
Result 2.5 (quasistatic induced field, infinite wire)
$$\mathbf E(s)=\left[\frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\frac{dI}{dt}\ln s+K\right]\hat{\mathbf z} \tag{7.29}$$

The constant $K$ depends on the history of $I(t)$. The result diverges as $s\to\infty$, which shows the quasistatic approximation fails there. It holds for $s\ll c\tau$ (Eq. 7.30), where $\tau$ is the time scale of $I$.

2.4 Inductance (§7.2.3)

Mutual inductance. The field of loop 1 is proportional to $I_1$ (Biot–Savart), so the flux through loop 2 is

Result 2.6 (mutual inductance)
$$\Phi_2=M_{21}I_1 \tag{7.31}$$

Expressing $\Phi_2=\int(\nabla\times\mathbf A_1)\cdot d\mathbf a_2=\oint\mathbf A_1\cdot d\mathbf l_2$ and using $\mathbf A_1=\frac{\mu_0I_1}{4\pi}\oint\frac{d\mathbf l_1}{r}$ gives the Neumann formula:

Result 2.7 (Neumann formula)
$$M_{21}=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint\oint\frac{d\mathbf l_1\cdot d\mathbf l_2}{r} \tag{7.32}$$

$M_{21}$ is purely geometric. Interchange of the loops leaves the integral unchanged: $M_{21}=M_{12}\equiv M$ (Eq. 7.33).

  • Ex. 7.11. Short solenoid (length $l$, radius $a$, $n_1$ turns per unit length) inside a long solenoid (radius $b$, $n_2$ turns per unit length). Driving the long solenoid with $I$ gives $B=\mu_0n_2I$ inside it; the flux through the short solenoid is $\mu_0\pi a^2n_1n_2Il$. Hence $M=\mu_0\pi a^2n_1n_2l$.

For a time-dependent current in loop 1, Faraday’s law gives $\mathcal E_2=-M\,dI_1/dt$ (Eq. 7.34), in the quasistatic regime.

Self inductance. The flux through a loop is proportional to its own current:

Result 2.8 (self inductance)
$$\Phi=LI \tag{7.35}$$
$$\mathcal E=-L\frac{dI}{dt} \tag{7.36}$$

$L$ is positive, depends only on geometry, and is measured in henries (H = V·s/A). The minus sign is the back emf.

  • Ex. 7.12. Toroid of rectangular cross section ($a$, $b$, $h$), $N$ turns: $B=\mu_0NI/2\pi s$; flux per turn $\frac{\mu_0NIh}{2\pi}\ln(b/a)$; $L=\frac{\mu_0N^2h}{2\pi}\ln(b/a)$ (Eq. 7.37).
  • Ex. 7.13 (RL circuit). Emf $\mathcal E_0$ switched on at $t=0$ with resistance $R$ and inductance $L$. $\mathcal E_0-L\,dI/dt=IR$; with $I(0)=0$:
Result 2.9 (current growth in an RL circuit)
$$I(t)=\frac{\mathcal E_0}{R}\left[1-e^{-(R/L)t}\right] \tag{7.38}$$

Time constant $\tau=L/R$. At $t=\tau$ the current is about two-thirds of $\mathcal E_0/R$.

2.5 Energy in magnetic fields (§7.2.4)

The work done against the back emf is $\int(-\mathcal E)I\,dt$, with $-\mathcal E\,I=LI\,dI/dt$. Building the current to $I$ from zero requires

Result 2.10 (energy of a circuit)
$$W=\tfrac12LI^2 \tag{7.39}$$

which depends only on $L$ and $I_{\text{final}}$. Using $LI=\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l$ and the generalisation to volume currents:

$$W=\frac12\oint(\mathbf A\cdot I)\,d\mathbf l \tag{7.40}\qquad W=\frac12\int(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf J)\,d\tau \tag{7.41}$$

Substituting $\mathbf J=\nabla\times\mathbf B/\mu_0$ and integrating by parts with $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)=\mathbf B\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf A)-\mathbf A\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)$ gives a surface term that vanishes when the volume is all space.

Result 2.11 (energy in the magnetic field)
$$W=\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int_{\text{all space}}B^2\,d\tau \tag{7.44}$$

Energy density $B^2/2\mu_0$. Its location is a matter of bookkeeping; the total $W$ is the physical quantity.

The energy is supplied through the induced electric field present while $\mathbf B$ is changing. Electrostatic energy is $W_{\text{elec}}=\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$ (Eq. 2.45).

  • Ex. 7.14 (coaxial cable). $\mathbf B=\mu_0I/2\pi s\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$ between radii $a$ and $b$. Energy per unit length $\frac{\mu_0I^2}{4\pi}\ln(b/a)$; comparison with $\frac12LI^2$ gives $L=\frac{\mu_0 l}{2\pi}\ln(b/a)$ for length $l$. This method defines $L$ when the flux is hard to find.
