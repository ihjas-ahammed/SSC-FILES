7.2.1 Faraday’s law

Source: book-sources/m2-sec2-faraday.html, Griffiths pp. 316–319. Equations 7.23–7.26; Examples 7.6 and 7.7 (Lenz’s law) in full.

Prerequisites

  • Magnetic flux $\Phi=\int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a$ and the flux rule $\mathcal E=-d\Phi/dt$ (Eq. 7.13, Section 7.1.3).
  • Stokes’ theorem: $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=\int(\nabla\times\mathbf E)\cdot d\mathbf a$.
  • Lorentz force $\mathbf F=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$ (Eq. 7.50).

1. Three experiments

Faraday’s experiments of 1831 each produced a current in a loop of wire. Their common result is stated in Section 3.

  • Experiment 1. The loop is pulled to the right through a magnetic field (Fig. 7.21a). The charges in the moving part of the loop feel the force $q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$. This is motional emf (Section 7.1.3); the flux rule gives it directly.
  • Experiment 2. The magnet is moved to the left and the loop is held still (Fig. 7.21b). The same current flows. Only the relative motion matters, which is why the emf is the same as in Experiment 1. But the loop is at rest. A stationary charge feels no magnetic force, $q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B=\mathbf 0$ when $\mathbf v=\mathbf 0$. So the force on the charges must be electric.
  • Experiment 3. Loop and magnet both at rest, while the field strength is changed (an electromagnet with a varying coil current, Fig. 7.21c). The current flows again. The force is again electric.

2. The induced electric field

Faraday’s inference: a changing magnetic field induces an electric field. In Experiments 2 and 3 that induced field drives the charges round the loop. In Experiment 1 the magnetic force does the driving. The book uses the name “Faraday’s law” only for the induced electric field.

Plus Two recall: Lenz’s law

Recall. From Class 12: the induced current opposes the change in flux that produces it.

Re-derive.

  1. Suppose the flux through a loop increases, so $d\Phi/dt>0$. By Eq. 7.26, $\mathcal E=-d\Phi/dt<0$: the induced emf drives current in the sense that produces a field opposing the increase.
  2. Suppose the flux decreases, so $d\Phi/dt<0$. Then $\mathcal E>0$ and the induced field points along the original field, opposing the decrease.
  3. In both cases the induced current tries to keep the flux unchanged.

Saved. The induced current opposes the change in flux (direction only, from Eq. 7.26).

3. Faraday’s law in integral and differential form

Assume the emf round a loop equals the rate of change of flux, as Experiments 2 and 3 show. Then

$$\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \tag{7.23}$$

Write the flux as a surface integral. Since $\Phi=\int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a$ and the loop is fixed while $\mathbf B$ changes, the time derivative acts on $\mathbf B$:

$$\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-\int\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}\cdot d\mathbf a \tag{7.24}$$

This is Faraday’s law in integral form. Apply Stokes’ theorem to the left side: $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=\int(\nabla\times\mathbf E)\cdot d\mathbf a$. Both sides are now surface integrals over the same surface. The surface is arbitrary, so the integrands are equal:

Result 2.1: Faraday’s law, differential form
$$\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} \tag{7.25}$$

When $\mathbf B$ is constant, this gives $\nabla\times\mathbf E=\mathbf 0$, the electrostatic rule.

4. The universal flux rule

All three experiments give the same formula, whatever changes the flux:

Result 2.2: universal flux rule
$$\mathcal E=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \tag{7.26}$$

Whenever the flux through a loop changes, for whatever reason, an emf $-d\Phi/dt$ appears in the loop.

The book objects to calling Eq. 7.26 “Faraday’s law”. It combines two mechanisms: a magnetic force on moving charges (Experiment 1) and an induced electric field (Experiments 2 and 3). Keep the two apart when you calculate a particular case.

5. Lenz’s law: Example 7.6

A long cylindrical magnet of length $L$ and radius $a$ carries a uniform magnetization $\mathbf M$ parallel to its axis. It passes at constant velocity through a ring of slightly larger diameter (Fig. 7.22).

  • Before the magnet reaches the ring, the flux through it is zero.
  • As the leading end enters, the field of the magnet ($\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf M$ inside a long magnet) gives a flux that rises to $\mu_0M\pi a^2$.
  • While the magnet is fully inside the ring the flux is constant, so there is no emf.
  • As the trailing end leaves, the flux falls back to zero.

So $\Phi$ rises, plateaus and falls (Fig. 7.23a), and $\mathcal E=-d\Phi/dt$ has two spikes of opposite sign (Fig. 7.23b). The first spike is negative: the first current pulse flows clockwise, and the second counterclockwise, as seen from the left (Lenz’s law gives the sense for each spike).

6. Lenz’s law: Example 7.7, the jumping ring

A solenoid with an iron core carries a current, and a metal ring rests on top of it (Fig. 7.24). When the current is switched on, the ring flies off.

{{FIG:m2-sec2-jumping-ring}}
  1. Before the current is switched on, the flux through the ring is zero.
  2. After switching on, a flux appears through the ring, pointing up (in the diagram).
  3. By Lenz’s law, the current induced in the ring produces a field opposing this new flux, so the ring current is opposite to the solenoid current.
  4. Opposite currents repel, so the ring is pushed away. This is the jumping ring.

Footnote 15 refers to further studies of the jumping ring and the floating ring.
