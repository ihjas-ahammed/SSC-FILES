2. Electromagnetic induction (§7.2)

2.1 Faraday’s law (§7.2.1)

In 1831 Faraday did three experiments with a loop and a magnet:

  • Experiment 1. He pulled a loop through a magnetic field. A current flowed. This is motional emf, and the flux rule gives it: $\mathcal E=-d\Phi/dt$.
  • Experiment 2. He moved the magnet and held the loop still. A current flowed again. The emf is the same as in Experiment 1, because only the relative motion matters. But now the loop is at rest, so the magnetic force cannot push the charges (stationary charges feel no magnetic force). Something else must be driving them.
  • Experiment 3. Loop and magnet both at rest, and the field strength changed (using an electromagnet). Current flowed again.

Faraday’s inspiration: a changing magnetic field induces an electric field. In Experiments 2 and 3 the induced electric field pushes the charges. Experiment 1 is not Faraday’s law in this sense, because there the magnetic force does the work. The book reserves “Faraday’s law” for the case where the electric field is induced by a changing magnetic field.

If the emf around a loop equals the rate of change of flux, then $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-d\Phi/dt$ (Eq. 7.23), and therefore

Result (Faraday’s law, integral form)
$$\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-\int\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}\cdot d\mathbf a \tag{7.24}$$

Applying Stokes’ theorem gives the differential form:

Result (Faraday’s law, differential form)
$$\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} \tag{7.25}$$

When $\mathbf B$ is constant, this reduces to $\nabla\times\mathbf E=\mathbf 0$, the electrostatic rule.

Universal flux rule. All three experiments give the same formula, whatever the reason for the flux change:

Result (universal flux rule)
$$\mathcal E=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \tag{7.26}$$

Whenever the magnetic flux through a loop changes, an emf $\mathcal E=-d\Phi/dt$ appears in the loop.

The book objects to calling Eq. 7.26 “Faraday’s law”, because it covers two different mechanisms: a magnetic force in Experiment 1 and an induced electric field in the other two. Keep the two mechanisms apart when you calculate.

Lenz’s law. The induced current flows in the direction that makes its own flux oppose the change. Nature “abhors a change in flux”, not the flux itself. The rule gives direction only; the induced current is usually a tiny fraction of the original.

  • Example 7.6. A long magnet with uniform magnetization $\mathbf M$ passes through a ring. The flux is zero, rises to $\mu_0M\pi a^2$ as the leading end enters, and falls back to zero as the trailing end leaves. The emf has two spikes of opposite sign (Fig. 7.23). Lenz’s law gives the current direction for each spike.
  • Example 7.7 (jumping ring). A metal ring placed on a solenoid with an iron core is repelled when the current is switched on. Before switching on, the flux through the ring is zero. Afterwards, the flux rises, so the induced current in the ring must be opposite to the solenoid current. Opposite currents repel.

2.2 The induced electric field (§7.2.2)

For a pure Faraday field (caused only by a changing $\mathbf B$, with $\rho=0$):

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0,\qquad\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}$$

This has the same mathematical form as magnetostatics, with $\mu_0\mathbf J$ replaced by $-\partial\mathbf B/\partial t$. So the induced field can be found the way a magnetic field is found from a current. The Biot–Savart analogue is

$$\mathbf E=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\frac{(\partial\mathbf B/\partial t)\times\hat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}\,d\tau \tag{7.27}$$

When symmetry allows, use Faraday’s law in integral form on an Ampèrian loop:

Result (integral form for symmetric cases)
$$\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \tag{7.28}$$

The rate of change of flux through the Ampèrian loop plays the role that $\mu_0I_{\text{enc}}$ plays in Ampère’s law.

  • Example 7.8. A uniform $\mathbf B(t)$ fills a circle of radius $R$. For a loop of radius $s$: $E(2\pi s)=-\pi s^2\,dB/dt$, so $\mathbf E=-\tfrac{s}{2}(dB/dt)\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$. If $B$ increases, $\mathbf E$ runs clockwise, as seen from above.
  • Example 7.9 (the wheel). A line charge $\lambda$ on the rim of a wheel of radius $b$, with a field $\mathbf B_0$ in the central region of radius $a$ that is switched off. The induced field is $\mathbf E=-(a^2/2b)(dB/dt)\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$. The torque on the rim is $N=-b\lambda\pi a^2\,dB/dt$, and the total angular momentum given to the wheel is $\lambda\pi a^2bB_0$. It does not matter how fast the field is turned off. The electric field, not the magnetic field, does the work on the charge.

2.3 The quasistatic approximation (§7.2.2, continued)

Faraday’s law needs $\mathbf B$ to be changing, but the magnetostatic formulas (Ampère’s law and Biot–Savart) are used to find $\mathbf B$. This is only approximately right, and it works when the field changes slowly. The regime is called quasistatic. Electromagnetic waves and radiation need the full theory.

  • Example 7.10. An infinite wire carries $I(t)$. In the quasistatic approximation $\mathbf B=\mu_0I/2\pi s$, so $\mathbf E=\left[\frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\frac{dI}{dt}\ln s+K\right]\hat{\mathbf z}$ (Eq. 7.29), where $K$ depends on the history of $I(t)$. This answer blows up at large $s$, which cannot be right. The quasistatic approximation holds only for $s\ll c\tau$ (Eq. 7.30), where $\tau$ is the time over which $I$ changes.

2.4 Inductance (§7.2.3)

Mutual inductance. Run a steady current $I_1$ around loop 1. It produces $\mathbf B_1$, which is proportional to $I_1$ by the Biot–Savart law, so the flux through loop 2 is also proportional to $I_1$:

Result (mutual inductance)
$$\Phi_2=M_{21}I_1 \tag{7.31}$$

$M_{21}$ is the mutual inductance. Writing the flux in terms of the vector potential and using Stokes’ theorem gives the Neumann formula:

$$M_{21}=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint\oint\frac{d\mathbf l_1\cdot d\mathbf l_2}{r} \tag{7.32}$$

$M_{21}$ depends only on the geometry. Swapping the roles of the loops does not change the integral, so $M_{21}=M_{12}$ (Eq. 7.33). We write $M$.

  • Example 7.11. A short solenoid of length $l$ and radius $a$ (with $n_1$ turns per unit length) lies inside a long solenoid of radius $b$ ($n_2$ turns per unit length). Run the current through the long solenoid instead: the field inside it is $B=\mu_0n_2I$, the flux through the short solenoid is $\mu_0\pi a^2n_1n_2Il$, and so $M=\mu_0\pi a^2n_1n_2l$.

If the current in loop 1 changes, Faraday’s law gives an emf in loop 2, $\mathcal E_2=-M\,dI_1/dt$ (Eq. 7.34). The current changes slowly enough for the quasistatic approximation to apply. There are no wires between the loops.

Self inductance. A changing current also induces an emf in its own loop. The flux is proportional to the current, $\Phi=LI$ (Eq. 7.35), so

Result (self inductance)
$$\mathcal E=-L\frac{dI}{dt} \tag{7.36}$$

Inductance is measured in henries (H); 1 H = 1 V·s/A. Inductance is always positive. The minus sign is the back emf, which opposes any change in current. Inductance plays for currents the role that mass plays for velocity.

  • Example 7.12. A toroid with rectangular cross section (inner radius $a$, outer radius $b$, height $h$), carrying $N$ turns: $B=\mu_0NI/2\pi s$, the flux per turn is $\frac{\mu_0NIh}{2\pi}\ln(b/a)$, and the total flux is $N$ times that. So $L=\frac{\mu_0N^2h}{2\pi}\ln(b/a)$ (Eq. 7.37).
  • Example 7.13: the RL circuit. A battery of emf $\mathcal E_0$ connects to a resistor $R$ and an inductor $L$ at $t=0$. Ohm’s law gives $\mathcal E_0-L\,dI/dt=IR$. With $I(0)=0$:
Result (RL growth)
$$I(t)=\frac{\mathcal E_0}{R}\left[1-e^{-(R/L)t}\right] \tag{7.38}$$

The time constant is $\tau=L/R$. After one time constant the current has reached about two-thirds of its final value $\mathcal E_0/R$. Without inductance the current would jump to $\mathcal E_0/R$ at once.

2.5 Energy in magnetic fields (§7.2.4)

Building up a current against the back emf takes work. This work is not lost as heat. It is stored, and it is returned when the current is switched off. The work per unit time against the back emf is $-\mathcal EI=LI\,dI/dt$, so building the current up to $I$ takes

Result (energy of a circuit)
$$W=\tfrac12LI^2 \tag{7.39}$$

This depends only on $L$ and the final current, not on how long the process takes. Writing $LI=\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l$ gives $W=\frac12\oint(\mathbf A\cdot I)\,d\mathbf l$ (Eq. 7.40), which generalises to volume currents:

$$W=\frac12\int(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf J)\,d\tau \tag{7.41}$$

Using Ampère’s law, $\mathbf J=\nabla\times\mathbf B/\mu_0$, and integration by parts (product rule 6), the energy can be written in terms of $\mathbf B$ alone:

Result (energy in the magnetic field)
$$W=\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int_{\text{all space}}B^2\,d\tau \tag{7.44}$$

The energy is “stored in the field”, with density $B^2/2\mu_0$. The boundary term from the integration by parts vanishes when the integral covers all space. The book notes that the choice of where the energy is “located” is bookkeeping; only the total matters.

Why does building a magnetic field take energy? Because a changing $\mathbf B$ induces an electric field, and that field does work while $\mathbf B$ grows. Electrostatic energy has the same form: $W_{\text{elec}}=\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$ (Eq. 2.45).

  • Example 7.14. A long coaxial cable carries current $I$ down the inner cylinder (radius $a$) and back along the outer one (radius $b$). The field between the cylinders is $\mu_0I/2\pi s$, and the energy per unit length is $\frac{\mu_0I^2}{4\pi}\ln(b/a)$. Comparing with $\frac12LI^2$ gives $L=\frac{\mu_0 l}{2\pi}\ln(b/a)$ per length $l$. This is a good way to find $L$ when the current spreads over a surface or volume, because then the flux is hard to find directly.
