3. Maxwell’s equations (§7.3)

3.1 Electrodynamics before Maxwell (§7.3.1)

The laws available are

  • (i) $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0$ (Gauss); (ii) $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$; (iii) $\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial\mathbf B/\partial t$ (Faraday); (iv) $\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J$ (Ampère).

Inconsistency. Since $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf X)\equiv0$, the divergence of (iv) must vanish:

$$\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)=\mu_0(\nabla\cdot\mathbf J)\quad\text{(7.45)}$$

The left side is zero; the right side is not zero when $\partial\rho/\partial t\neq0$. Applying the same test to (iii) gives $-\partial(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)/\partial t=0$, which is consistent. Hence (iv) fails for non-steady currents.

Capacitor test (Fig. 7.43). The flat surface spanning an Ampèrian loop gives $I_{\text{enc}}=I$; a balloon surface between the plates gives $I_{\text{enc}}=0$. “The current enclosed” depends on the surface.

Ampère’s law was derived from the Biot–Savart law and has no validity guarantee outside magnetostatics; the defect is theoretical, not experimental.

3.2 How Maxwell fixed Ampère’s law (§7.3.2)

By the continuity equation and Gauss’s law,

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=-\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=-\nabla\cdot\left(\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}\right)$$

Adding $\mu_0\epsilon_0\,\partial\mathbf E/\partial t$ to Ampère’s law removes the inconsistency:

Result 3.1 (Ampère–Maxwell law)
$$\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t} \tag{7.46}$$

The term changes nothing in magnetostatics. Its importance for waves is treated in Chapter 9. The argument from charge conservation is more compelling than Maxwell’s ether-based argument.

Result 3.2 (displacement current)
$$\mathbf J_d\equiv\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t} \tag{7.47}$$

This is not a current of charge; it is the term added to $\mathbf J$. The integral form is

$$\oint\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf l=\mu_0I_{\text{enc}}+\mu_0\epsilon_0\int\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}\cdot d\mathbf a \tag{7.48}$$

Capacitor check. Between closely spaced plates $E=Q/\epsilon_0A$, so $\partial E/\partial t=I/\epsilon_0A$. The flat surface gives $\mu_0I$ from conduction; the balloon surface gives $\mu_0\epsilon_0\cdot I/\epsilon_0=\mu_0I$ from displacement. Both agree.

  • Ex. 7.15. Concentric spherical shells with a radial ohmic current $\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E$ and charge $\pm Q(t)$. The displacement current $\mathbf J_d=-\sigma Q\hat{\mathbf r}/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$ exactly cancels $\mathbf J$. Hence $\mathbf B=\mathbf 0$ despite the current, because the configuration is not static.

3.3 Maxwell’s equations (§7.3.3)

Result 3.3 (Maxwell’s equations in vacuum, with sources)
$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0},\quad\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\quad\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\quad\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t} \tag{7.49}$$

With the Lorentz force $\mathbf F=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$ (Eq. 7.50), these equations contain the whole classical theory apart from the properties of matter. The continuity equation $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=-\partial\rho/\partial t$ (Eq. 7.51) follows from them.

Boundary conditions must be supplied with the equations; they are usually implicit in the problem (for example, fields vanish far from a localised source).

With fields on the left and sources on the right (Eq. 7.52):

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0},\quad\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\quad\nabla\times\mathbf E+\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}=\mathbf 0,\quad\nabla\times\mathbf B-\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}=\mu_0\mathbf J$$

Fields are caused by charges and currents; changing fields propagate such effects.

Background: magnetic charge (§7.3.4, outside the syllabus). Replacing $\mathbf E\to\mathbf B$, $\mathbf B\to-\mu_0\epsilon_0\mathbf E$ exchanges the two pairs of source-free equations. Allowing magnetic sources, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=\mu_0\rho_m$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\mu_0\mathbf J_m-\partial\mathbf B/\partial t$ (Eq. 7.53). The force law for a monopole is Problem 7.40. No magnetic monopole has been found; their existence would explain the quantisation of electric charge (Prob. 8.21).

3.4 Maxwell’s equations in matter (§7.3.5)

  • Bound charge $\rho_b=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P$ (Eq. 7.56). Bound current from magnetization $\mathbf J_b=\nabla\times\mathbf M$ (Eq. 7.57).
  • Polarization current $\mathbf J_p=\partial\mathbf P/\partial t$ (Eq. 7.58). Its divergence $-\partial\rho_b/\partial t$ satisfies continuity; a changing $\mathbf M$ produces no analogous charge accumulation.
$$\rho=\rho_f-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P \tag{7.59}\qquad\mathbf J=\mathbf J_f+\nabla\times\mathbf M+\frac{\partial\mathbf P}{\partial t} \tag{7.60}$$
$$\mathbf D\equiv\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P \tag{7.62}\qquad\mathbf H\equiv\frac1{\mu_0}\mathbf B-\mathbf M \tag{7.64}$$
Result 3.4 (free-charge form)
$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f,\quad\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\quad\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\quad\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t} \tag{7.65}$$

These are a division of the sources, not a more general theory than Eq. 7.49. Constitutive relations are required. For linear media:

$$\mathbf P=\epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E,\quad\mathbf M=\chi_m\mathbf H \tag{7.66}\qquad\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E,\quad\mathbf H=\frac1\mu\mathbf B \tag{7.67}$$

where $\epsilon=\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)$ and $\mu=\mu_0(1+\chi_m)$. The displacement current in matter is $\mathbf J_d=\partial\mathbf D/\partial t$ (Eq. 7.68).

3.5 Boundary conditions (§7.3.6)

Integral forms of Eq. 7.65:

  • (i) $\oint_{\mathcal S}\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf a=Q_{f,\text{enc}}$; (ii) $\oint_{\mathcal S}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a=0$ — for any closed surface $\mathcal S$;
  • (iii) $\oint_{\mathcal P}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a$; (iv) $\oint_{\mathcal P}\mathbf H\cdot d\mathbf l=I_{\text{enc}}+\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal S}\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf a$ — for any surface $\mathcal S$ bounded by $\mathcal P$.

A thin pillbox gives the normal components; a thin Ampèrian loop gives the tangential components.

Result 3.5 (normal components)
$$D^\perp_{\text{above}}-D^\perp_{\text{below}}=\sigma_f \tag{7.69}\qquad B^\perp_{\text{above}}-B^\perp_{\text{below}}=0 \tag{7.70}$$
Result 3.6 (tangential components)
$$\mathbf E^\parallel_{\text{above}}-\mathbf E^\parallel_{\text{below}}=\mathbf 0 \tag{7.71}\qquad\mathbf H^\parallel_{\text{above}}-\mathbf H^\parallel_{\text{below}}=\mathbf K_f\times\hat{\mathbf n} \tag{7.72}$$

$\hat{\mathbf n}$ points from below to above.

In linear media (Eq. 7.73):

Result 3.7 (linear media)
$$\epsilon_{\text{above}}E^\perp_{\text{above}}-\epsilon_{\text{below}}E^\perp_{\text{below}}=\sigma_f,\qquad B^\perp_{\text{above}}-B^\perp_{\text{below}}=0$$
$$E^\parallel_{\text{above}}-E^\parallel_{\text{below}}=0,\qquad\frac1{\mu_{\text{above}}}B^\parallel_{\text{above}}-\frac1{\mu_{\text{below}}}B^\parallel_{\text{below}}=\mathbf K_f\times\hat{\mathbf n}$$

With no free charge or free current (Eq. 7.74), the right-hand sides vanish. These are the conditions used in reflection and refraction (Chapter 9).

4. Background: the field of a rotating magnet (§7.4, outside the syllabus)

Faraday’s disk-and-magnet experiments show that a magnet rotating about its symmetry axis does not drag its field round (Fig. 7.51). A magnet rotating about a perpendicular axis does; Barnett (1912) confirmed that the Earth’s magnetic field is not dragged by the rotation of the Earth. Field lines are a visual construct; their labelling has no physical content (Fig. 7.52). Statements about field lines “moving” are therefore a choice of description. Footnote 36 of the book corrects footnote 34: moving field lines do not induce electric fields, but time-dependent fields do.
