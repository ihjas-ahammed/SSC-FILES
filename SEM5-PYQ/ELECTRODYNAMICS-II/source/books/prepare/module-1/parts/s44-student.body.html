4.4 Linear dielectrics: my reading notes

Book pp. 186–194 and 202–208. Pages 195–201 are not in the excerpt, so the first part of "forces on dielectrics" is missing here.

The big idea first

Up to now the book kept P and E separate. In this section it finally says: if the material is linear, P is just a fixed fraction of E. Everything after this is that one line, used in different situations.

1. The rule: P is proportional to E

For many materials, a weak field gives a polarization that grows in step with it:

$$\mathbf P = \epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E \quad(4.30)$$

$\chi_e$ is the susceptibility. The $\epsilon_0$ is pulled out so that $\chi_e$ has no units. Its value depends on the material and on things like temperature.

A catch: E here is the total field, and part of it comes from the polarization itself. So you cannot just plug in the external field. The way out is to start from D, which only needs the free charge.

Footnote 10 says a strong field brings in a cubic term. Eq. 4.30 is only the first term of a series.

2. Where D and E become proportional

Working it out
$\mathbf D = \epsilon_0\mathbf E + \epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E = \epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)\mathbf E \quad(4.31)$
So $\mathbf D = \epsilon\mathbf E$ with $\epsilon = \epsilon_0(1+\chi_e) \quad(4.32, 4.33)$.
The dielectric constant is $\epsilon_r = 1+\chi_e = \epsilon/\epsilon_0 \quad(4.34)$.
  • In vacuum there is no polarization, so $\epsilon = \epsilon_0$. The book does not like the name "permittivity of free space", because it hides that vacuum is just a special case.
  • For ordinary materials $\epsilon_r$ is bigger than 1, because P usually points the same way as E.
  • The table of dielectric constants shows water at 80.1, silicon at 11.7, air at about 1.0005.

3. Example 4.5: a charged metal ball in a dielectric shell

A metal ball of radius $a$ carries charge $Q$. Dielectric fills the space out to radius $b$. I need the potential at the centre, but I can't get E without P, and I can't get P without E. The way out: use the free charge $Q$, which I know, and Gauss's law for D:

$$\mathbf D = \frac{Q}{4\pi r^2}\hat{\mathbf r} \quad (r>a)$$

Then $\mathbf E = \mathbf D/\epsilon$ in the dielectric and $\mathbf D/\epsilon_0$ outside:

Result
$\mathbf E = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon r^2}\hat{\mathbf r}$ for $ab$.

Integrating gives the centre potential $V = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi}\left(\dfrac{1}{\epsilon_0 b} + \dfrac{1}{\epsilon a} - \dfrac{1}{\epsilon b}\right)$.

The bound charge explains why the field drops: the dielectric near the metal gets a negative surface layer at $a$ (opposite to $Q$) and a positive one at $b$. A conducting shell would cancel the field completely; the dielectric cancels only part of it.

4. Whole space filled with one dielectric

If one homogeneous linear dielectric fills all the space, the curl of P is zero, so D depends only on the free charge. The field is just the vacuum field divided by $\epsilon_r$:

Result
$\mathbf E = \dfrac{1}{\epsilon_r}\mathbf E_{\text{vac}} \quad(4.35)$, and for a point charge $\mathbf E = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon}\dfrac{q}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf r} \quad(4.36)$.

Coulomb's law is not wrong. The bound charge simply shields the charge.

{{FIG:s44-point-charge-shielding}}

5. Example 4.6: a capacitor with dielectric

The field is trapped between the plates. Filling them with dielectric divides E, and so V, by $\epsilon_r$. For a fixed charge, the capacitance goes up by $\epsilon_r$:

Result
$C = \epsilon_r C_{\text{vac}} \quad(4.37)$. This is a common way to make a capacitor bigger.

6. Crystals: direction matters

Some crystals polarize more easily along some axes than others. Then each component of P depends on all three components of E, and there are nine susceptibilities (4.38). A material that behaves the same in every direction is called isotropic. When the book just says "linear dielectric", it means isotropic.

7. Boundary problems (background)

Inside a homogeneous linear dielectric, the bound charge is a fixed fraction of the free charge (4.39), so with no free charge inside, the potential obeys Laplace's equation. At a boundary, write the conditions in terms of free charge and potential:

$$\epsilon_{\text{above}}E^\perp_{\text{above}} - \epsilon_{\text{below}}E^\perp_{\text{below}} = \sigma_f \quad(4.40)$$
$$V_{\text{above}} = V_{\text{below}} \quad(4.42)$$

Example 4.7 is the dielectric sphere in a uniform field. It is like the conducting sphere of Ex. 3.8, but the cancellation is only partial:

  • The three conditions: continuous V at $r = R$; $\epsilon\,\partial V_{\text{in}}/\partial r = \epsilon_0\,\partial V_{\text{out}}/\partial r$ at $r = R$; and $V_{\text{out}} \to -E_0 r\cos\theta$ far away.
  • Expand inside and outside in Legendre polynomials, match term by term (only $l=1$ survives), and get
Result
$A_1 = -\dfrac{3}{\epsilon_r+2}E_0$, and inside $\mathbf E = \dfrac{3}{\epsilon_r+2}\mathbf E_0 \quad(4.49)$. The field inside is uniform, though weaker than $\mathbf E_0$.

8. Force on a dielectric slab (partly in excerpt)

A slab is pulled into a charged capacitor. The fringing fields that pull it are hard to work out, so use energy: the force is minus the change of energy per unit displacement. At fixed charge, $W = \tfrac12 Q^2/C$, so

Result
$F = -\dfrac{dW}{dx} = \tfrac12 V^2\dfrac{dC}{dx} \quad(4.64)$, and with $dC/dx = -\epsilon_0\chi_e w/d$: $F = -\dfrac{\epsilon_0\chi_e w}{2d}V^2 \quad(4.65)$.
  • The minus sign: the slab is pulled into the capacitor.
  • Careful with the sign. If you hold V fixed and use $\tfrac12 CV^2$, you get the wrong sign. With a battery, the work $V\,dQ$ has to be added (4.66), and then the answer comes out the same as (4.64).
  • The force depends only on the charges present at that moment, not on whether Q or V is held fixed.
{{FIG:s44-slab-force}}

9. Why the simple formula for χ is not quite right (background)

The naive guess is $\chi_e = N\alpha/\epsilon_0$, with $N$ atoms per volume (4.70). But $\alpha$ was defined for an atom in an outside field, while the field that acts on each atom is the total field, which includes the others. Averaging over a small sphere around each atom gives $\mathbf E = (1 - N\alpha/3\epsilon_0)\mathbf E_{\text{else}}$ (4.71). Solving gives the Clausius–Mossotti formula:

Result
$\chi_e = \dfrac{N\alpha/\epsilon_0}{1 - N\alpha/3\epsilon_0}$, equivalently $\alpha = \dfrac{3\epsilon_0}{N}\dfrac{\epsilon_r-1}{\epsilon_r+2} \quad(4.72)$.

For polar molecules, the Boltzmann factor $e^{-u/kT}$ with $u = -pE\cos\theta$ gives the Langevin formula (4.73), $P = Np[\coth(pE/kT) - kT/pE]$. For $pE \ll kT$ the medium is linear.
