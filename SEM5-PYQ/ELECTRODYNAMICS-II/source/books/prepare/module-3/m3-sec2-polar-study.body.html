9.1.4 Polarization of transverse waves

1. Transverse and longitudinal waves

On a string, shaking it up and down gives a transverse wave: the displacement is perpendicular to the direction of travel. A slinky can also carry a longitudinal (compression) wave, where the displacement is along the direction of travel. Sound in air is longitudinal. Electromagnetic waves are transverse, as Section 9.2 shows.

2. Two independent transverse directions

Perpendicular to the direction of travel there are two independent directions. A transverse wave can therefore be polarized in two independent ways. Shaking the string up and down (vertical) gives

$$\tilde{\mathbf f}^v=\tilde Ae^{i(kz-\omega t)}\hat x\tag{9.34}$$

and shaking it left and right (horizontal) gives

$$\tilde{\mathbf f}^h=\tilde Ae^{i(kz-\omega t)}\hat y\tag{9.35}$$

Any other direction in the $xy$ plane is a combination:

$$\tilde{\mathbf f}=\tilde Ae^{i(kz-\omega t)}\hat n\tag{9.36}$$

$\hat n$ is the polarization vector. It defines the plane in which the string vibrates (Fig. 9.8).

{{FIG:m3-sec2-polar-vec}}

For a transverse wave, $\hat n$ is perpendicular to the direction of travel:

$$\hat n\cdot\hat z=0\tag{9.37}$$

If $\theta$ is the angle that $\hat n$ makes with the $x$ axis in the $xy$ plane:

$$\hat n=\cos\theta\,\hat x+\sin\theta\,\hat y\tag{9.38}$$

Then the wave of Fig. 9.8c is the sum of a horizontal and a vertical wave with amplitudes $\tilde A\cos\theta$ and $\tilde A\sin\theta$:

$$\tilde{\mathbf f}=(\tilde A\cos\theta)e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\hat x+(\tilde A\sin\theta)e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\hat y\tag{9.39}$$

3. Notes on the sign and on circular polarization

Changing the sign of $\hat n$ is equivalent to advancing the phase constant by $180^\circ$, since both change the sign of the wave (footnote 4). The book uses the convention that $\hat n$ and the phase are chosen together.

If the two components in Eq. 9.39 have equal amplitude but are $90^\circ$ out of phase, the string moves in a circle. That is circular polarization. Problem 9.8 asks which way the circle turns, and how to produce it; the book’s optics terms are right circular and left circular.

The notation for elliptical or circular polarization is simplified in optics by using a complex $\hat n$. The book does not use it.
