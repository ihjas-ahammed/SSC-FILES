1. Monochromatic and plane

A monochromatic wave has one frequency $\omega$; its colour is set by $\omega$ in the visible range. A plane wave is uniform over every plane perpendicular to the direction of travel (Fig. 9.9). Take the direction of travel to be $z$, with no $x$ or $y$ dependence. Then the fields have the form

$$\tilde{\mathbf E}(z,t)=\tilde{\mathbf E}_0e^{i(kz-\omega t)},\qquad\tilde{\mathbf B}(z,t)=\tilde{\mathbf B}_0e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\tag{9.44}$$

with $\omega=ck$. The complex amplitudes $\tilde{\mathbf E}_0$, $\tilde{\mathbf B}_0$ carry direction and phase; the physical fields are the real parts.

2. Constraints from Maxwell’s equations

Plane waves of the form Eq. 9.44 satisfy the wave equation, but Maxwell’s equations impose more. Because $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and the fields do not depend on $x$ or $y$:

$$(\tilde E_0)_z=(\tilde B_0)_z=0\tag{9.45}$$

So $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$ are both perpendicular to the direction of travel. Electromagnetic waves are transverse. (Footnote 8: the real part and the imaginary part obey the same equations, so the complex solution is valid.)

Faraday’s law fixes the relation between the two amplitudes:

$$-k(\tilde E_0)_y=\omega(\tilde B_0)_x,\qquad k(\tilde E_0)_x=\omega(\tilde B_0)_y\tag{9.46}$$

which is the same as

$$\tilde{\mathbf B}_0=\frac{k}{\omega}(\hat z\times\tilde{\mathbf E}_0)\tag{9.47}$$

So $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$ are perpendicular to each other and to $\hat z$. They are in phase, and their real amplitudes are related by

$$B_0=\frac{k}{\omega}E_0=\frac{1}{c}E_0\tag{9.48}$$
Plus Two recall: transverse, in-phase fields

Recall. From Class 12: in an electromagnetic wave, $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$ oscillate in phase, at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel.

Re-derive.

  1. Assume $\tilde{\mathbf E}$ has only an $x$ component. Then $\mathbf B$ must be in the $y$ direction, by Eq. 9.47 with $\hat z\times\hat x=\hat y$.
  2. Faraday’s law: $\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial\mathbf B/\partial t$ gives $k E_0=\omega B_0$.
  3. Both fields have the same phase $kz-\omega t$, so they vanish and peak together.

Saved. $\mathbf E\perp\mathbf B\perp\hat z$, in phase, with $B_0=E_0/c$ Eq. 9.48.

3. Example 9.2

Take $\mathbf E$ along $x$. Then $\mathbf B$ is along $y$ by Eq. 9.47, and

$$\tilde{\mathbf E}=\tilde E_0e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\hat x,\qquad\tilde{\mathbf B}=\frac{1}{c}\tilde E_0e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\hat y$$

Taking the real parts gives the physical fields:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf E(z,t)&=E_0\cos(kz-\omega t+\delta)\hat x,\\ \mathbf B(z,t)&=\frac{1}{c}E_0\cos(kz-\omega t+\delta)\hat y\end{aligned}\tag{9.49}$$

This is the model for a monochromatic plane wave (Fig. 9.10). The wave is said to be polarized in the $x$ direction, by convention the direction of $\mathbf E$.

4. Arbitrary direction

For a wave travelling along a unit vector $\hat k$, define the propagation vector $\mathbf k=k\hat k$. The phase $kz$ is replaced by $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r$ (Fig. 9.11):

$$\tilde{\mathbf E}(\mathbf r,t)=\tilde E_0e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)}\hat n,\qquad\tilde{\mathbf B}(\mathbf r,t)=\frac{1}{c}\tilde E_0e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)}(\hat k\times\hat n)=\frac1c\hat k\times\tilde{\mathbf E}\tag{9.50}$$

$\hat n$ is the polarization vector, and transversality is $\hat n\cdot\hat k=0$ Eq. 9.51. The real fields are

$$\mathbf E(\mathbf r,t)=E_0\cos(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t+\delta)\hat n\tag{9.52}$$
$$\mathbf B(\mathbf r,t)=\frac1cE_0\cos(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t+\delta)(\hat k\times\hat n)\tag{9.53}$$
