9.2.3 Energy and momentum in electromagnetic waves

Before you start

1. Energy density of a plane wave

From Eq. 8.5, the energy per unit volume in the fields is

$$u=\frac12\left(\epsilon_0E^2+\frac{1}{\mu_0}B^2\right)\tag{9.54}$$

For a monochromatic plane wave (Eq. 9.49 or 9.53), $B=E/c$, so $B^2/\mu_0=\epsilon_0E^2$ (since $1/(\mu_0c^2)=\epsilon_0$). Then

$$B^2=\frac{1}{c^2}E^2=\mu_0\epsilon_0E^2\tag{9.55}$$

and the electric and magnetic parts of the energy are equal:

$$u=\epsilon_0E^2=\epsilon_0E_0^2\cos^2(kz-\omega t+\delta)\tag{9.56}$$
Plus Two recall: energy density of an electric field

Recall. From Class 12: a field $E$ in vacuum stores energy $\tfrac12\epsilon_0E^2$ per unit volume.

Re-derive.

  1. Use the magnetic result Eq. 8.5, $\tfrac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2$, for the second term.
  2. With $B=E/c$ Eq. 9.48 and $c^2=1/(\epsilon_0\mu_0)$, $\tfrac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2=\tfrac12\epsilon_0E^2$.
  3. So the magnetic term equals the electric term, and each is $\tfrac12\epsilon_0E^2$.

Saved. $u=\epsilon_0E^2$ Eq. 9.56, with the electric and magnetic parts equal.

2. Energy flux

The Poynting vector gives the energy flux density:

$$\mathbf S=\frac{1}{\mu_0}(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)\tag{9.57}$$

For a wave travelling along $z$, $\mathbf E\times\mathbf B$ points along $z$, and

$$\mathbf S=c\epsilon_0E_0^2\cos^2(kz-\omega t+\delta)\hat z=cu\,\hat z\tag{9.58}$$

So $\mathbf S$ equals the energy density times the velocity of the wave. Check: in time $\Delta t$ the energy in a cylinder of cross section $A$ and length $c\,\Delta t$ passes through area $A$, which is $uAc\Delta t$. Dividing by $A\Delta t$ gives $uc$, as Eq. 9.58 says.

3. Momentum density

Fields carry momentum as well as energy. Eq. 8.29 gives the momentum density:

$$\mathbf g=\frac{1}{c^2}\mathbf S\tag{9.59}$$

For a plane wave, $\mathbf g=(u/c)\hat z$ (Eq. 9.60). Footnote 9 warns against taking the real part of a complex energy density: $\mathrm{Re}(\tilde u)$ gives $\epsilon_0E_0^2\cos[2(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t+\delta)]$, which is not $u$. Use the real fields.

$$\mathbf g=\frac{1}{c}\epsilon_0E_0^2\cos^2(kz-\omega t+\delta)\hat z=\frac{1}{c}u\,\hat z\tag{9.60}$$

4. Time averages and intensity

Light has a wavelength near $5\times10^{-7}$ m and a period near $10^{-15}$ s. Any measurement covers many cycles, so only the average over a cycle matters. Since the average of $\cos^2$ over one period is $\tfrac12$ (footnote 10), the averages are

$$\langle u\rangle=\frac12\epsilon_0E_0^2\tag{9.61}$$
$$\langle\mathbf S\rangle=\frac12c\epsilon_0E_0^2\hat z\tag{9.62}$$
$$\langle\mathbf g\rangle=\frac{1}{2c}\epsilon_0E_0^2\hat z\tag{9.63}$$

The average power per unit area is the intensity:

$$I\equiv\langle S\rangle=\frac12c\epsilon_0E_0^2\tag{9.64}$$

5. Radiation pressure

Light at normal incidence on a perfect absorber delivers momentum to the surface. In time $\Delta t$ the momentum transferred to area $A$ is $\Delta p=\langle g\rangle Ac\Delta t$. The radiation pressure is the force per unit area:

$$P=\frac{1}{A}\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}=\frac12\epsilon_0E_0^2=\frac{I}{c}\tag{9.65}$$

A perfect reflector gives twice this value, because the momentum reverses direction. The naïve account in terms of the electric field driving charges, which then feel $q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$, is qualitative. Problem 9.11 shows that it is not complete: the velocity is $90^\circ$ out of phase with the fields, so there must be a damping force to absorb energy.

Problem 9.10 asks for the radiation pressure of sunlight of intensity about 1300 W/m$^2$ on an absorber and on a reflector. Problems 9.11–9.13 (magnetic force on a particle, complex averages, the Maxwell stress tensor) are listed in the book but are not worked here.
