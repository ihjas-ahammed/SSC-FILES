9.2.3 Energy and Momentum in Electromagnetic Waves

According to Eq. 8.5, the energy per unit volume in electromagnetic fields is

$$u=\frac12\left(\epsilon_0E^2+\frac{1}{\mu_0}B^2\right).\qquad(9.54)$$

In the case of a monochromatic plane wave (Eq. 9.49 or 9.53),9

$$B^2=\frac{1}{c^2}E^2=\mu_0\epsilon_0E^2,\qquad(9.55)$$

so the electric and magnetic contributions are equal:

$$u=\epsilon_0E^2=\epsilon_0E_0^2\cos^2(kz-\omega t+\delta).\qquad(9.56)$$

As the wave travels, it carries this energy along with it. The energy flux density (energy per unit area, per unit time) transported by the fields is given by the Poynting vector (Eq. 8.10):

$$\mathbf S=\frac{1}{\mu_0}(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B).\qquad(9.57)$$

For monochromatic plane waves propagating in the $z$ direction,

$$\mathbf S=c\epsilon_0E_0^2\cos^2(kz-\omega t+\delta)\hat z=cu\hat z.\qquad(9.58)$$

Notice that $\mathbf S$ is the energy density ($u$) times the velocity of the waves ($c\hat z$) – as it should be. For in a time $\Delta t$, a length $c\Delta t$ passes through area $A$ (Fig. 9.12), carrying with it an energy $uAc\Delta t$. The energy per unit time, per unit area, transported by the wave is therefore $uc$.

Electromagnetic fields not only carry energy, they also carry momentum. In fact, we found in Eq. 8.29 that the momentum density stored in the fields is

$$\mathbf g=\frac{1}{c^2}\mathbf S.\qquad(9.59)$$

For monochromatic plane waves, then,

$$\mathbf g=\frac{1}{c}\epsilon_0E_0^2\cos^2(kz-\omega t+\delta)\hat z=\frac{1}{c}u\hat z.\qquad(9.60)$$

In the case of light, the wavelength is so short ($\sim5\times10^{-7}$ m), and the period so brief ($\sim10^{-15}$ s), that any macroscopic measurement will encompass many cycles. Typically, therefore, we’re not interested in the rapidly fluctuating cosine-squared term in the energy and momentum densities; all we want is the average value. Now, the average of cosine-squared over a complete cycle10 is 1/2, so

$$\langle u\rangle=\frac12\epsilon_0E_0^2,\qquad(9.61)$$
$$\langle\mathbf S\rangle=\frac12c\epsilon_0E_0^2\hat z,\qquad(9.62)$$
$$\langle\mathbf g\rangle=\frac{1}{2c}\epsilon_0E_0^2\hat z.\qquad(9.63)$$

I use brackets, $\langle\ \rangle$, to denote the (time) average over a complete cycle (or many cycles, if you prefer). The average power per unit area transported by an electromagnetic wave is called the intensity:

$$I\equiv\langle S\rangle=\frac12c\epsilon_0E_0^2.\qquad(9.64)$$

When light falls (at normal incidence) on a perfect absorber, it delivers its momentum to the surface. In a time $\Delta t$, the momentum transfer is (Fig. 9.12) $\Delta p=\langle g\rangle Ac\Delta t$, so the radiation pressure (average force per unit area) is

$$P=\frac{1}{A}\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}=\frac12\epsilon_0E_0^2=\frac{I}{c}.\qquad(9.65)$$

(On a perfect reflector the pressure is twice as great, because the momentum switches direction, instead of simply being absorbed.) We can account for this pressure qualitatively, as follows: The electric field (Fig. 9.10) drives charges in the $x$ direction, and the magnetic field then exerts on them a force $q(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$ in the $z$ direction. The net force on all the charges in the surface produces the pressure.11

9.3.1 Propagation in Linear Media

Inside matter, but in regions where there is no free charge or free current, Maxwell’s equations become

$$\begin{aligned}\text{(i)}\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf D&=0, & \text{(iii)}\ \nabla\times\mathbf E&=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\\ \text{(ii)}\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf B&=0, & \text{(iv)}\ \nabla\times\mathbf H&=\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}\end{aligned}\qquad(9.66)$$
