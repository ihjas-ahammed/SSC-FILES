9.4.3 The Frequency Dependence of Permittivity

In the preceding sections, we have seen that the propagation of electromagnetic waves through matter is governed by three properties of the material: the permittivity $\epsilon$, the permeability $\mu$, and the conductivity $\sigma$. Actually, each of these parameters depends to some extent on the frequency of the waves you are considering. Indeed, it is well known from optics that $n\approx\sqrt{\epsilon_r}$ is a function of wavelength (Fig. 9.19 shows the graph for a typical glass). A prism or a raindrop bends blue light more sharply than red, and spreads white light out into a rainbow of colors. This phenomenon is called dispersion. By extension, whenever the speed of a wave depends on its frequency, the supporting medium is said to be dispersive.

The electrons in a nonconductor are bound to specific molecules. The actual binding forces can be quite complicated, but we shall picture each electron as attached to the end of a spring, with force constant $k_{\rm spring}$ (Fig. 9.21):

$$F_{\rm binding}=-k_{\rm spring}x=-m\omega_0^2x,\qquad(9.153)$$

where $x$ is the displacement from equilibrium, $m$ is the electron’s mass, and $\omega_0$ is the natural oscillation frequency, $\sqrt{k_{\rm spring}/m}$. If this strikes you as an implausible model, look back at Ex. 4.1, where we were led to a force of precisely this form. As a matter of fact, practically any binding force can be approximated this way for sufficiently small displacements from equilibrium, as you can see by expanding the potential energy in a Taylor series about the equilibrium point:

$$U(x)=U(0)+xU'(0)+\frac12x^2U''(0)+\cdots.$$

The first term is a constant, with no dynamical significance (you can always adjust the zero of potential energy so that $U(0)=0$). The second term automatically vanishes, since $dU/dx=-F$, and by the nature of an equilibrium, the force at that point is zero. The third term is precisely the potential energy of a spring with force constant $k_{\rm spring}=d^2U/dx^2|_0$ (the second derivative is positive, for a point of stable equilibrium). As long as the displacements are small, the higher-order terms in the series can be neglected. Geometrically, all I am saying is that practically any function can be fit near a minimum by a suitable parabola.

9.5 Guided Waves

9.5.1 Wave Guides

So far, we have dealt with plane waves of infinite extent; now we consider electromagnetic waves confined to the interior of a hollow pipe, or wave guide (Fig. 9.23). We’ll assume the wave guide is a perfect conductor, so that $\mathbf E=0$ and $\mathbf B=0$ inside the material itself, and hence the boundary conditions at the inner wall are26

$$\text{(i)}\ \mathbf E^\parallel=\mathbf 0,\qquad\text{(ii)}\ B^\perp=0.\qquad(9.177)$$
