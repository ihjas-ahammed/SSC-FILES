Section 1: growth and decay of current in an L-R circuit (Murugeshan 12.1, 12.2)

12.1 Growth of current in an L-R circuit

Source: book-sources/m4-s12-1-growth.html, Murugeshan pp. 312–313 (printed). Syllabus item 15. Equations (1)–(4) are given in full.

Prerequisites

  • Ohm’s law and the emf of a source (Plus Two; pre-physics P1).
  • First-order differential equations solved by separation of variables (Beyond; pre-math M4).
  • Exponentials and natural logarithms (pre-math M3).

1. Setup

An inductance $L$ and a resistance $R$ are connected in series with a steady emf $E$ through a key (Fig. 12.1). On closing the key the current rises from zero to a final value $I_0$. $I$ denotes the current at time $t$.

2. Loop equation

The induced back emf is $\varepsilon=-L\,dI/dt$. The loop equation is

$$E = RI + L\frac{dI}{dt}\tag{12.1(1)}$$

3. Final current

At the maximum, $dI/dt=0$, so

$$E = RI_0\tag{12.1(2)}$$
Recall (Plus Two) Ohm’s law gives $I_0=E/R$ for a steady current, which is the value the inductor allows once its back emf vanishes.

4. Variables separated

Substituting Eq. (2) into Eq. (1) gives $R(I_0-I)=L\,dI/dt$, that is

$$\frac{dI}{I_0 - I} = \frac{R}{L}\,dt$$

5. Integration and boundary condition

Integration gives

$$-\log(I_0 - I) = \frac{R}{L}t + C\tag{12.1(3)}$$

The boundary condition $I=0$ at $t=0$ fixes $C=-\log I_0$, so $\log_e\left[(I_0-I)/I_0\right]=-(R/L)t$.

6. Solution

$$I = I_0\left(1 - e^{-(R/L)t}\right)\tag{12.1(4)}$$

7. Time constant

The time constant is $\tau = L/R$. At $t=\tau$, $I=(1-e^{-1})I_0 = 0.632\,I_0$. The current reaches $0.865\,I_0$ at $2\tau$ and $0.950\,I_0$ at $3\tau$ (Fig. 12.2).

8. Worked example (printed p. 314)

For $R=10\ \Omega$, $L=0.5$ H and $E=10$ V, $R/L=20\ \text{s}^{-1}$. The time to reach 63.2% of $I_0$ is $t=\frac{\ln 2.717}{20}=0.05$ s, which equals $\tau=L/R$.

12.2 Decay of current in an L-R circuit

Source: book-sources/m4-s12-2-decay.html, Murugeshan pp. 313–314 (printed), with Examples 1 and 2. Syllabus item 16. Equations (1) and (2) are given in full. Example 1 is worked in §12.1.

Prerequisites

  • §12.1 of these notes: the loop equation and the time constant.
  • Exponentials and natural logarithms (pre-math M3).

1. Setup

The cell is disconnected and the two ends of the circuit are joined. The emf is zero, and the inductor drives the current through $R$ by its back emf.

2. Loop equation

$$0 = RI + L\frac{dI}{dt}\tag{12.2(1)}$$

Separating variables, $dI/I = -(R/L)\,dt$.

3. Solution

Integration gives $\log_e I = -(R/L)t + C$. The initial condition $I=I_0$ at $t=0$ gives $C=\log_e I_0$, and so

$$I = I_0 e^{-(R/L)t}\tag{12.2(2)}$$

4. Interpretation

The decay time constant is $L/R$, the time in which the current falls to $1/e$ of $I_0$. The rate of decay is $dI/dt=-(R/L)I$. Growth and decay are complementary (Fig. 12.5).

5. Worked example (printed p. 315)

$I_0=E/R=2$ A and $R/L=10\ \text{s}^{-1}$, so $I=2e^{-10t}$. At $t=0.05$ s, $I=1.213$ A. At $t=0.2$ s, $I=0.271$ A.

Section 2: charge in C-R and L-C circuits; leakage (Murugeshan 12.3 to 12.5)

12.3 Growth and decay of charge in a C-R circuit

Source: book-sources/m4-s2-cr-lc.html, Murugeshan pp. 315–317 (printed). Syllabus items 15 and 16. Equations (a)(1)–(a)(3) and (b)(1)–(b)(3) are given in full.

Prerequisites

  • $Q=CV$ and the p.d. across a capacitor (pre-physics P3).
  • First-order equations solved by separation of variables (pre-math M4).

1. Charging

Recall (Plus Two) $Q=CV$, so the p.d. across the capacitor is $Q/C$. The emf is shared between the capacitor and the resistor in series.

A capacitor $C$ and a resistance $R$ are connected to an emf $E$ through a key (Fig. 12.6). $Q$ denotes the charge on $C$ at time $t$, and $I=dQ/dt$.

$$E = \frac{Q}{C} + RI\tag{12.3(a)(1)}$$

2. Final charge

At the maximum charge $Q_0$, $dQ/dt=0$ and $E=Q_0/C$.

3. Variables separated

$$\begin{aligned}(Q_0 - Q) &= CR\frac{dQ}{dt}\\ \frac{dQ}{Q_0 - Q} &= \frac{dt}{CR}\end{aligned}\tag{12.3(a)(2)}$$

4. Solution

With $Q=0$ at $t=0$,

$$Q = Q_0\left(1 - e^{-t/CR}\right)\tag{12.3(a)(3)}$$

The time constant is $CR$. At $t=CR$ the charge is $0.632\,Q_0$.

5. Discharging

With $E=0$ and the capacitor charged to $Q_0$,

$$R\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{Q}{C} = 0\tag{12.3(b)(1)}$$
$$Q = Q_0 e^{-t/CR}\tag{12.3(b)(2)}$$
$$\begin{aligned}I &= \frac{dQ}{dt} = -\frac{Q_0}{CR}e^{-t/CR}\\ &= -\frac{Q}{CR}\end{aligned}\tag{12.3(b)(3)}$$

At $t=CR$, $Q = 0.368\,Q_0$; the time constant is also the time to fall to $1/e$.

6. Worked examples (printed pp. 316–317)

(1) With $R=2\ \text{M}\Omega$ and $t=0.5$ s for $Q/Q_0=3/4$, Eq. (a)(3) gives $C=0.18\ \mu$F. (2) For $C=0.1\ \mu$F and $R=10\ \text{M}\Omega$, the p.d. halves in $CR\ln 2=0.6931$ s.

12.4 Measurement of high resistance by leakage (background)

Background. Outside the Module 4 items (15–22). Kept because it applies the discharge law of §12.3. The book numbers no equations in this section. Source: book-sources/m4-s2-cr-lc.html, Murugeshan pp. 318–319 (printed).

1. Principle

A capacitor of capacitance $C$ and initial charge $Q_0$ discharges through a resistance $R$ for a time $t$. From §12.3 the remaining charge is $Q = Q_0e^{-t/CR}$, so

$$R = \frac{t}{C\log_e(Q_0/Q)} = \frac{t}{2.3026\,C\log_{10}(Q_0/Q)}$$

A high $R$ gives a slow discharge. Measuring $Q_0/Q$ then determines $R$.

2. Circuit and procedure

$C$ is a capacitor of known value, $R$ the high resistance, B.G. a ballistic galvanometer, $E$ a cell, and $K_1$, $K_2$, $K_3$ tap keys (Fig. 12.8).

(i) The capacitor is charged through $K_1$ and then discharged through the galvanometer, giving a throw $\theta_0\propto Q_0$. (ii) The capacitor is charged again, allowed to leak through $R$ for a time $t$, and then discharged through the galvanometer, giving a throw $\theta\propto Q$. Since $Q_0/Q = \theta_0/\theta$,

$$R = \frac{t}{2.3026\,C\log_{10}(\theta_0/\theta)}$$

3. Graph

$\log_{10}(\theta_0/\theta)$ plotted against $t$ is a straight line through the origin (Fig. 12.9). Its slope gives $t/\log_{10}(\theta_0/\theta)$, which with $C$ known gives $R$.

4. Worked examples (printed pp. 318–319)

(1) $C=2\ \mu$F, $R=100\ \text{M}\Omega$, $Q_0/Q=2$: $t=2.3026\,CR\log_{10}2=138.7$ s. (2) $C=0.2\ \mu$F, throws of 12 cm and 6 cm, $t=2$ s: $R=14.43\ \text{M}\Omega$.

12.5 Charging and discharging of a capacitor through an inductance

Source: book-sources/m4-s2-cr-lc.html, Murugeshan pp. 319–321 (printed). Syllabus items 15 and 16. Charging equations are tagged (i)(1)–(4) and discharge equations (ii)(1)–(4), following the book’s separate numbering. Section 12.6 onwards is not included.

Prerequisites

  • Charge and p.d. of a capacitor; self-inductance and back emf (pre-physics P2, P3).
  • Simple harmonic motion (pre-math H2).

1. Charging

Let $C$ and $L$ be joined to an emf $E$ through a key (Fig. 12.10), and $Q$ the charge on $C$ at time $t$.

$$L\frac{dI}{dt} + \frac{Q}{C} = E\tag{12.5(i)(1)}$$

With $I=dQ/dt$, and $Q_0=CE$, this becomes

$$\begin{aligned}L\frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + \frac{Q}{C} &= E\\ \frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + \frac{1}{LC}(Q - Q_0) &= 0\end{aligned}\tag{12.5(i)(2)}$$

which is simple harmonic motion with period

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{LC}\tag{12.5(i)(3)}$$
$$\nu = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}\tag{12.5(i)(4)}$$

2. Discharge

With no external emf (Fig. 12.11),

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{Q}{C} + L\frac{dI}{dt} &= 0\\ \frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + \frac{Q}{LC} &= 0\end{aligned}\tag{12.5(ii)(1)}$$

whose general solution, with $\omega=1/\sqrt{LC}$, is

$$Q = A\sin(\omega t+\theta)\tag{12.5(ii)(2)}$$

With $Q=Q_0$ at $t=0$ and $\theta=\pi/2$,

$$Q = Q_0\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{LC}} + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\tag{12.5(ii)(3)}$$
$$\nu = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}\tag{12.5(ii)(4)}$$

3. Energy

The energy alternates between the electric field of $C$ and the magnetic field of $L$.

Section 3: emf of a rotating coil; mean, r.m.s. and virtual values (Murugeshan 13.1)

13.1 EMF induced in a rotating coil

Source: book-sources/m4-s3-ac-emf.html, Murugeshan pp. 329–333 (printed). Syllabus item 17. The book does not number the equations of this section; they are shown unnumbered.

Prerequisites

  • Magnetic flux and Faraday’s law (pre-physics P4).
  • Sinusoidal functions and their integrals (pre-math M5, M7).

1. Set-up

A rectangular coil of $N$ turns, sides $a$ and $b$, rotates at angular velocity $\omega$ about an axis perpendicular to a uniform field $\mathbf B$ (Fig. 13.1). The normal makes angle $\theta=\omega t$ with $\mathbf B$ at time $t$, taking $\theta=0$ at the instant the coil plane is perpendicular to $\mathbf B$.

$$\phi = NBA\cos\omega t,\qquad A=ab$$

2. Induced emf

Recall (Plus Two) Flux $\phi=BA\cos\theta$ and emf $-d\phi/dt$ (Faraday). With $N$ turns and $\theta=\omega t$ the emf is sinusoidal.
$$E = -\frac{d\phi}{dt} = E_0\sin\omega t,\qquad E_0 = NBA\omega$$

$E_0$ is the peak value. The period is $T=1/\nu$ and $\omega=2\pi\nu$ (Fig. 13.3).

3. Mean, r.m.s. and virtual values

$$I_{mean} = \frac{2I_0}{\pi} = 0.637\,I_0,\qquad E_{mean} = 0.637\,E_0$$
$$\begin{gathered}\overline{I^2} = \frac{I_0^2}{2} \\ I_{rms} = \frac{I_0}{\sqrt2} = 0.707\,I_0 \\ E_{rms} = \frac{E_0}{\sqrt 2}\end{gathered}$$

The r.m.s. value equals the virtual (effective) value: the direct current giving the same heat in the same resistance.

4. Form factor and half-cycle mean

$$\begin{aligned}\text{Form factor} &= \frac{I_{rms}}{I_{mean}} \\ &= \frac{0.707}{0.637} \\ &= 1.11\end{aligned}$$

Over half a period, $E_{av} = 2E_0/\pi$.

5. Impedance and reactance

The impedance is $Z = E/I$; reactance is the part due to $L$ or $C$ ($X_L$, $X_C$); admittance is $Y=1/Z$.

Section 4: AC circuits with R, L and C; phasors and the j operator (Murugeshan 13.2)

13.2 Alternating current circuit theory

Source: book-sources/m4-s4-ac-circuits.html, Murugeshan pp. 334–337 (printed). Syllabus items 18, 19 and 20. The parallel circuit, §13.2(vi), is in Section 5 with §13.4. Equations are tagged with the book’s numbers.

Prerequisites

  • Phase of a sinusoid (pre-physics H2).
  • Complex numbers and the phasor (pre-math B1, B2).

1. Pure resistance (i)

Recall (Plus Two) Ohm’s law $V=IR$ (Plus Two) gives $I=E/R$ in complex form. The current is in phase with the emf.

For $E=E_0e^{j\omega t}$ across $R$ (Fig. 13.4), $I = E/R = I_0e^{j\omega t}$ with $I_0=E_0/R$. The current is in phase with the voltage (Fig. 13.5).

2. Pure inductance (ii)

$$I = \frac{E}{j\omega L} = \frac{E_0}{\omega L}e^{j(\omega t-\pi/2)}\tag{13.2(ii)(1)}$$
$$I = I_0\sin\left(\omega t - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\tag{13.2(ii)(2)}$$

The current lags the emf by $\pi/2$. The impedance is $j\omega L$.

3. Pure capacitance (iii)

From $q/C=E$, $I=dq/dt=j\omega CE$, so $I = I_0 e^{j(\omega t+\pi/2)}$ with $I_0 = E_0/(1/\omega C)$. The current leads the emf by $\pi/2$. The impedance is $1/(j\omega C)$.

4. Inductance and resistance in series (iv)

$$I = \frac{E}{R + j\omega L}\tag{13.2(iv)(1)}$$
$$I = \frac{E}{Z}\tag{13.2(iv)(2)}$$
$$Z = R + j\omega L\tag{13.2(iv)(3)}$$

With $\tan\theta=\omega L/R$, $Z = \sqrt{R^2+\omega^2L^2}\,e^{j\theta}$, and

$$I = \frac{E_0}{\sqrt{R^2+\omega^2L^2}}\,e^{j(\omega t-\theta)}\tag{13.2(iv)(4)}$$
$$I = I_0e^{j(\omega t-\theta)}\tag{13.2(iv)(5)}$$
$$I_0 = \frac{E_0}{\sqrt{R^2+\omega^2L^2}}\tag{13.2(iv)(6)}$$

The current lags the emf by $\theta$.

5. Capacitance and resistance in series (v)

$$I\left(R+\frac{1}{j\omega C}\right) = E = E_0e^{j\omega t}\tag{13.2(v)(1)}$$
$$I = \frac{E}{R + 1/(j\omega C)}\tag{13.2(v)(2)}$$
$$I = \frac{E}{Z}\tag{13.2(v)(3)}$$
$$\begin{aligned}I &= \frac{E}{R - j/(\omega C)} \\ &= \frac{E_0e^{j\omega t}}{\sqrt{R^2 + 1/(\omega C)^2}}\,e^{-j\theta}\end{aligned} \tag{13.2(v)(4)}$$
$$\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1/\omega C}{R}\right)\tag{13.2(v)(5)}$$
$$I = \frac{E_0}{\sqrt{R^2+(1/\omega C)^2}}\,e^{j(\omega t+\theta)}\tag{13.2(v)(6)}$$

The current leads the voltage by $\theta$.

6. The j operator

$j$ rotates a phasor $90^\circ$ anticlockwise and $-j$ rotates it $90^\circ$ clockwise. The total emf is $E = E_R + jE_L - jE_C$. The complex impedance $Z=R+jX$ has resistance $R$ and effective reactance $X=X_L-X_C$.

Section 5: series LCR and parallel resonance (Murugeshan 13.2(vi), 13.3, 13.4)

13.2 (vi), 13.3 and 13.4 Series and parallel resonance

Source: book-sources/m4-s5-series-parallel.html, Murugeshan pp. 338–347 (printed). Syllabus items 20 and 21. The series derivation is given by two methods: (a) first method, differential equation; (b) second method, complex impedance. Equations are tagged with the book’s numbers.

Prerequisites

  • Impedance and phase of R, L and C (Section 4).
  • Complex numbers and the modulus (pre-math B1, B3).

1. Parallel LC (§13.2(vi))

$$\begin{aligned}I &= I_L + I_C \\ &= E_0\left(\omega C - \frac{1}{\omega L}\right)\cos\omega t\end{aligned} \tag{13.2(vi)(1)}$$

The current vanishes when $\omega C = 1/\omega L$, giving

$$\nu_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}\tag{13.2(vi)(2)}$$

2. Series LCR, first method (§13.3)

Recall (Plus Two) Series elements add their opposition. Here the reactances subtract, so $Z=\sqrt{R^2+(X_L-X_C)^2}$.
$$L\frac{d^2I}{dt^2} + R\frac{dI}{dt} + \frac{1}{C}\frac{dQ}{dt} = E_0\omega\cos\omega t\tag{13.3(a)(1)}$$

With $I = I_0\sin(\omega t-\phi)$, equating coefficients gives

$$I = I_0\sin(\omega t-\phi)\tag{13.3(a)(2)}$$
$$\left(-L\omega^2 + \frac{1}{C}\right)I_0 = -E_0\omega\sin\phi\tag{13.3(a)(3)}$$
$$R\omega I_0 = E_0\omega\cos\phi\tag{13.3(a)(4)}$$
$$\tan\phi = \frac{\omega L - 1/\omega C}{R}\tag{13.3(a)(5)}$$
$$I_0 = \frac{E_0}{\sqrt{R^2 + (\omega L - 1/\omega C)^2}}\tag{13.3(a)(6)}$$
$$I = \frac{E_0}{\sqrt{R^2 + (\omega L - 1/\omega C)^2}}\sin(\omega t - \phi)\tag{13.3(a)(7)}$$

The current lags if $X_L>X_C$, leads if $X_L\lt X_C$, and is in phase if $X_L=X_C$.

3. Series resonance

The impedance is least, $Z=R$, when $\omega L = 1/\omega C$; the current is then $E_0/R$. The resonant frequency is $\nu_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$.

$$\begin{aligned}I &= \frac{E_0}{\sqrt{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{C\omega}\right)^2}}\\ &\quad\times\sin(\omega t-\phi)\end{aligned}\tag{13.3(res)(1)}$$

4. Q-factor, voltage magnification and bandwidth

At resonance the voltage across $L$ is $QE_0$, where $Q=\omega_0L/R$. A larger $Q$ gives a sharper peak.

5. Series LCR, second method (§13.3)

$$E = E_0 e^{j\omega t}\tag{13.3(b)(1)}$$
$$\begin{gathered}Z = \sqrt{R^2+(\omega L-1/\omega C)^2}\,e^{j\phi} \\ \tan\phi = \frac{\omega L-1/\omega C}{R}\end{gathered} \tag{13.3(b)(2)}$$
$$I = \frac{E_0}{\sqrt{R^2+(\omega L-1/\omega C)^2}}\,e^{j(\omega t-\phi)}\tag{13.3(b)(3)}$$
$$I = I_0 e^{j(\omega t-\phi)}\tag{13.3(b)(4)}$$
$$\begin{aligned}i &= \operatorname{Im}(I)\\ &= \frac{E_0}{\sqrt{R^2+(\omega L-1/\omega C)^2}}\sin(\omega t-\phi)\end{aligned}\tag{13.3(b)(5)}$$

The actual current is the imaginary part of the complex current.

6. Parallel resonance: the rejector (§13.4)

$C$ is in parallel with $R$ and $L$ in series. The admittance is least, and the impedance greatest, at $\omega_0=\sqrt{1/LC - R^2/L^2}$, which is $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ when $R$ is small. The impedance at resonance is $Z=L/(RC)$.

7. Worked examples

§13.3 Example 1 (printed p. 344): $Z=53.97\ \Omega$, $I_{rms}=3.71$ A, $\cos\phi=0.964$, true power 716 W. §13.3 Example 2 (printed p. 344): resonance at $C=2\ \mu$F, $I=1.0$ A, p.d. 100 V across $R$ and 1570 V across $L$ and $C$. §13.4 Example 1 (printed p. 347): $\nu_0=112\,590$ Hz; peak make-up current $15\times10^{-6}$ A.

Section 6: power in AC circuits (Murugeshan 13.5)

13.5 Power in an AC circuit

Source: book-sources/m4-s6-power.html, Murugeshan pp. 347–351 (printed), with Examples 1–5. Syllabus item 22. §13.6 (choke coil) is outside this module; its heading only is shown. Equations (1) and (2) and the Example 2 equations are tagged with the book’s numbers.

Prerequisites

  • Power in a DC circuit (pre-physics P8).
  • Product-to-sum identities and the average of $\cos^2$ over a cycle (pre-math M5, M7).

1. Instantaneous power

Recall (Plus Two) $P=VI=I^2R$ for DC (Plus Two). For AC the average power carries the factor $\cos\phi$.
$$E\times I = \frac{1}{2}E_0I_0\left[\cos\phi - \cos(2\omega t - \phi)\right]\tag{13.5(1)}$$

2. Average power

$$P = \frac{1}{2}E_0I_0\cos\phi = E_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\phi\tag{13.5(2)}$$

The apparent power is $E_{rms}I_{rms}$. The power factor is $\cos\phi$, which equals the true power divided by the apparent power.

3. Special cases

Purely resistive: $\cos\phi=1$. Purely inductive or purely capacitive: $\cos\phi=0$ and the true power is zero.

4. Wattless current

The current is wattless when no power is consumed. A pure inductor or capacitor, with no resistance, draws a wattless current; this is the principle of the choke coil. The component $I\cos\phi$ gives the power and $I\sin\phi$ does not.

5. Worked examples

Example 1 (printed p. 348): $\cos\phi=0.062$; power 0.00189 W. Example 2 (printed pp. 348–349): $R=16.7\ \Omega$, $L=37.3$ mH, from the equations tagged 13.5(ex2)(1)–(5). Example 3 (printed pp. 350–351): power component 0.20 A, wattless component 0.87 A. Example 4 (printed p. 351): wattmeter reading 514.5 W. Example 5 (printed p. 351): no power is dissipated in a pure inductor.

Example 2 equations

$$\frac{50^2}{[R^2+(100\pi)^2L^2]}R = 100\ \text{W}\tag{13.5(ex2)(1)}$$
$$\frac{50^2R}{[R^2+(200\pi)^2L^2]} = 50\ \text{W}\tag{13.5(ex2)(2)}$$
$$2500\,R = 100\left[R^2+10^4\pi^2L^2\right]\tag{13.5(ex2)(3)}$$
$$2500\,R = 50\left[R^2+4\times10^4\pi^2L^2\right]\tag{13.5(ex2)(4)}$$
$$\begin{aligned}R^2 &= 2\times10^4\times\pi^2L^2\\ R &= 100\sqrt2\,\pi L\end{aligned}\tag{13.5(ex2)(5)}$$

§13.6 (choke coil) begins on printed p. 351 and is not part of this module.
