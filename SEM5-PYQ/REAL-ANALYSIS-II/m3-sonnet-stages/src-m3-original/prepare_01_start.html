One idea for the whole module

You are handed ε > 0; you must hand back a mesh bound δ_ε > 0

Cut the interval [a,b] into pieces [x_{i-1},x_i] and pick a sample point (tag) t_i in each piece. Build a rectangle of width x_i - x_{i-1} and signed height f(t_i). The sum of these rectangle areas is the Riemann sum S(f;\dot P) = \sum_{i=1}^n f(t_i)(x_i - x_{i-1}).

A function f is Riemann integrable with integral L if, whatever \varepsilon > 0 an opponent hands you, you can hand back a cut-size limit \delta_\varepsilon > 0 so that every partition whose widest piece has length \lVert P\rVert < \delta_\varepsilon—and every legal choice of tags inside those pieces—produces a sum S(f;\dot P) within \varepsilon of L.

The chain that carries the module

Proving integrability straight from Definition 7.1.1 needs you to guess L first. To avoid guessing L, Bartle builds one chain:

Definition 7.1.1 (T03) \Rightarrow Cauchy Criterion (T10) \Rightarrow Squeeze Theorem (T12) + Step functions \in \mathcal{R} (T14) \Rightarrow Continuous \in \mathcal{R} (T15) & Monotone \in \mathcal{R} (T16).

Then Mean Value Theorem (P12) connects derivatives to Riemann sums to give FTC First Form (T20), while Additivity (T17) + continuity at c gives FTC Second Form (T23), unlocking Substitution (T25), Integration by Parts (T31) and Taylor's Remainder (T32).

The whole module in one story

The story in five sentences

1. A Riemann sum S(f;\dot P) = \sum_{i=1}^n f(t_i)(x_i - x_{i-1}) approximates signed area using rectangles on a partition P with sample tags t_i, and f \in \mathcal{R}[a,b] means all sums with mesh \lVert P\rVert < \delta_\varepsilon cluster within \varepsilon of one number L = \int_a^b f (T01–T04).

2. Every integrable function must be bounded (T08), may be altered at finitely many points without changing its integral (T06), and obeys linearity and order (T07), though direct \varepsilon–\delta proofs work cleanly only for simple functions like constants, step functions, x, and Thomae's function (T05, T09).

3. The Cauchy Criterion (T10) and Squeeze Theorem (T12) eliminate the need to know L in advance: by trapping f between step functions (T13–T14), we prove that every continuous function (T15) and every monotone function (T16) on [a,b] is integrable, while Dirichlet's function is not (T11), and integrals split additively across subintervals (T17–T19).

4. The two forms of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus connect \mathcal{R}[a,b] to derivatives: FTC 1 evaluates \int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a) via the Mean Value Theorem when F'=f off a finite set and f\in\mathcal{R}[a,b] (T20–T21), while FTC 2 differentiates the Lipschitz indefinite integral F(z) = \int_a^z f at any point where f is continuous (T22–T24), yielding the Substitution Theorem (T25).

5. Lebesgue's Criterion crowns the theory—a bounded function on [a,b] is Riemann integrable if and only if its discontinuities form a null set (T26–T27)—which immediately proves the Composition, Absolute Value, and Product Theorems (T28–T30), and hence Integration by Parts (T31) and Taylor's Theorem with Integral Remainder (T32).

PREREQUISITES · P01–P14

What you bring from earlier courses

P01

Plus Two integrals: area vs antiderivative

Two ideas you treated as the same in school; this module proves why they agree.

What you already know

In Plus Two you used \int_a^b f(x)\,dx in two ways: (1) the signed area between y=f(x) and the x-axis on [a,b], and (2) F(b)-F(a) where F'(x)=f(x) (such as \int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} and \int \sin x\,dx = -\cos x).

In Real Analysis, Definition 7.1.1 defines \int_a^b f purely from rectangle sums (view 1), and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Theorem 7.3.1) is the bridge proving that F(b)-F(a) gives the exact same number.

P02

Sigma (\Sigma) notation and linearity of finite sums

Every Riemann sum is a finite sum, so standard algebra applies without limits.

Rules for \sum_{i=1}^n

For any real numbers a_i, b_i and constant c \in \mathbb{R}:

\sum_{i=1}^n c\,a_i = c\sum_{i=1}^n a_i, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^n (a_i + b_i) = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i + \sum_{i=1}^n b_i.

If a_i \le b_i for each i=1,\dots,n, then \sum_{i=1}^n a_i \le \sum_{i=1}^n b_i. And summing a constant 1 over n terms gives \sum_{i=1}^n 1 = n.

P03

Standard finite and geometric sums

Three closed-form sums used in squeeze and null-set proofs.

Formulas to know cold

Integers: \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}, so \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} k = \frac{(n-1)n}{2}.

Squares: \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n k^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}.

Halving geometric series: \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{2^k} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{8} + \cdots = \frac{1/2}{1 - 1/2} = 1, so \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\varepsilon}{2^k} = \varepsilon.

P04

Supremum, infimum and completeness of ℝ

Every bounded non-empty set of reals has a least upper bound \sup and greatest lower bound \inf.

Real Analysis I refresher

If A \subseteq \mathbb{R} is non-empty and bounded above, s = \sup A means: (i) a \le s for all a \in A, and (ii) whatever \varepsilon > 0 you are handed, there is some a \in A with a > s - \varepsilon.

P05

Triangle inequality and the "arbitrary ε" rule

The workhorse inequalities of every \varepsilon–\delta proof.

Three rules used in almost every proof

1. Unpacking absolute value: \lvert a\rvert < \varepsilon \iff -\varepsilon < a < \varepsilon, and \lvert S - L\rvert < \varepsilon \iff L - \varepsilon < S < L + \varepsilon.

2. Triangle and reverse triangle inequalities: \lvert a + b\rvert \le \lvert a\rvert + \lvert b\rvert and \lvert a + b\rvert \ge \big\lvert\lvert a\rvert - \lvert b\rvert\big\rvert \ge \lvert a\rvert \lvert b\rvert.

3. Arbitrary \varepsilon rule: If x \le y + \varepsilon for every \varepsilon > 0, then x \le y. In particular, if \lvert A - B\rvert < \varepsilon for every \varepsilon > 0, then A = B. (Why? If \lvert A - B\rvert > 0, taking \varepsilon = \tfrac12\lvert A B\rvert > 0 gives a contradiction.)

P06

Cauchy sequences in ℝ converge (Bartle 3.5.5)

How we prove a limit A exists without knowing A beforehand.

Theorem 3.5.5 · Cauchy Convergence Criterion

A sequence (a_n) of real numbers converges to some real number A \in \mathbb{R} if and only if for every \varepsilon > 0 there is K \in \mathbb{N} such that \lvert a_m - a_n\rvert < \varepsilon for all m, n \ge K.

In Theorem 7.2.1 we build a sequence of Riemann sums a_m = S(f;\dot P_m) satisfying \lvert a_m a_n\rvert < 1/n for all m > n; that immediately forces (a_m) to be Cauchy and therefore convergent to a real number A.

P07

Continuous on [a,b]: Boundedness, Max–Min, IVT

Module 1 theorems on a closed bounded interval [a,b].

Theorems 5.3.2, 5.3.4, 5.3.7

If f : [a,b] \to \mathbb{R} is continuous on the closed bounded interval [a,b], then:

  • Boundedness (5.3.2): there is M > 0 with \lvert f(x)\rvert \le M for all x \in [a,b].
  • Maximum–Minimum (5.3.4): f attains its minimum and maximum on [a,b] (and on every closed subinterval I_i = [x_{i-1},x_i]): there exist u_i, v_i \in I_i with f(u_i) \le f(x) \le f(v_i) for all x \in I_i.
  • Bolzano's IVT (5.3.7): f takes every value between \min_{[a,b]} f and \max_{[a,b]} f.
P08

Uniform Continuity Theorem (Bartle 5.4.3)

One \delta works across the whole closed bounded interval [a,b].

Theorem 5.4.3 · Uniform Continuity Theorem

If f : [a,b] \to \mathbb{R} is continuous on the closed bounded interval [a,b], then f is uniformly continuous on [a,b]: whatever \varepsilon > 0 you are handed, there is a single \delta > 0 such that for all u, v \in [a,b] with \lvert u v\rvert < \delta, we have \lvert f(u) - f(v)\rvert < \varepsilon.

In Theorem 7.2.7 we apply this with \varepsilon/(b-a) in place of \varepsilon, so that on every subinterval of length <\delta the gap \max f - \min f is Simultaneously less than \varepsilon/(b-a).

P09

Step functions and Lipschitz functions (Bartle 5.4.9, 5.4.4)

Two special classes of functions from Module 1.

Definitions 5.4.9 and 5.4.4

Step function (5.4.9): \varphi : [a,b] \to \mathbb{R} is a step function if [a,b] is the union of finitely many non-overlapping intervals J_1, \dots, J_m (open, closed, or half-open) on each of which \varphi is constant.

Lipschitz function (5.4.4): F : [a,b] \to \mathbb{R} is Lipschitz if there is M > 0 such that \lvert F(z) - F(w)\rvert \le M\lvert z - w\rvert for all z, w \in [a,b]. Every Lipschitz function is uniformly continuous (and hence continuous): take \delta = \varepsilon/M.

P10

Monotone functions and their jumps (Bartle 5.6.4)

Increasing or decreasing functions can jump, but only countably many times.

Monotone functions

f : [a,b] \to \mathbb{R} is increasing if x_1 \le x_2 \Rightarrow f(x_1) \le f(x_2), and decreasing if x_1 \le x_2 \Rightarrow f(x_1) \ge f(x_2). On any subinterval [x_{k-1},x_k], an increasing function automatically has its minimum at the left endpoint x_{k-1} and its maximum at the right endpoint x_k. By Theorem 5.6.4 (cited in 7.3.13(b)), the set of discontinuities of a monotone function is at most countable.

P11

Derivative rules: continuity, product, chain (Bartle §6.1)

Three differentiation facts from Module 2.

Theorems 6.1.2, 6.1.3(c), 6.1.6
  • Differentiable \Rightarrow continuous (6.1.2): If F is differentiable at c, then F is continuous at c.
  • Product Rule (6.1.3c): (FG)' = F'G + FG'. (Reversed in Theorem 7.3.17 to give Integration by Parts.)
  • Chain Rule (6.1.6): If H(t) = F(\varphi(t)), then H'(t) = F'(\varphi(t))\,\varphi'(t). (Used in Theorem 7.3.8 for Substitution.)
P12

Mean Value Theorem (Bartle 6.2.4)

The single point where Module 2 enters the proof of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

Theorem 6.2.4 · Mean Value Theorem

If F is continuous on the closed interval [x_{i-1},x_i] and differentiable on the open interval (x_{i-1},x_i), then there exists a point u_i \in (x_{i-1},x_i) such that

F(x_i) - F(x_{i-1}) = F'(u_i)(x_i - x_{i-1}).

Notice that F'(u_i)(x_i - x_{i-1}) has the exact shape of one term of a Riemann sum with tag u_i!

P13

Countable sets and the Archimedean property

Listing a set in a sequence r_1, r_2, r_3, \dots and choosing n > 1/\varepsilon.

Countability and Archimedes

Archimedean Property: Given any \varepsilon > 0, there is n \in \mathbb{N} with n > 1/\varepsilon, i.e. 1/n < \varepsilon.

Countable set: A set whose elements can be listed in a sequence r_1, r_2, r_3, \dots (finite or countably infinite). In particular, the rational numbers \mathbb{Q} and \mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1] are countable.

P14

Dirichlet and Thomae functions (Bartle 5.1.6(g), (h))

Two benchmark functions from Module 1 that test where Riemann integration succeeds and fails.

Examples 5.1.6(g) and 5.1.6(h)

Dirichlet's function C7: f(x) = 1 if x \in \mathbb{Q}, f(x) = 0 if x \notin \mathbb{Q}. Discontinuous at every point of [0,1] because both rationals and irrationals are dense in \mathbb{R}.

Thomae's function C8: On [0,1], h(x) = 0 if x is irrational, h(0) = 1, and h(m/n) = 1/n if m, n \in \mathbb{N} have no common integer factor except 1. Continuous at every irrational in [0,1] and discontinuous at every rational in [0,1].

MATHS TOOLS · M01–M10

The ten tools built from zero

M01

Telescoping sums: why middle terms vanish

When you add consecutive differences (a_i - a_{i-1}), only the first and last survive.

From zero · write out four terms

Suppose you have numbers a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n and you add the differences (a_i - a_{i-1}):

\sum_{i=1}^n (a_i - a_{i-1}) = (\cancel{a_1} - a_0) + (\cancel{a_2} - \cancel{a_1}) + (\cancel{a_3} - \cancel{a_2}) + \cdots + (a_n - \cancel{a_{n-1}}) = a_n - a_0.

Three places we use this in Module 3:

  • Lengths of subintervals: If a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n = b, then \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - x_{i-1}) = x_n x_0 = b - a. The pieces of a partition always add up to the total length of [a,b].
  • Squares (Example 7.1.4(c)): \sum_{i=1}^n \tfrac12(x_i^2 - x_{i-1}^2) = \tfrac12(x_n^2 - x_0^2) = \tfrac12(b^2 - a^2).
  • Cubes (Exercise 7.1.14): \sum_{i=1}^n \tfrac13(x_i^3 - x_{i-1}^3) = \tfrac13(b^3 - a^3).
M02

Partitions and mesh: why \lVert P\rVert \to 0 is stronger than n \to \infty

The norm \lVert P\rVert is the length of the single widest subinterval, not the number of pieces.

How to find the norm of a partition

Given P = (x_0, x_1, \dots, x_n) on [a,b], list the n consecutive gaps \Delta x_i = x_i - x_{i-1} and pick the largest one: \lVert P\rVert = \max\{\Delta x_1, \dots, \Delta x_n\}.

For an equal partition into n pieces of equal width, every gap is (b-a)/n, so \lVert P\rVert = (b-a)/n. Here n \to \infty makes \lVert P\rVert \to 0. For an unequal partition, having many pieces does NOT force \lVert P\rVert to be small!

M03

Tags and endpoints: one point can tag two intervals

A tag t_i can be any point in [x_{i-1},x_i], including the left or right endpoint.

Why "at most 2n subintervals" appears in proofs

In each subinterval I_i = [x_{i-1},x_i] we pick one tag t_i \in [x_{i-1},x_i]. What if a special point c happens to be the partition point x_k between I_k = [x_{k-1},x_k] and I_{k+1} = [x_k,x_{k+1}]? Then an opponent could legally pick t_k = c as the right-endpoint tag of I_k and t_{k+1} = c as the left-endpoint tag of I_{k+1}.

So a single point c can be the tag of at most 2 subintervals, and a finite set E of n points can be the tag of at most 2n subintervals.

M04

Bounding a sum by the largest term times total length

How to turn \big\lvert\sum_{i=1}^n (f(t_i)-g(t_i))\Delta x_i\big\rvert into a single simple product.

Two standard sum estimates

Since subinterval lengths \Delta x_i = x_i - x_{i-1} are always positive, the triangle inequality gives

\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\,\Delta x_i\right\rvert \le \sum_{i=1}^n \lvert a_i\rvert\,\Delta x_i \le \left(\max_{1\le i\le n}\lvert a_i\rvert\right)\sum_{i=1}^n \Delta x_i = \left(\max_{1\le i\le n}\lvert a_i\rvert\right)(b-a).

Case 1 (small height everywhere): If \lvert a_i\rvert < \varepsilon/(b-a) on every piece, the whole sum is < \frac{\varepsilon}{b-a}(b-a) = \varepsilon.

Case 2 (one point c changed, Theorem 7.1.3): If f(x) = g(x) for all x \ne c, then f(t_i) - g(t_i) = 0 except on the (at most 2) subintervals whose tag is c. Each has width \Delta x_i \le \lVert P\rVert and height \lvert f(c) - g(c)\rvert \le \lvert f(c)\rvert + \lvert g(c)\rvert, so

\lvert S(f;\dot P) - S(g;\dot P)\rvert \le 2\big(\lvert f(c)\rvert + \lvert g(c)\rvert\big)\lVert P\rVert.

M05

Telescoping with monotonicity and with FTC

Two places where telescoping turns n rectangles into f(b)-f(a) or F(b)-F(a).

Staircase cancellation and MVT cancellation

1. Monotone staircase (Theorem 7.2.8): On n equal pieces of width (b-a)/n, an increasing function f has \max_{I_k} f = f(x_k) and \min_{I_k} f = f(x_{k-1}). The difference between upper and lower step integrals is

\frac{b-a}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n \big(f(x_k) - f(x_{k-1})\big) = \frac{b-a}{n}\big(f(b) - f(a)\big).

2. FTC First Form (Theorem 7.3.1): Applying the MVT to F on each [x_{i-1},x_i] gives F(x_i) - F(x_{i-1}) = f(u_i)\Delta x_i. Summing over i=1,\dots,n telescopes the left side to F(b) - F(a) and turns the right side into S(f;\dot P_u).

M06

Elementary step functions \varphi_J as building blocks

Write any step function as \sum_{j=1}^m k_j\varphi_{J_j} and read off its integral immediately.

Indicator recipe

For any subinterval J \subseteq [a,b] with endpoints c < d, the elementary step function \varphi_J(x) equals 1 on J and 0 outside J. Its integral is simply the length of J: \int_a^b \varphi_J = d - c (Lemma 7.2.4).

If \varphi equals k_1 on J_1, k_2 on J_2, \dots, k_m on J_m (where J_1,\dots,J_m partition [a,b]), then \varphi(x) = \sum_{j=1}^m k_j\varphi_{J_j}(x), so \int_a^b \varphi = \sum_{j=1}^m k_j(d_j - c_j): sum of (height \times width).

M07

The \varepsilon/2^k covering trick for countable sets

How an infinite sequence of points can be covered by intervals of total length \le \varepsilon.

Why countable sets have zero total length

Suppose Z = \{r_1, r_2, r_3, \dots\} is countable. If we gave every point r_k an interval of the same length \delta, the total length \delta + \delta + \cdots would be \infty. Instead, we shrink the interval width by half at each step!

Put r_k inside the open interval J_k = \left(r_k \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2^{k+1}},\, r_k + \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2^{k+1}}\right). The length of J_k is 2\cdot\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2^{k+1}} = \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2^k}. By P03, the total length of all infinitely many intervals is \sum_{k=1}^\infty \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2^k} = \varepsilon.

M08

The \varepsilon/2 (or \varepsilon/3) argument and \delta = \min\{\delta_1,\delta_2\}

How to combine two or three separate error estimates at the same time.

Combining tolerances

When a proof splits an error into two parts, \lvert A - B\rvert \le \text{Error}_1 + \text{Error}_2, ask for each part to be < \varepsilon/2 (or < \varepsilon/3 if there are three parts, as in the Additivity Theorem 7.2.9). If \text{Error}_1 < \varepsilon/2 needs \lVert P\rVert < \delta_1 and \text{Error}_2 < \varepsilon/2 needs \lVert P\rVert < \delta_2, choose

\delta = \min\{\delta_1, \delta_2\} > 0.

Then any partition with \lVert P\rVert < \delta satisfies both \lVert P\rVert < \delta_1 and \lVert P\rVert < \delta_2 simultaneously, so \text{Error}_1 + \text{Error}_2 < \varepsilon/2 + \varepsilon/2 = \varepsilon.

M09

Choosing \delta_\varepsilon explicitly from C\delta < \varepsilon

Do the algebra in scratch work first so your proof starts with a clean choice of \delta_\varepsilon.

Backwards in scratch, forwards in proof

If your scratch work shows that \lVert P\rVert < \delta implies \lvert S(f;\dot P) - L\rvert \le C\delta (for instance \lvert S(g;\dot P) - 8\rvert \le 5\delta in Example 7.1.4(b)), then to force C\delta < \varepsilon you simply pick any \delta_\varepsilon < \varepsilon/C (such as \delta_\varepsilon = \varepsilon/(2C), e.g. \varepsilon/10). If your estimate isalready strict (\lvert S - L\rvert < \delta), then \delta_\varepsilon = \varepsilon works directly.

M10

Splitting a Riemann sum by where the tags land

How Bartle handles jumps and popcorn functions (Examples 7.1.4(b),(d), 7.1.7; Exercises 7.1.4–5).

Two ways to split \dot P = \dot P_1 \cup \dot P_2

1. Tag in a subinterval [c_1,c_2] (Exercises 7.1.4–5): Suppose \lVert P\rVert < \delta and \dot P_1 consists of the subintervals I_i whose tag t_i lands in [c_1,c_2]. Because each I_i has width <\delta, no such interval can stick out past c_1-\delta or c_2+\delta, and every point of [c_1+\delta,c_2-\delta] must lie in one of them. Thus the union U_1 of intervals in \dot P_1 is trapped between:

[c_1+\delta,\, c_2-\delta] \subseteq U_1 \subseteq [c_1-\delta,\, c_2+\delta],

so the total width of \dot P_1 lies between (c_2-c_1)-2\delta and (c_2-c_1)+2\delta (or between 1-\delta and 1+\delta when c_1=a=0 is the endpoint of [0,3]).

2. Tag in a finite "bad" set E = \{x : f(x) \ge \varepsilon/2\} (Example 7.1.7): Put \dot P_1 = subintervals with tag t_i \in E, and \dot P_2 = subintervals with tag t_i \notin E. If E has n points, \dot P_1 has at most 2n subintervals (by M03) of total width \le 2n\lVert P\rVert, while on \dot P_2 the height is <\varepsilon/2 and total width is \le b-a.
