T10

Relative extrema; the Interior Extremum Theorem

Where can a hill-top or valley-bottom be? (related: CALC-24 Q10 critical points.)

PuzzleA smooth hill has a top. What must the slope be exactly at the top? Now ask the same of $f(x) = x$ on [0, 1], whose top is at x = 1. Same answer? Find out why not.
{{fig:interior_ext}}
Definition · relative extremum (Bartle §6.2)Let $f : I \to \mathbb R$. f has a relative maximum at $c \in I$ if there is a neighbourhood $V = V_\delta(c)$ such that $f(x) \le f(c)$ for all $x \in V \cap I$. f has a relative minimum at $c$ if $f(c) \le f(x)$ for all $x \in V \cap I$. A relative extremum is either. "Relative" = best among the points near c only. Absolute = best on the whole domain.
Theorem 6.2.1 · Interior Extremum TheoremLet $c$ be an interior point of the interval $I$ at which $f : I \to \mathbb R$ has a relative extremum. If the derivative of $f$ at $c$ exists, then $f'(c) = 0$. Corollary 6.2.2: if f is continuous on I and has a relative extremum at an interior point c, then either $f'(c)$ does not exist or $f'(c) = 0$. These are the only places to look.
Skeleton · Interior Extremum (maximum case)Let f have a relative maximum at the interior point c and let $f'(c)$ exist. Suppose $f'(c) > 0$. Then ? for x just right of c, so $f(x) > f(c)$: contradiction. Suppose $f'(c) < 0$. Then ? for x just left of c: contradiction. So $f'(c) = 0$. ∎ (Minimum: apply to $-f$.)
Knowc interior; $f(x) \le f(c)$ for x near c; the quotient $\frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c}$ has a limit $f'(c)$.Want$f'(c) = 0$. We show "$> 0$" and "$< 0$" are impossible.
Proof (relative maximum)
Suppose $f'(c) > 0$.Proof by contradiction (M10): we kill both strict signs.
The quotient $\frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c}$ has limit $f'(c) > 0$, so there is a neighbourhood $V \subseteq I$ of c in which the quotient is $> 0$ for $x \ne c$.Local sign of a limit (Thm 4.2.9). "$V \subseteq I$" is possible because c is interior.
Take $x \in V$ with $x > c$. Then $f(x) - f(c) = (x - c) \cdot (\text{positive}) > 0$, i.e. $f(x) > f(c)$.Positive times positive.
Such x exist arbitrarily near c, which contradicts "$f(x) \le f(c)$ near c".Definition of relative maximum.
Suppose $f'(c) < 0$. In the same V (shrunk), the quotient is $< 0$. Take $x < c$: $(x - c) < 0$ and quotient $< 0$ give $f(x) - f(c) > 0$. Contradiction again.Bartle writes "similarly"; this is the "how": the left side is where it breaks.
Hence $f'(c) = 0$. ∎Neither $> 0$ nor $< 0$ is possible.
Hypotheses checklist (what fails without each)
  • c is interior$f(x) = x$ on [0, 1]: max at 1, min at 0, but $f' = 1$ N7
  • $f'(c)$ exists$f(x) = |x|$ on [−1, 1]: min at 0, no derivative there C5
  • (converse) $f'(c) = 0$ ⇒ extremum?$x^3$ at 0: $f'(0) = 0$ but no extremum C17

Where each was used. Interior: the neighbourhood V lies inside I, so points on both sides of c exist. Derivative exists: gives the limit whose sign we read. Extremum: forbids $f(x) > f(c)$ nearby.

Your intuition lies · "critical point = extremum"A critical point is only a candidate. $x^3$ at 0 has slope 0 and no extremum C17. $|x|$ at 0 has no slope and is a minimum C5. Endpoints are never interior, so check them separately.
Worked example 1 · full (CALC-24 Q10)

Find all critical points of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x + 1$ and decide which are extrema.

$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x - 1)(x + 1)$, zero at $x = \pm 1$. f' exists everywhere, so any relative extremum is at $\pm 1$ (Thm 6.2.1). Values: $f(-1) = -1 + 3 + 1 = 3$, $f(1) = 1 - 3 + 1 = -1$. Sign of $f'$: $+$ on $(-\infty, -1)$, $-$ on $(-1, 1)$, $+$ on $(1, \infty)$, so a relative max 3 at −1 and a relative min −1 at 1 (First Derivative Test, T15).

On $[-2, 2]$ also test the ends: $f(-2) = -8 + 6 + 1 = -1$, $f(2) = 8 - 6 + 1 = 3$. Absolute max 3 (at −1 and at 2), absolute min −1 (at 1 and at −2).

T11

Rolle's Theorem

Same height at both ends, so the tangent is flat somewhere between.

PuzzleYou leave home and return home. Was there a moment when your height above sea level was not changing?
{{fig:rolle}}
Theorem 6.2.3 · Rolle's TheoremSuppose that $f$ is continuous on a closed interval $I = [a, b]$, that the derivative $f'$ exists at every point of the open interval $(a, b)$, and that $f(a) = f(b) = 0$. Then there exists at least one point $c$ in $(a, b)$ such that $f'(c) = 0$. "At least one": there may be several. If $f(a) = f(b) = h \ne 0$, apply the theorem to $f - h$.
Skeleton · RolleIf $f \equiv 0$, any c works. Otherwise f takes a positive (say) value. By the Max–Min Theorem f has a maximum at some $c \in [a,b]$ with $f(c) >$ ?. Then $c \ne a, b$ because ?. So c is interior and f has a relative maximum there, hence ?. ∎
Knowf continuous on [a, b]; differentiable on (a, b); $f(a) = f(b) = 0$.Wanta point $c \in (a, b)$ with $f'(c) = 0$.
Proof
If $f(x) = 0$ for all x, then $f' = 0$ and any $c \in (a, b)$ works.Trivial case first.
Otherwise f takes a nonzero value. Replacing f by $-f$ if necessary, assume f takes a positive value.$f'(c) = 0 \iff (-f)'(c) = 0$, so nothing is lost.
f is continuous on the closed bounded interval [a, b], so it attains a maximum $M = f(c)$ at some $c \in [a, b]$; and $M > 0$.Max–Min Theorem 5.3.4 (Module 1, T9). Needs closed, bounded, continuous.
$c \ne a$ and $c \ne b$, since $f(a) = f(b) = 0 < M$. So $c \in (a, b)$.This is why the equal end values are in the hypotheses.
f is differentiable at the interior point c and has a relative (indeed absolute) maximum there, so $f'(c) = 0$. ∎Interior Extremum Theorem 6.2.1 (T10). Differentiability is needed only on the open interval.
Hypotheses checklist
  • f continuous on the closed [a, b]$f(x) = x$ on $[0, 1)$, $f(1) = 0$: differentiable inside, ends both 0, but $f' = 1$ C18
  • f differentiable on (a, b)$f(x) = 1 - |x|$ on $[-1, 1]$: ends 0, slope only $\pm 1$ C18
  • $f(a) = f(b)$$f(x) = x$ on $[0, 1]$: $f' = 1$ C18

Where each was used. Continuity on [a, b]: Max–Min gives the maximum point. Equal ends: the maximum is not at an end. Differentiability on (a, b): $f'(c)$ exists at the interior maximum, so 6.2.1 applies.

Your intuition lies · "c is the top"Rolle gives some flat point, not necessarily the highest point, and not only one. It may be a hill-top, a valley or a flat shoulder.

The most useful theorem of the module: Rolle, tilted.

PuzzleA car covers 120 km in exactly 2 hours. Must the speedometer have read exactly 60 km/h at some instant? (Yes. Why?)

Then there exists at least one point $c$ in $(a, b)$ such that $$f(b) - f(a) = f'(c)(b - a).$$ Reading: the average slope over [a, b] equals the instantaneous slope at some c. Geometrically: some tangent is parallel to the chord.

Zero derivative means constant

If the speed is 0 all the time, you did not move.

Puzzle$\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x$ has derivative $2\sin x\cos x - 2\cos x\sin x = 0$. What does that tell you without any trigonometry?

Increasing and decreasing: the sign of f′

Intervals of increase and decrease (CALC-24 Q9, Q20(1); old MCQ MTS-22 MCQ 5).

Puzzle$x^3$ is always climbing, yet its derivative is 0 at x = 0. And $f'(0) > 0$ at a single point does not make a function climb near that point. Both are true: see below.
{{fig:increasing}}
Definition · increasing, decreasing (Bartle)$f : I \to \mathbb R$ is increasing on I if $x_1, x_2 \in I$, $x_1 < x_2$ imply $f(x_1) \le f(x_2)$; strictly increasing if $f(x_1) < f(x_2)$. Decreasing: $f(x_1) \ge f(x_2)$; strictly decreasing: $>$. Bartle's "increasing" is non-strict, so a constant function is both increasing and decreasing N14.
Theorem 6.2.7 · MonotonicityLet $f : I \to \mathbb R$ be differentiable on the interval $I$. Then (a) $f$ is increasing on I if and only if $f'(x) \ge 0$ for all $x \in I$; (b) $f$ is decreasing on I if and only if $f'(x) \le 0$ for all $x \in I$. Strict version: if $f' > 0$ on I then f is strictly increasing. The converse is false ($x^3$).

T15

The First Derivative Test

Sign change of f′ means an extremum (CALC-24 Q18).

Puzzle$|x|$ has a minimum at 0 but no derivative there. What does the test need instead of $f'(0)$?
{{fig:first_test}}
Theorem 6.2.8 · First Derivative TestLet $f$ be continuous on the interval $I = [a, b]$ and let $c$ be an interior point of I. Assume that $f$ is differentiable on $(a, c)$ and $(c, b)$.

(a) If there is a neighbourhood $(c - \delta, c + \delta) \subseteq I$ such that $f'(x) \ge 0$ for $c - \delta < x < c$ and $f'(x) \le 0$ for $c < x < c + \delta$, then $f$ has a relative maximum at $c$.

(b) If $f'(x) \le 0$ for $c - \delta < x < c$ and $f'(x) \ge 0$ for $c < x < c + \delta$, then $f$ has a relative minimum at $c$.

Up then down = hill-top; down then up = valley. $f'(c)$ itself need not exist.
Skeleton · (a)Left of c: for $x \in (c - \delta, c)$ apply the MVT on ? to get $f(c) - f(x) = f'(\xi)(c - x) \ge 0$. Right of c: for $x \in (c, c + \delta)$ apply MVT on ? to get $f(x) - f(c) = f'(\xi)(x - c) \le 0$. So $f(x) \le f(c)$ on the whole neighbourhood. ∎
Proof of (a)
Let $x \in (c - \delta, c)$. f is continuous on $[x, c]$ and differentiable on $(x, c)$.Differentiable on $(a, c)$, and continuous at c by hypothesis.
MVT: $f(c) - f(x) = f'(\xi)(c - x)$ for some $\xi \in (x, c)$. Here $f'(\xi) \ge 0$ and $c - x > 0$, so $f(c) \ge f(x)$.$\xi$ lies in $(c - \delta, c)$ where $f' \ge 0$.
Let $x \in (c, c + \delta)$. MVT on $[c, x]$: $f(x) - f(c) = f'(\xi)(x - c)$, $\xi \in (c, x)$, and $f'(\xi) \le 0$, so $f(x) \le f(c)$.Bartle says "how?"; this is how. Now $\xi$ lies in $(c, c + \delta)$.
So $f(x) \le f(c)$ for all x in $(c - \delta, c + \delta)$: relative maximum at c. ∎Definition of relative maximum. (b): apply (a) to $-f$.

Proof
