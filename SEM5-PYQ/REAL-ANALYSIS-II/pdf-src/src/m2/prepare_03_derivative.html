The derivative at a point

T01

The derivative: a limit of chord slopes

From the tangent of Plus Two to the ε–δ definition (related FYUGP CALC-24 Q5).

{{fig:secant_tangent}}
Definition 6.1.1 · say it word for wordLet $I \subseteq \mathbb R$ be an interval, let $f : I \to \mathbb R$, and let $c \in I$. We say that a real number $L$ is the derivative of f at c if, given any $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $\delta(\varepsilon)>0$ such that if $x \in I$ satisfies $0 < |x - c| < \delta(\varepsilon)$, then $$\left|\frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} - L\right| < \varepsilon.$$ In this case we say f is differentiable at c and write $f'(c) = L$. In plain words: the slope of the chord from $(c, f(c))$ to $(x, f(x))$ can be forced as close to L as anyone demands, just by taking x close enough to c (and not equal to c). Equivalent short form: $f'(c) = \lim_{x\to c} \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c}$. If c is an endpoint of I the limit is one-sided.
Plus Two said h, Bartle says x − cPlus Two: $\frac{f(c + h) - f(c)}{h}$ as $h \to 0$. Put $h = x - c$ and it is the same limit. Why "0 < |x − c|"? At $x = c$ the quotient would be 0/0, so $x = c$ is never allowed.

Result · sequence form (Bartle Ex 6.1.13)If f is differentiable at c then $f'(c) = \lim_{n\to\infty} n\{f(c + \tfrac1n) - f(c)\}$. Reason: $x_n = c + \frac1n \to c$ with $x_n \ne c$, and $n\{f(x_n) - f(c)\} = \frac{f(x_n) - f(c)}{x_n - c}$ (sequential criterion for limits).

The converse is false. $f = |x|$, $c = 0$: $n\{\,|1/n| - 0\,\} = 1$, so the sequence converges to 1, yet $f'(0)$ does not exist (T02).

T02

Differentiable ⇒ continuous (and not conversely)

The first theorem of the unit; the piecewise trap (related FYUGP CALC-24 Q13).

{{fig:abs_corner}}
Theorem 6.1.2If $f : I \to \mathbb R$ has a derivative at $c \in I$, then f is continuous at c. In plain words: no jumps and no holes at a point where there is a tangent. Contrapositive: if f is not continuous at c, then f′(c) does not exist. That is the quickest way to answer a piecewise question.
SkeletonFor $x \ne c$ write $f(x) - f(c) = $ ? $\cdot (x - c)$. Let $x \to c$: the first factor tends to ?, the second to ?. So $f(x) \to f(c)$. ∎
KnowThe quotient $\frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c}$ has limit $f'(c)$ as $x \to c$.Want$\lim_{x\to c} f(x) = f(c)$, that is, $\lim (f(x) - f(c)) = 0$.
Proof of 6.1.2
For $x \ne c$: $f(x) - f(c) = \dfrac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c}\,(x - c)$.Multiply and divide by $x - c \ne 0$. This is only algebra, and it needs $x \ne c$.
Let $x \to c$: the first factor tends to $f'(c)$, the second to 0.Hypothesis for the first factor; $x - c \to 0$ is trivial.
So $\lim_{x\to c}(f(x) - f(c)) = f'(c)\cdot 0 = 0$.Product of limits (Theorem 4.2.4): both limits exist.
Hence $\lim_{x\to c} f(x) = f(c)$, so f is continuous at c. ∎Definition of continuity at a cluster point (5.1.2).
Hypotheses checklist · "the converse is false"
  • f′(c) must exist$|x|$ is continuous at 0 but has no derivative there C5
  • the derivative must be finite$x^{1/3}$ is continuous at 0, quotient $x^{-2/3}$; at $x_n = n^{-3}$ it equals $n^2 \to \infty$
  • the same point c everywheredifferentiable at c says nothing about continuity at other points (N4)
Where each hypothesis was usedStep 2 uses the existence of the limit $f'(c)$ (a real number). Nothing else is needed: no continuity near c, no interval beyond c ∈ I.
Worked example 1 · |x| at 0

$\dfrac{|x| - |0|}{x - 0} = \dfrac{|x|}{x} = \begin{cases} +1 & x>0 \\ -1 & x<0 \end{cases}$. Two sequences $x_n = \frac1n$ and $y_n = -\frac1n$ both tend to 0, but the quotient gives $1$ and $-1$.

So no limit: $f'(0)$ does not exist, although $|x|$ is continuous. C5 (The same quotient is the old MCQ "$\lim_{x\to0}|x|/x$": answer, no limit.)

Worked example 2 · piecewise at a joint (CALC-24 Q13, fill the gaps)

$f(x) = x^2$ for $x \le 1$, $f(x) = 4\sqrt x$ for $x>1$. Is f differentiable at 1?

$f(1) = $ ?. $\lim_{x\to1^+} 4\sqrt x = $ ?. Equal? ? So f is ? at 1, hence by 6.1.2 ?.

Answer: $f(1) = 1$, right limit $= 4$. Not equal, so f is discontinuous at 1 and $f'(1)$ does not exist. The question "if so, find the value" is a trap.

Making it work: $F(x) = x^2$ ($x \le 1$), $ax + b$ ($x>1$). Continuity: $a + b = 1$. Left derivative: $\frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = x + 1 \to 2$. Right derivative (with $b = 1 - a$): $\frac{a(x-1)}{x - 1} = a$. So $a = 2$, $b = -1$, $F'(1) = 2$.

Check yourself
$G(x) = x^2$ for $x \le 1$, $G(x) = \sqrt x$ for $x>1$. Differentiable at 1?
Continuous: both sides give 1. Left derivative: $x + 1 \to 2$. Right derivative: $\frac{\sqrt x - 1}{x - 1} = \frac1{\sqrt x + 1} \to \frac12$. Different, so not differentiable at 1.
Exam trap · piecewise functionsAlways in this order: (1) values equal at the joint? (continuity); (2) one-sided derivatives equal? Matching values alone are not enough (G above); matching values and slopes give differentiability.

T03

Algebra of derivatives: sum, product, quotient

The rules you know from Plus Two, now with proofs (Bartle 6.1.3).

Picture · a rectangle that growsLet f be the width and g the height. Nudge x: the width grows by $\Delta f$ and the height by $\Delta g$. The new area is the old one plus a strip $g\,\Delta f$, plus a strip $f\,\Delta g$, plus a tiny corner $\Delta f\,\Delta g$. Divide by $\Delta x$: the corner disappears because $\Delta f \to 0$. That is $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$.
Theorem 6.1.3Let $f, g : I \to \mathbb R$ be differentiable at $c \in I$. Then:
(a) for $\alpha \in \mathbb R$, $(\alpha f)'(c) = \alpha f'(c)$;
(b) $(f + g)'(c) = f'(c) + g'(c)$;
(c) Product Rule: $(fg)'(c) = f'(c)g(c) + f(c)g'(c)$;
(d) Quotient Rule: if $g(c) \ne 0$, then $\left(\dfrac fg\right)'(c) = \dfrac{f'(c)g(c) - f(c)g'(c)}{(g(c))^2}$. In plain words: differentiate one piece at a time. Product: "first-prime second plus first second-prime". Quotient: "bottom times top-prime, minus top times bottom-prime, over bottom squared".
Skeleton · product ruleFor $x \ne c$: $\dfrac{f(x)g(x) - f(c)g(c)}{x - c} = \dfrac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c}\,\cdot$ ? $+$ ? $\cdot\dfrac{g(x) - g(c)}{x - c}$. Let $x \to c$ using that g is ? at c. ∎
Proof of (a), (b), (c), (d)

(a), (b). $\frac{(\alpha f)(x) - (\alpha f)(c)}{x - c} = \alpha\frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c}$, and the quotient of a sum is the sum of quotients. Apply the limit laws (4.2.4).

(c)

For $x \ne c$: $\dfrac{f(x)g(x) - f(c)g(c)}{x - c} = \dfrac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c}\,g(x) + f(c)\,\dfrac{g(x) - g(c)}{x - c}$.Add and subtract $f(c)g(x)$ in the numerator. This one trick is the whole proof.
g is continuous at c, so $g(x) \to g(c)$.Theorem 6.1.2 (T02): g is differentiable at c. Without it the first term has no limit.
Let $x \to c$: the limit is $f'(c)g(c) + f(c)g'(c)$. ∎Limit laws: the four factors have limits $f'(c)$, $g(c)$, $f(c)$, $g'(c)$.

(d)

Since g is continuous at c and $g(c) \ne 0$, $g \ne 0$ on a neighbourhood J of c.Local sign (Theorem 4.2.9). This is what makes $f/g$ defined near c.
For $x \in J$, $x \ne c$: $\dfrac{f(x)/g(x) - f(c)/g(c)}{x - c} = \dfrac{1}{g(x)g(c)}\left[\dfrac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c}\,g(c) - f(c)\,\dfrac{g(x) - g(c)}{x - c}\right]$.Common denominator, then add and subtract $f(c)g(c)$.
Let $x \to c$ (using $g(x) \to g(c)$): the limit is $\dfrac{f'(c)g(c) - f(c)g'(c)}{g(c)^2}$. ∎Limit laws.
Hypotheses checklist
  • both f and g differentiable at c$|x|\cdot|x| = x^2$ is differentiable at 0 although $|x|$ is not: the rule does not apply, and the converse is false C5
  • $g(c) \ne 0$ for the quotient$f = 1$, $g = x$ at 0: $1/x$ is not even defined at 0
  • same point c, and f, g both on Ipieces defined on different intervals cannot be combined
Exam trap$(fg)' \ne f'g'$. With $f = g = x$: $(x^2)' = 2x$, but $f'g' = 1$. Likewise $(f/g)' \ne f'/g'$.

T04

Sums, products of many functions; the power rule

Induction on the product rule (Bartle 6.1.4).

Picture · a box with n sidesA product of n factors changes by letting one factor at a time change while the others stand still: n terms, each with one prime.
Corollary 6.1.4Let $f_1, \dots, f_n$ be functions on I, differentiable at $c \in I$.
(a) $(f_1 + \cdots + f_n)'(c) = f_1'(c) + \cdots + f_n'(c)$.
(b) $(f_1 f_2 \cdots f_n)'(c) = f_1'(c)f_2(c)\cdots f_n(c) + \cdots + f_1(c)\cdots f_{n-1}(c)f_n'(c)$.
In particular, $(f^n)'(c) = n\,(f(c))^{n-1}f'(c)$, and taking $f(x) = x$: $D\,x^n = n x^{n-1}$ for $n \in \mathbb N$. In plain words: bring the power down, lower it by one, and multiply by the derivative of what is inside (the last factor is the one students forget).
Skeleton · inductionBase $n = 1$: ?. Step: assume true for n; write $f_1\cdots f_{n+1} = (f_1\cdots f_n)\cdot f_{n+1}$ and apply the ? rule. ∎
Proof (power rule by induction)
$n = 1$: $D\,x = 1 = 1\cdot x^0$.The quotient $\frac{x - c}{x - c} = 1$.
Assume $D\,x^k = kx^{k-1}$. Write $x^{k+1} = x^k\cdot x$.So the product rule (T03(c)) applies with $f = x^k$, $g = x$, both differentiable.
$D\,x^{k+1} = kx^{k-1}\cdot x + x^k\cdot 1 = (k + 1)x^k$. ∎Add the two terms: $kx^k + x^k$.
Result · negative powers (quotient rule)$x^{-n} = 1/x^n$ with $g(x) = x^n \ne 0$ for $x \ne 0$: $D\,x^{-n} = \dfrac{0 - nx^{n-1}}{x^{2n}} = -nx^{-n-1}$. At $x = 2$, $n = 3$: $-3/16 = -0.1875$.
Hypotheses checklist
  • n is a natural number (finite product)real powers $x^r$ come later (Bartle §8.3); quote $D\,x^r = rx^{r-1}$ as a known fact
  • each $f_i$ differentiable at c$f = |x|$: $f^2 = x^2$ has derivative 0 at 0, but $2f f'$ has no meaning because $f'(0)$ does not exist C5
Exam trap$(f^n)' = n f^{n-1}f'$, not $nf^{n-1}$. Test with $f = x^2$, $n = 3$: $x^6$ has derivative $6x^5$; "$3f^2$" gives $3x^4$ only, and the missing factor $f' = 2x$ gives $3x^4\cdot 2x = 6x^5$.

T05

Carathéodory's characterisation

"Fill the hole in the chord-slope" (Bartle 6.1.5); the tool that proves the chain rule.

{{fig:caratheodory}}
Theorem 6.1.5 · CarathéodoryLet f be defined on an interval I containing the point c. Then f is differentiable at c if and only if there exists a function $\varphi$ on I that is continuous at c and satisfies $$f(x) - f(c) = \varphi(x)\,(x - c) \quad \text{for } x \in I.$$ In this case, $\varphi(c) = f'(c)$. In plain words: write the difference $f(x) - f(c)$ as "something times $(x - c)$". Differentiable means you can choose that "something" so that it behaves nicely (is continuous) at c. Its value at c is the derivative.
Skeleton(⇒) Define $\varphi(x) = $ ? for $x \ne c$ and $\varphi(c) = $ ?. Check continuity at c and the identity at every x. (⇐) For $x \ne c$ divide to get $\frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} = $ ?; let $x \to c$. ∎
Proof of 6.1.5

(⇒) Assume $f'(c)$ exists.

Define $\varphi(x) = \dfrac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c}$ for $x \ne c$ and $\varphi(c) = f'(c)$.The difference quotient is the only possible choice for $x \ne c$; the hole at c is filled with the derivative.
$\varphi$ is continuous at c: $\lim_{x\to c}\varphi(x) = f'(c) = \varphi(c)$.Definition of $f'(c)$.
$f(x) - f(c) = \varphi(x)(x - c)$ for every $x \in I$.For $x \ne c$ multiply out. For $x = c$ both sides are 0, so check c too.

(⇐) Assume $\varphi$ exists, continuous at c.

For $x \ne c$: $\dfrac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} = \varphi(x)$.Divide by $x - c \ne 0$.
$\lim_{x\to c}\varphi(x) = \varphi(c)$.$\varphi$ is continuous at c.
So the quotient has a limit: $f'(c)$ exists and equals $\varphi(c)$. ∎Definition 6.1.1.
Hypotheses checklist
  • $\varphi$ continuous at c (not everywhere)$|x|$ at 0: $\varphi(x) = \operatorname{sgn} x$ for $x \ne 0$ is forced, with no continuous value at 0 C5
  • the identity holds for all $x \in I$, including c$\varphi$ is determined for $x \ne c$ by the quotient; only its value at c is free
  • I an interval containing c$\varphi$ must be defined on all of I, otherwise the chain rule argument cannot compose
Worked example 1 · $f(x) = x^3$ (Bartle's illustration)

$x^3 - c^3 = (x^2 + cx + c^2)(x - c)$. So $\varphi(x) = x^2 + cx + c^2$, a polynomial, continuous everywhere.

$f'(c) = \varphi(c) = c^2 + c^2 + c^2 = 3c^2$. Check at $c = 1$: $\varphi(x) = x^2 + x + 1$, $\varphi(1) = 3$, and $\varphi(2) = 7 = \frac{8 - 1}{2 - 1}$.

Worked example 2 · $\sqrt x$ at $c > 0$, fill the gaps

$\sqrt x - \sqrt c = \dfrac{x - c}{\sqrt x + \sqrt c}$ (multiply top and bottom by $\sqrt x + \sqrt c$). So $\varphi(x) = $ ? for $x \ne c$, and $\varphi(c) = $ ?.

Answer: $\varphi(x) = \dfrac1{\sqrt x + \sqrt c}$, continuous at c since $\sqrt x + \sqrt c \to 2\sqrt c \ne 0$ (this needs $c>0$). $f'(c) = \dfrac1{2\sqrt c}$; at $c = 4$: $\frac14$. At $c = 0$ there is no $\varphi$ ($\varphi(x) = x^{-1/2}$ blows up).

Check yourself
Find $\varphi$ for $f(x) = x^2 + 3x$ at c and deduce $f'(c)$.
$f(x) - f(c) = (x^2 - c^2) + 3(x - c) = (x + c + 3)(x - c)$. So $\varphi(x) = x + c + 3$ and $f'(c) = 2c + 3$.
Your intuition will lie to you here"$\varphi$ is the derivative function." No: $\varphi$ depends on the point c and on x. It is the chord slope from c, whose value at x = c is $f'(c)$.

T06

The chain rule

Derivative of a composite (Bartle 6.1.6; 10-mark proof).

Picture · gearsIf y changes 3 times as fast as u, and u changes 2 times as fast as x, then y changes 6 times as fast as x. The rates multiply. The only care: the outer rate is measured at the inner value $f(c)$, not at c.
Theorem 6.1.6 · Chain RuleLet I, J be intervals in $\mathbb R$, let $g : I \to \mathbb R$ and $f : J \to \mathbb R$ be functions such that $f(J) \subseteq I$, and let $c \in J$. If f is differentiable at c and g is differentiable at $f(c)$, then the composite $g \circ f$ is differentiable at c and $$(g \circ f)'(c) = g'(f(c))\cdot f'(c).$$ In plain words: differentiate the outside (leave the inside alone), then multiply by the derivative of the inside.
Why the quick proof fails (N1)Quick idea: $\frac{g(f(x)) - g(f(c))}{x - c} = \frac{g(f(x)) - g(f(c))}{f(x) - f(c)}\cdot\frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c}$. But $f(x) = f(c)$ may happen at points x arbitrarily close to c (for $x^2\sin(1/x)$ at 0, $f(\frac1{n\pi}) = 0$), so you would divide by zero. Carathéodory avoids all division.
SkeletonGet $\varphi$ for f at c. Get $\psi$ for g at $d = f(c)$. Substitute $y = f(x)$: $g(f(x)) - g(f(c)) = $ ? $\cdot (x - c)$. Show the bracket is continuous at c with value ?. ∎
Proof of 6.1.6
There is $\varphi$ on J, continuous at c, with $f(x) - f(c) = \varphi(x)(x - c)$ and $\varphi(c) = f'(c)$.Carathéodory (⇒) for f at c (T05).
With $d = f(c)$ there is $\psi$ on I, continuous at d, with $g(y) - g(d) = \psi(y)(y - d)$ and $\psi(d) = g'(d)$.Carathéodory again, for the outer function g at d.
Put $y = f(x)$ ($x \in J$, allowed since $f(J) \subseteq I$): $g(f(x)) - g(f(c)) = \psi(f(x))\,[f(x) - f(c)] = \big[\psi(f(x))\,\varphi(x)\big](x - c)$.Substitute the identity of step 2, then of step 1. Pure algebra, no division.
$\Phi(x) = \psi(f(x))\,\varphi(x)$ is continuous at c.f is continuous at c (6.1.2); $\psi$ is continuous at $f(c)$, so $\psi\circ f$ is continuous at c (composite theorem, Module 1); $\varphi$ is continuous at c; products of continuous functions are continuous.
$\Phi(c) = \psi(f(c))\varphi(c) = g'(f(c))\,f'(c)$.$\psi(d) = g'(d)$ and $\varphi(c) = f'(c)$.
By Carathéodory (⇐), $g\circ f$ is differentiable at c with $(g\circ f)'(c) = \Phi(c)$. ∎Step 3 is exactly the Carathéodory identity for $g \circ f$.
Marks (10): statement 2 · Carathéodory for f 2 · for g 2 · composition identity 2 · continuity of Φ and conclusion 2.
Hypotheses checklist
  • f differentiable at c$f = |x|$ at 0 with $g(y) = y^2$: $(g\circ f)(x) = x^2$ happens to work, but $2|x|\,f'$ is meaningless
  • g differentiable at the point $f(c)$, not at c$g = |y|$, $f(x) = x$ at $c = 0$: $g\circ f = |x|$ has no derivative C5
  • $f(J) \subseteq I$ (composite defined)$g = \sqrt y$ on $[0,\infty)$, $f(x) = x - 1$ on $\mathbb R$: $g(f(x))$ is undefined for $x<1$
NoteThe hypotheses are sufficient, not necessary: $g = |y|$, $f = x^2$ at 0 gives $g \circ f = x^2$, differentiable although g is not differentiable at $f(0) = 0$.
Worked example 1 · Bartle Ex 6.1.5(b)

$h(x) = \sqrt{x^2 - 2x + 5}$. Name the inner $f(x) = x^2 - 2x + 5$ and outer $g(y) = \sqrt y$.

Inner value $f(x) = (x - 1)^2 + 4 \ge 4 > 0$, so g is differentiable at $f(x)$ for every x.

$g'(y) = \frac1{2\sqrt y}$ evaluated at $y = f(x)$, times $f'(x) = 2x - 2$: $$h'(x) = \frac{2x - 2}{2\sqrt{x^2 - 2x + 5}} = \frac{x - 1}{\sqrt{x^2 - 2x + 5}}.$$ Check at $x = 3$: $\frac{2}{\sqrt8} = 0.7071$.

Result · even and odd (Bartle Ex 6.1.9)If f is differentiable and even ($f(-x) = f(x)$) then $f'$ is odd; if f is odd then $f'$ is even.

T07

The derivative of |f| and of sgn

Where absolute-value functions have corners (Bartle Example 6.1.7(c), Ex 6.1.7, 6.1.8).

Picture · folding the graph$|f|$ is the graph of f with every part below the x-axis folded up. Smooth parts stay smooth; only where f crosses the axis with a non-zero slope does the fold make a corner. Example $|x^2 - 1|$: corners (cusps) at $(\pm1, 0)$, a hump of height 1 at $x = 0$, and the two sides rising like $x^2 - 1$ for $|x|>1$.
Example 6.1.7(c) and Exercise 6.1.7$g(x) = |x|$ is differentiable at every $x \ne 0$ with $g'(x) = \operatorname{sgn} x$ (where $\operatorname{sgn} x = 1, 0, -1$ for $x > 0, = 0, < 0$); $g'(0)$ does not exist. Consequently, if f is differentiable at c and $f(c) \ne 0$, then $$|f|'(c) = \operatorname{sgn}(f(c))\,f'(c).$$ If $f(c) = 0$, then $|f|$ is differentiable at c if and only if $f'(c) = 0$. In plain words: away from zeros, $|f|$ has slope $+f'$ where f is positive and $-f'$ where f is negative. At a zero of f the fold is smooth only if f is already flat there.
Skeleton · $f(c) = 0$For $x \ne c$: $\dfrac{|f(x)| - 0}{x - c} = \pm\left|\dfrac{f(x)}{x - c}\right|$ with sign ? for $x>c$ and ? for $x<c$. Right limit $= $ ?, left limit $= $ ?. Equal iff ?. ∎
Proof (f(c) = 0 case)
For $x>c$: $\dfrac{|f(x)| - |f(c)|}{x - c} = \dfrac{|f(x)|}{x - c} = \left|\dfrac{f(x)}{x - c}\right|$. For $x<c$: it equals $-\left|\dfrac{f(x)}{x - c}\right|$.$f(c) = 0$; the sign of $x - c$ is + on the right and − on the left, and $|x - c|$ is the absolute value of the denominator.
As $x \to c$, $\left|\dfrac{f(x)}{x - c}\right| \to |f'(c)|$.$\frac{f(x)}{x - c} = \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} \to f'(c)$, and $|\cdot|$ is continuous.
So the right limit is $+|f'(c)|$ and the left limit is $-|f'(c)|$.Step 1 and 2 combined.
They agree iff $|f'(c)| = 0$, that is $f'(c) = 0$. ∎One-sided limits must be equal for the limit to exist (M03, Module 1).

T08

Derivatives of tan, sec, cot, csc

The quotient rule applied to sin and cos (Bartle Example 6.1.7(d)).

Picture$\tan x$ climbs between its vertical asymptotes at $\pm\pi/2$; its slope is never below 1 (it is $\sec^2 x \ge 1$) and shoots to infinity near the asymptotes. At $x = \pi/4$ the slope is $\sec^2\frac\pi4 = 2$.
Example 6.1.7(d) · use $\sin' = \cos$, $\cos' = -\sin$ (Plus Two facts)$D\tan x = \sec^2 x$ and $D\sec x = \sec x\tan x$ for $x \ne (2k + 1)\pi/2$;
$D\cot x = -\csc^2 x$ and $D\csc x = -\csc x\cot x$ for $x \ne k\pi$. Rule of thumb: the "co-" functions get a minus sign.
SkeletonWrite the function as a quotient: $\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}$. Apply (d) of Theorem 6.1.3, valid where the bottom is ?. Simplify using ?. ∎
Proof

$D\tan x = \dfrac{\cos x\cdot\cos x - \sin x\cdot(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x} = \dfrac{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x$.Theorem 6.1.3(d); then $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$.
$D\sec x = D\dfrac{1}{\cos x} = \dfrac{0\cdot\cos x - 1\cdot(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x} = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos^2 x} = \sec x\tan x$. ∎$f = 1$, $f' = 0$ in the quotient rule.

T09

x² sin(1/x): differentiable, but f′ is not continuous

The most famous example of the unit (Bartle Example 6.1.7(e), Ex 6.1.10, 6.1.12).

{{fig:xsin_deriv}}
Example 6.1.7(e)Let $f(x) = x^2\sin(1/x)$ for $x \ne 0$ and $f(0) = 0$. Then f is differentiable at every $x \in \mathbb R$, with $$f'(x) = 2x\sin\frac1x - \cos\frac1x \ (x \ne 0), \qquad f'(0) = 0,$$ but $f'$ has no limit at 0, so $f'$ is not continuous at 0. In plain words: a function can have a derivative everywhere and still have a derivative that wiggles with the same height $\pm1$ no matter how close you get to 0. The tangent exists at 0 (it is the x-axis), but the slopes next to 0 do not settle on 0.
Skeleton(1) For $x \ne 0$: product and chain rule. (2) At 0 use ? (rules do not apply). Quotient $\frac{f(x) - 0}{x} = $ ?; squeeze with $|\cdot| \le$ ?. (3) Two sequences $x_n \to 0$ with $f'(x_n) \to$ ? and ?. ∎
Proof
For $x \ne 0$: $f'(x) = 2x\sin\frac1x + x^2\cos\frac1x\cdot\left(-\frac1{x^2}\right) = 2x\sin\frac1x - \cos\frac1x$.Product rule, with the chain rule for $\sin(1/x)$ (inner $1/x$, derivative $-1/x^2$). Valid because $1/x$ is differentiable at $x \ne 0$.
At 0: $\dfrac{f(x) - f(0)}{x - 0} = x\sin\dfrac1x$.Definition 6.1.1. The rules cannot be used: $\sin(1/x)$ is not even defined at 0 (N2).
$\left|x\sin\frac1x\right| \le |x| \to 0$, so $f'(0) = 0$.$|\sin| \le 1$ and squeeze: for ε take $\delta = \varepsilon$.
Let $x_n = \dfrac1{2n\pi}$: $f'(x_n) = 2x_n\cdot 0 - \cos 2n\pi = -1$. Let $y_n = \dfrac1{(2n + 1)\pi}$: $f'(y_n) = 0 - \cos(2n + 1)\pi = +1$.$\sin(2n\pi) = \sin(2n+1)\pi = 0$; $\cos 2n\pi = 1$; $\cos(2n + 1)\pi = -1$.
$x_n \to 0$, $y_n \to 0$, but $f'(x_n) \to -1$ and $f'(y_n) \to +1$. So $\lim_{x\to0}f'(x)$ does not exist; in particular it is not $f'(0) = 0$. ∎Two sequences with different limits (M07); discontinuity criterion 5.1.4.
Check: n = 1: $x_1 = \frac1{2\pi} = 0.159$, $f'(x_1) = -1$; $y_1 = \frac1{3\pi} = 0.106$, $f'(y_1) = +1$. Also $|f'(x)| \le 2|x| + 1 \le 3$ on $[-1, 1]$: bounded.
What can and cannot be used here
  • rules (product, chain) at $x \ne 0$fine: $1/x$ is differentiable there
  • rules at $x = 0$$\sin(1/x)$ is undefined at 0: you must use the limit of the quotient (N2)
  • "differentiable ⇒ $f'$ continuous"false: this example C6
Result · which powers work (Bartle Ex 6.1.12)$f(x) = x^r\sin(1/x)$, $f(0) = 0$, $r>0$. The quotient at 0 is $x^{r-1}\sin(1/x)$.
$r>1$$|x^{r-1}\sin\frac1x| \le |x|^{r-1} \to 0$: $f'(0) = 0$ exists. $r = 1$quotient $\sin\frac1x$, no limit (two sequences): $f'(0)$ does not exist. $0<r<1$$|x|^{r-1} \to \infty$, unbounded: no derivative.
Worked example 1 · done above; extension $x^2\sin(1/x^2)$ (Ex 6.1.10, C19)

$g(x) = x^2\sin(1/x^2)$, $g(0) = 0$. At 0: quotient $x\sin(1/x^2) \to 0$. For $x \ne 0$: $g'(x) = 2x\sin\frac1{x^2} - \frac2x\cos\frac1{x^2}$.

At $x_n = \dfrac1{\sqrt{2n\pi}}$: $\sin\frac1{x_n^2} = \sin 2n\pi = 0$, $\cos = 1$, so $g'(x_n) = -2\sqrt{2n\pi}$. For $n = 1$: $-2\sqrt{2\pi} = -5.013$; for $n = 8$: $-2\sqrt{16\pi} = -14.18$. Unbounded: $g'$ is not even bounded on $[-1,1]$.

Worked example 2 · $x^3\sin(1/x)$, fill the gaps

$f(0) = 0$. Quotient at 0: $x^2\sin\frac1x \to$ ?. For $x \ne 0$: $f'(x) = 3x^2\sin\frac1x + x^3\cos\frac1x\cdot$ ? $=$ ?. Is $f'$ continuous at 0?

Answer: $f'(0) = 0$; $f'(x) = 3x^2\sin\frac1x - x\cos\frac1x$, and $|f'(x)| \le 3x^2 + |x| \to 0 = f'(0)$. Yes, continuous. So $r = 2$ is the borderline: $f'$ exists but is discontinuous; $r = 3$ is smoother.
