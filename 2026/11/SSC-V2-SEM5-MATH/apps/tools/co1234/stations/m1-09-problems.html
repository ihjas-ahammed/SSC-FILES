Find all points of $\mathbf R^2$ at which all partial derivatives of $f=x_1^4x_2-x_1^2x_2^3$ are zero.

Factor each partial and run through the cases.

$f_1=4x_1^3x_2-2x_1x_2^3=2x_1x_2(2x_1^2-x_2^2)$, $f_2=x_1^4-3x_1^2x_2^2=x_1^2(x_1^2-3x_2^2)$.

Find all points of $\mathbf R^3$ where all partials of $f=x_1x_2x_3$ vanish. Show none is a local maximum or minimum.

A point is not an extremum if every neighbourhood contains values on both sides of $f(\boldsymbol\xi)$.

$f_1=x_2x_3$, $f_2=x_1x_3$, $f_3=x_1x_2$ all vanish iff at least two coordinates are zero: the three coordinate axes. At such a point $f=0$. Take the point $(a,0,0)$ (the other axes are the same by symmetry). If $a\ne0$: $f(a,t,t)=at^2$ and $f(a,t,-t)=-at^2$ have opposite signs for every small $t\ne0$. If $a=0$: $f(t,t,t)=t^3$ changes sign. So no point is a local maximum or minimum.

Explain why $D_{\mathbf u}f(\boldsymbol\xi)=(D_{\mathbf u}f^1(\boldsymbol\xi),\dots,D_{\mathbf u}f^m(\boldsymbol\xi))$.

A vector limit exists iff each coordinate's limit exists, and then it is the vector of those limits.

$\dfrac{f(\boldsymbol\xi+t\mathbf u)-f(\boldsymbol\xi)}{t}=\left(\dfrac{f^1(\boldsymbol\xi+t\mathbf u)-f^1(\boldsymbol\xi)}{t},\dots,\dfrac{f^m(\boldsymbol\xi+t\mathbf u)-f^m(\boldsymbol\xi)}{t}\right)$. Letting $t\to0$ coordinate by coordinate gives the result (a sequence or function in $\mathbf R^m$ converges iff its coordinates do).

Use it to find $D_{\mathbf u}f(0)$ for $f(x_1,x_2)=(x_1+x_2,x_1x_2)$, $\mathbf u=(1,0)$. (Answer: $(1,0)$.)

$f=\dfrac{x_1^2x_2}{x_1^4+x_2^2}$ for $(x_1,x_2)\ne\mathbf 0$, $f(\mathbf 0)=0$. Prove $f$ has directional derivatives in every direction at $\mathbf 0$ but is not continuous there. Does $f$ have a local maximum or minimum at $\mathbf 0$?

Compute the limit along $t\mathbf u$; look along the parabola $x_2=x_1^2$.

For $\mathbf u=(u_1,u_2)$: $\dfrac{f(t\mathbf u)}{t}=\dfrac{t^3u_1^2u_2}{t(t^4u_1^4+t^2u_2^2)}=\dfrac{u_1^2u_2}{t^2u_1^4+u_2^2}\to\dfrac{u_1^2}{u_2}$ if $u_2\ne0$; if $u_2=0$, $f(t\mathbf u)=0$ and the limit is $0$. So every directional derivative exists.
On $x_2=x_1^2$ ($x_1\ne0$): $f=\dfrac{x_1^4}{2x_1^4}=\tfrac12$, which does not tend to $f(\mathbf 0)=0$: not continuous.
On $x_2=-x_1^2$, $f=-\tfrac12$. Every neighbourhood of $\mathbf 0$ has values $\tfrac12$ and $-\tfrac12$, so $\mathbf 0$ is neither a local maximum nor a local minimum.

Is this $f$ differentiable at $\mathbf 0$? (No: differentiable implies continuous, theorem 19.13.)

 Exercise 19.26: derivatives, tangent flats, chain rule

Why is every $f:\mathbf R^n\to\mathbf R^m$ with polynomial coordinates differentiable everywhere? What about rational functions?

Theorem 19.17.

Each partial derivative of a polynomial is a polynomial, hence continuous everywhere; by 19.17 $f$ is differentiable everywhere. For rational coordinates $p/q$ the partials are rational with denominator a power of $q$, continuous wherever $q\ne0$; so $f$ is differentiable at every point where no denominator vanishes.

Explain why each is differentiable at the point and find its derivative and tangent flat: (i) $x_1^2+2x_1x_2+x_2^3$ at $(1,2)$; (ii) $(\cos x_1x_2,\sin x_1x_2)^T$ at $(\pi,1)$; (iii) $(x_1,x_1+x_2,x_1x_2)^T$ at $(1,1)$; (iv) $(x_1x_2^2,x_2^2x_3^3)^T$ at $(1,2,3)$; (v) $(1,x,x^2)^T$ at $0$.

All partials are continuous (19.17); $f'$ is the matrix of partials (19.16); tangent flat $\mathbf y=f(\boldsymbol\xi)+f'(\boldsymbol\xi)(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi)$.

(i) $f'=(2x_1+2x_2,\;2x_1+3x_2^2)$, $f'(1,2)=(6,14)$, $f(1,2)=13$: $y-13=6(x_1-1)+14(x_2-2)$.
(ii) $f'=\begin{pmatrix}-x_2\sin x_1x_2&-x_1\sin x_1x_2\\x_2\cos x_1x_2&x_1\cos x_1x_2\end{pmatrix}$; at $(\pi,1)$, $\sin\pi=0$, $\cos\pi=-1$: $f'=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\-1&-\pi\end{pmatrix}$, $f=(-1,0)$. Tangent flat: $y_1=-1$, $y_2=-(x_1-\pi)-\pi(x_2-1)$.
(iii) $f'=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\x_2&x_1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, $f=(1,2,1)$: $y_1=x_1$, $y_2=x_1+x_2$, $y_3=x_1+x_2-1$.
(iv) $f'=\begin{pmatrix}x_2^2&2x_1x_2&0\\0&2x_2x_3^3&3x_2^2x_3^2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4&4&0\\0&108&108\end{pmatrix}$, $f=(4,108)$: $y_1-4=4(x_1-1)+4(x_2-2)$, $y_2-108=108(x_2-2)+108(x_3-3)$.
(v) $f'(0)=(0,1,0)^T$ (from $(0,1,2x)^T$), $f(0)=(1,0,0)$: the line $y_1=1$, $y_2=x$, $y_3=0$.

For $f$ of 2(i) at $(1,2)$: slope in the direction $(1,-1)$; the direction of greatest slope and its value; a normal to the contour $x_1^2+2x_1x_2+x_2^3=13$; the tangent line.

$\nabla f(1,2)=(6,14)$. Along $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$: $(6-14)/\sqrt2=-4\sqrt2$. Greatest slope along $(6,14)$, i.e. the direction $(3,7)/\sqrt{58}$, value $\sqrt{36+196}=\sqrt{232}=2\sqrt{58}\approx15.2$. Normal: $(6,14)$ (or $(3,7)$). Tangent line: $6(x_1-1)+14(x_2-2)=0$, i.e. $3x_1+7x_2=17$.

Explain when (I), (III), (IV)′ of §19.21 make sense for matrices $L,M$ and column vectors, and prove them.

Compute one entry of each side with theorem 19.16.

(I) $\dfrac{d}{d\mathbf x}(L\mathbf x+\mathbf c)=L$: $L$ is $m\times n$, $\mathbf x$ is $n\times1$, $\mathbf c$ is $m\times1$. The $i$th coordinate is $\sum_jl_{ij}x_j+c_i$, whose partial in $x_k$ is $l_{ik}$, continuous; so the derivative is the matrix $(l_{ik})=L$.
(III) $\dfrac{d}{d\mathbf x}(L\mathbf y+M\mathbf z)=L\dfrac{d\mathbf y}{d\mathbf x}+M\dfrac{d\mathbf z}{d\mathbf x}$: $\mathbf y$ ($p\times1$) and $\mathbf z$ ($q\times1$) differentiable functions of $\mathbf x$, $L$ $m\times p$, $M$ $m\times q$. Entry $(i,k)$: $\partial_k(\sum_jl_{ij}y_j+\sum_jm_{ij}z_j)=\sum_jl_{ij}\partial_ky_j+\sum_jm_{ij}\partial_kz_j$, the $(i,k)$ entry of the right side.
(IV)′ $\dfrac{d}{d\mathbf x}(\mathbf y^T\mathbf z)=\mathbf z^T\dfrac{d\mathbf y}{d\mathbf x}+\mathbf y^T\dfrac{d\mathbf z}{d\mathbf x}$: $\mathbf y,\mathbf z$ both $m\times1$. $\partial_k\sum_iy_iz_i=\sum_iz_i\partial_ky_i+\sum_iy_i\partial_kz_i$, the $k$th entry of the right side (a $1\times n$ row). In each case the right side is continuous when the derivatives are, so 19.17 applies (or argue directly with $o(\mathbf h)$).

$f:\mathbf R\to\mathbf R$ differentiable, $y=\phi(x_1,x_2)=f(x_1x_2)$. Prove $x_1\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial x_1}=x_2\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial x_2}$.

Chain rule with the inner function $u=x_1x_2$.

$\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial x_1}=f'(x_1x_2)x_2$, $\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial x_2}=f'(x_1x_2)x_1$. So $x_1\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial x_1}=x_1x_2f'(x_1x_2)=x_2\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial x_2}$. (The identity holds at every point; the book's condition $f'(x_1x_2)\ne0$ is not needed.)

$y=f(x_1/x_2)$: show $x_1y_1+x_2y_2=0$.

$y=f(x_1,x_2)$, $x_1=r\cos\theta$, $x_2=r\sin\theta$. Express $\partial y/\partial x_2$ in terms of $\partial y/\partial r$ and $\partial y/\partial\theta$.

Chain rule for $y_r$ and $y_\theta$, then solve two linear equations.

$y_r=\cos\theta\,y_{x_1}+\sin\theta\,y_{x_2}$, $y_\theta=-r\sin\theta\,y_{x_1}+r\cos\theta\,y_{x_2}$. Multiply the first by $\sin\theta$, the second by $\cos\theta/r$, and add: $$\frac{\partial y}{\partial x_2}=\sin\theta\frac{\partial y}{\partial r}+\frac{\cos\theta}{r}\frac{\partial y}{\partial\theta}\qquad(r\ne0).$$

 Exercise 19.47: second derivatives and classification

$f(\mathbf x)=(A\mathbf x+\mathbf a)^T(B\mathbf x+\mathbf b)$, $A,B$ $m\times n$, $\mathbf a,\mathbf b$ $m\times1$ ($\mathbf x$ is $n\times1$; the book misprints "$m\times1$"). Find linear equations satisfied by its local maxima and minima.

(IV)′ and (I), as in example 19.30.

$f'(\mathbf x)=(B\mathbf x+\mathbf b)^TA+(A\mathbf x+\mathbf a)^TB$. Setting it to zero and transposing: $A^T(B\mathbf x+\mathbf b)+B^T(A\mathbf x+\mathbf a)=\mathbf 0$, i.e. $$(A^TB+B^TA)\mathbf x=-(A^T\mathbf b+B^T\mathbf a).$$ With $A=B$, $\mathbf a=\mathbf b=-\mathbf b'$ this is the normal equations.

Show $f=x_1e^{-x_2}$ is twice differentiable everywhere and find $f''$.

$f_1=e^{-x_2}$, $f_2=-x_1e^{-x_2}$; $f_{11}=0$, $f_{12}=f_{21}=-e^{-x_2}$, $f_{22}=x_1e^{-x_2}$. All continuous, so $f$ is twice differentiable (19.17 applied to $f'^T$): $$f''=\begin{pmatrix}0&-e^{-x_2}\\-e^{-x_2}&x_1e^{-x_2}\end{pmatrix}.$$ (It has no stationary point: $f_1\ne0$.)

$f=\dfrac{x_1^3x_2}{x_1^2+x_2^2}$, $f(\mathbf 0)=0$. Show $f'$ is continuous at $\mathbf 0$, all second partials exist at $\mathbf 0$, and $f_{12}(0,0)\ne f_{21}(0,0)$. Why is proposition 19.33 not contradicted?

Compute at $\mathbf 0$ from the definition; away from $\mathbf 0$ by the quotient rule. $f_{ij}=\partial_i(f_j)$.

At $\mathbf 0$: $f(h,0)=f(0,k)=0$, so $f_1(\mathbf 0)=f_2(\mathbf 0)=0$. Away from $\mathbf 0$ ($r^2=x_1^2+x_2^2$): $$f_1=\frac{x_1^4x_2+3x_1^2x_2^3}{r^4},\qquad f_2=\frac{x_1^5-x_1^3x_2^2}{r^4}.$$ $|f_1|\le4r^5/r^4=4r\to0$ and $|f_2|\le2r\to0$: $f'$ is continuous at $\mathbf 0$.
Second partials at $\mathbf 0$: $f_1(h,0)=0$, $f_1(0,k)=0$, $f_2(h,0)=h$, $f_2(0,k)=0$. So $f_{11}(\mathbf 0)=0$, $f_{21}(\mathbf 0)=\partial_2f_1=\lim f_1(0,k)/k=0$, $f_{12}(\mathbf 0)=\partial_1f_2=\lim f_2(h,0)/h=1$, $f_{22}(\mathbf 0)=0$. Hence $f_{12}(\mathbf 0)=1\ne0=f_{21}(\mathbf 0)$.
19.33 assumes the second partials are continuous at $\mathbf 0$; they are not here (e.g. $f_{12}$ along $x_2=0$ is $1$ but along $x_1=0$ it is $0$), so there is no contradiction.

$\lambda$ an eigenvalue of a real symmetric $M$. Prove $\lambda^2\le\sum_i\sum_jm_{ij}^2$.

A unit eigenvector $\mathbf u$ with $M\mathbf u=\lambda\mathbf u$ (§19.37), and Cauchy–Schwarz.

$\lambda^2=\|\lambda\mathbf u\|^2=\|M\mathbf u\|^2=\sum_i\left(\sum_jm_{ij}u_j\right)^2\le\sum_i\left(\sum_jm_{ij}^2\right)\left(\sum_ju_j^2\right)=\sum_i\sum_jm_{ij}^2.$ (Alternatively, $\sum\lambda_k^2=\tr(M^2)=\sum_{i,j}m_{ij}^2$.) This is the inequality $|\mathbf h^TL\mathbf h|\le(\sum l_{ij}^2)^{1/2}\|\mathbf h\|^2$ of §19.40 applied to $\mathbf h=\mathbf u$.
