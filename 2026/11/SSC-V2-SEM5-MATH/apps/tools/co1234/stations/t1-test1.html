$C$ is a convex cone if $\theta_1\mathbf x_1+\theta_2\mathbf x_2\in C$ for all $\mathbf x_1,\mathbf x_2\in C$, $\theta_1,\theta_2\ge0$. The union of the two half-axes $\{(t,0)\}\cup\{(0,t)\}$, $t\ge0$, is a cone, but $(1,0)+(0,1)$ is not in it.

$\mathbf x,\mathbf y\in C\cap D$, $0\le\theta\le1$: $\theta\mathbf x+(1-\theta)\mathbf y$ lies in $C$ and in $D$ (both convex), so in $C\cap D$.

$f$ is quasiconvex if $\dom f$ and all sublevel sets $\{f\le\alpha\}$ are convex; equivalently $f(\theta\mathbf x+(1-\theta)\mathbf y)\le\max\{f(\mathbf x),f(\mathbf y)\}$. Example: $\log x$ on $\mathbf R_{++}$ (or $\sqrt{|x|}$), quasiconvex but not convex.

Statement: disjoint convex $C$, $D$ ⇒ $\exists\mathbf a\ne0$, $b$: $\mathbf a^T\mathbf x\le b$ on $C$, $\ge b$ on $D$. Proof: closest pair $\mathbf c,\mathbf d$; $\mathbf a=\mathbf d-\mathbf c$, $b=\tfrac12(\|\mathbf d\|^2-\|\mathbf c\|^2)$; $f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf a^T\mathbf x-b=(\mathbf d-\mathbf c)^T(\mathbf x-\tfrac12(\mathbf d+\mathbf c))$. If $\mathbf u\in D$ had $f(\mathbf u)\lt0$ then $(\mathbf d-\mathbf c)^T(\mathbf u-\mathbf d)\lt0$, so $\frac{d}{dt}\|\mathbf d+t(\mathbf u-\mathbf d)-\mathbf c\|^2\big|_0\lt0$: a point of $D$ (convexity) closer to $\mathbf c$ than $\mathbf d$, contradiction. Same for $C$. Mention: converse false; strict separation needs extra conditions; supporting hyperplane theorem follows. (Derivation in §2.5.)
