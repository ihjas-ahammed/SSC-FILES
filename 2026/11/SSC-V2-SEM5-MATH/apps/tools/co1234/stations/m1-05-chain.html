The derivative of a composite is the product of the derivative matrices: $(f\circ g)'(\boldsymbol\xi)=f'(g(\boldsymbol\xi))\,g'(\boldsymbol\xi)$. Written entry by entry this is the familiar chain rule for partial derivatives. For an inverse function the derivative is the inverse matrix, which needs a non-zero Jacobian $\det f'(\boldsymbol\xi)$. Proposition 19.24 (chain rule)

If $g$ is differentiable at $\boldsymbol\xi$ and $f$ is differentiable at $\boldsymbol\eta=g(\boldsymbol\xi)$, then $f\circ g$ is differentiable at $\boldsymbol\xi$ and $$(f\circ g)'(\boldsymbol\xi)=f'(\boldsymbol\eta)\,g'(\boldsymbol\xi),\qquad\text{i.e.}\quad\frac{d\mathbf z}{d\mathbf x}=\frac{d\mathbf z}{d\mathbf y}\frac{d\mathbf y}{d\mathbf x}.$$

Proof sketch (as in the book). We have $f(\mathbf y)-f(\boldsymbol\eta)-f'(\boldsymbol\eta)(\mathbf y-\boldsymbol\eta)=o(\mathbf y-\boldsymbol\eta)$ and $g(\mathbf x)-g(\boldsymbol\xi)-g'(\boldsymbol\xi)(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi)=o(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi)$. Put $\mathbf y=g(\mathbf x)$ in the first and replace $g(\mathbf x)-g(\boldsymbol\xi)$ by $g'(\boldsymbol\xi)(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi)+o(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi)$:

$$f(g(\mathbf x))-f(g(\boldsymbol\xi))-f'(\boldsymbol\eta)\{g'(\boldsymbol\xi)(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi)+o(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi)\}=o\{g'(\boldsymbol\xi)(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi)+o(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi)\},$$

so $f(g(\mathbf x))-f(g(\boldsymbol\xi))-f'(\boldsymbol\eta)g'(\boldsymbol\xi)(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi)=o(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi)$. (The last step, that $o$ of something of order $\|\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi\|$ is $o(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi)$, is the part the book does not fully justify.)

Comparing entries of $\dfrac{d\mathbf z}{d\mathbf x}=\dfrac{d\mathbf z}{d\mathbf y}\dfrac{d\mathbf y}{d\mathbf x}$ ($m\times n=(m\times p)(p\times n)$):

$$\frac{\partial z_i}{\partial x_j}=\frac{\partial z_i}{\partial y_1}\frac{\partial y_1}{\partial x_j}+\frac{\partial z_i}{\partial y_2}\frac{\partial y_2}{\partial x_j}+\dots+\frac{\partial z_i}{\partial y_p}\frac{\partial y_p}{\partial x_j}.$$

 Inverse functions and the Jacobian

If $f$ has an inverse and both are differentiable, differentiating $f\circ f^{-1}(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x$ by the chain rule gives $f'(\boldsymbol\xi)(f^{-1})'(\boldsymbol\eta)=I$, i.e. formula (VI): $\dfrac{d\mathbf y}{d\mathbf x}=\left(\dfrac{d\mathbf x}{d\mathbf y}\right)^{-1}$.

The Jacobian of $f:\mathbf R^n\to\mathbf R^n$ at $\boldsymbol\xi$ is $\det f'(\boldsymbol\xi)$. If the first partials are continuous at $\boldsymbol\xi$ and $\det f'(\boldsymbol\xi)\ne0$, a suitable differentiable inverse exists (the inverse function theorem, quoted without proof). This is the vector form of "$f'(\xi)\ne0$".

 Polar coordinates $x_1=r\cos\theta$, $x_2=r\sin\theta$. Find the Jacobian $\partial(x_1,x_2)/\partial(r,\theta)$. Where is the inverse not guaranteed?

$\begin{vmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{vmatrix}=r$. Non-zero except at $r=0$, the origin.
