Two systems are weak alternatives if at most one is feasible, strong alternatives if exactly one is. Duality produces them: the dual of a feasibility problem either certifies infeasibility or does not. Farkas' lemma is the famous linear case. Weak alternatives via the dual function (§5.8.1)

Feasibility problem: find $\mathbf x$ with $f_i(\mathbf x)\le0$, $h_i(\mathbf x)=0$ (objective $0$, so $p^\star\in\{0,\infty\}$). Its dual function $g(\boldsymbol\lambda,\boldsymbol\nu)=\inf_{\mathbf x}\big(\sum\lambda_if_i+\sum\nu_ih_i\big)$ is positively homogeneous, so $d^\star\in\{0,\infty\}$.

Weak alternatives: at most one of these is feasible: $$\text{(I)}\ f_i(\mathbf x)\le0,\ h_i(\mathbf x)=0\qquad\text{(II)}\ \boldsymbol\lambda\succeq0,\ g(\boldsymbol\lambda,\boldsymbol\nu)\gt0.$$ (If (II) is feasible, $d^\star=\infty$, so $p^\star=\infty$ by weak duality: (I) infeasible.) Strict version: (I′) $f_i(\mathbf x)\lt0$, $h_i(\mathbf x)=0$ and (II′) $\boldsymbol\lambda\succeq0$, $\boldsymbol\lambda\ne0$, $g(\boldsymbol\lambda,\boldsymbol\nu)\ge0$ are weak alternatives.

 Strong alternatives (§5.8.2)

If the $f_i$ are convex, the $h_i$ affine ($A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$) and a mild condition holds (e.g. there is $\mathbf x\in\relint\mathcal D$ with $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$), then: strict inequalities (I′) $f_i(\mathbf x)\lt0$, $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$ and (II′) $\boldsymbol\lambda\succeq0$, $\boldsymbol\lambda\ne0$, $g\ge0$ are strong alternatives; for nonstrict inequalities (I) and (II) are strong alternatives if moreover the optimum of the associated problem (minimise $s$ s.t. $f_i(\mathbf x)-s\le0$, $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$) is attained.

Proof sketch for the strict case: consider minimise $s$ s.t. $f_i(\mathbf x)-s\le0$, $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$. It is strictly feasible (take $s$ large), so strong duality holds; (I′) is feasible iff $p^\star\lt0$; the dual is maximise $g(\boldsymbol\lambda,\boldsymbol\nu)$ s.t. $\boldsymbol\lambda\succeq0$, $\mathbf 1^T\boldsymbol\lambda=1$, and $d^\star=p^\star\ge0$ iff (II′) is feasible.

Examples (§5.8.3)

Linear inequalities $A\mathbf x\preceq\mathbf b$: $g(\boldsymbol\lambda)=-\mathbf b^T\boldsymbol\lambda$ if $A^T\boldsymbol\lambda=0$, $-\infty$ otherwise. Strong alternatives: $A\mathbf x\preceq\mathbf b$ versus $\boldsymbol\lambda\succeq0$, $A^T\boldsymbol\lambda=0$, $\mathbf b^T\boldsymbol\lambda\lt0$. Strict: $A\mathbf x\prec\mathbf b$ versus $\boldsymbol\lambda\succeq0$, $\boldsymbol\lambda\ne0$, $A^T\boldsymbol\lambda=0$, $\mathbf b^T\boldsymbol\lambda\le0$ (example 2.21 again).

Intersection of ellipsoids. $f_i(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^TA_i\mathbf x+2\mathbf b_i^T\mathbf x+c_i\lt0$ ($A_i\succ0$) has no solution iff there is $\boldsymbol\lambda\succeq0$, $\boldsymbol\lambda\ne0$ with $-\mathbf b(\boldsymbol\lambda)^TA(\boldsymbol\lambda)^{-1}\mathbf b(\boldsymbol\lambda)+c(\boldsymbol\lambda)\ge0$, where $A(\boldsymbol\lambda)=\sum\lambda_iA_i$ etc.

Farkas' lemma. Exactly one of these is feasible: $$A\mathbf x\preceq0,\ \mathbf c^T\mathbf x\lt0\qquad\text{or}\qquad A^T\mathbf y+\mathbf c=0,\ \mathbf y\succeq0.$$ Equivalent form: $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$, $\mathbf x\succeq0$ is solvable, or $A^T\mathbf y\succeq0$, $\mathbf b^T\mathbf y\lt0$ is solvable, never both.

 Either $\mathbf b$ is a nonnegative combination of the columns of $A$, or a hyperplane through $\mathbf 0$ (normal $\mathbf y$) has all columns on one side and $\mathbf b$ strictly on the other.

Example 5.10 (arbitrage-free bounds on price). $n$ assets with prices $\mathbf p$ and payoffs $V_{ij}$ in scenario $i$. An arbitrage is $\mathbf x$ with $\mathbf p^T\mathbf x\lt0$ and $V\mathbf x\succeq0$. By Farkas, there is no arbitrage iff $\mathbf p=V^T\mathbf y$ for some $\mathbf y\succeq0$ (state prices). So the arbitrage-free range of the price of a new asset is found by two LPs over $\mathbf y$.

Show $\{\mathbf x\succeq0,\ x_1+x_2=-1\}$ is empty using Farkas.

$A=(1\ 1)$, $b=-1$. Take $y=1$: $A^Ty=(1,1)\succeq0$ and $by=-1\lt0$. So $A\mathbf x=b$, $\mathbf x\succeq0$ has no solution.

 Prove that the two Farkas systems cannot both be feasible.

If $A\mathbf x\preceq0$, $\mathbf c^T\mathbf x\lt0$ and $A^T\mathbf y+\mathbf c=0$, $\mathbf y\succeq0$, then $0\gt\mathbf c^T\mathbf x=-\mathbf y^TA\mathbf x\ge0$ (since $\mathbf y\succeq0$, $A\mathbf x\preceq0$): contradiction.
