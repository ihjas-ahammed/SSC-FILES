VOICE / STYLE:
Calm, friendly physics teacher explaining a nanoscience idea to undergraduate students.
SLOW, unhurried teaching pace, noticeably slower than a social-media reel. Clear diction, natural, not robotic.
Pause about half a second at each line break and a full second at blank lines, so students can follow the shapes on screen.
Say "three D" for 3D. Light emphasis on key words, never shouting.
No music, no sound effects, dry studio recording.
Target total length: about 74–85 seconds.
Read the script exactly as written. Do not add an intro, outro or sign-off.

SCRIPT:
A nanomaterial needs only one tiny dimension.
So which ones are tiny?

Count the directions an electron can move freely.
Squeeze a side below a hundred nanometers:
that side is confined.

Squeeze all three:
a nanodot.
No free direction:
zero-dimensional.
Its energy levels turn discrete, like an atom’s.

Squeeze two sides:
a nanowire or nanotube.
Free along its length:
one-dimensional.

Squeeze only the thickness:
a nanosheet, like graphene.
How thin is thin?
Nanometres thick, free across the plane:
two-dimensional.

Pack nanoparticles together into a bulk solid:
a three D nanomaterial.
Even a heap of powder keeps nano properties, because each grain is still confined.

Quick recap:
dot, wire, sheet, solid, thickness, powder.

Fewer free directions, more quantum.
