VOICE / STYLE:
Calm, friendly physics teacher explaining a nanoscience idea to undergraduate students.
SLOW, unhurried teaching pace, noticeably slower than a social-media reel. Clear diction, natural, not robotic.
Pause about half a second at each line break and a full second at blank lines, so students can follow the animation.
Light emphasis on key words, never shouting.
No music, no sound effects, dry studio recording.
Target total length: about 67–77 seconds.
Read the script exactly as written. Do not add an intro, outro or sign-off.

SCRIPT:
Big machines lose energy to friction.
At the nanoscale, friction can vanish.

Roll a graphene sheet into a tube.
Slide one tube inside another.
The inner tube glides almost frictionless.

Why? The walls have no dangling bonds.
Atoms line up perfectly, so no fluid fits between:
the gap is vacuum.
No fluid, no drag.

Nature does it too.
A bacterium’s flagella motor spins on a molecular bearing, all its life.

Now build a nanocar.
Load it with a drug, steer it to a tumour, and release it there.
Healthy cells are spared:
a gentler path than chemo.

Quick recap:
sheet, tube, nested, vacuum, motor, nanocar.

No friction, new machines.
