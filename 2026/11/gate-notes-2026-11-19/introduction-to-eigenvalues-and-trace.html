Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

6.1 Introduction to Eigenvalues

Linear equations $Ax = b$ come from steady-state problems. Eigenvalues are most important in dynamic problems. The solution of $du/dt = Au$ changes with time. It can grow, decay, or oscillate. We cannot find this solution by elimination. This chapter starts a new part of linear algebra based on $Ax = \lambda x$. All matrices in this chapter are square.

A good model uses the powers $A, A^2, A^3, \dots$ of a matrix. Suppose you need the hundredth power $A^{100}$. After a few steps, you cannot recognize the starting matrix $A$. The matrix $A^{100}$ is very close to $\begin{bmatrix} .6 & .6 \\ .4 & .4 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} .8 & .3 \\ .2 & .7 \end{bmatrix}$$
$$A^2 = \begin{bmatrix} .70 & .45 \\ .30 & .55 \end{bmatrix}$$
$$A^3 = \begin{bmatrix} .650 & .525 \\ .350 & .475 \end{bmatrix}$$
$$A^{100} \approx \begin{bmatrix} .6000 & .6000 \\ .4000 & .4000 \end{bmatrix}$$

We found $A^{100}$ with the eigenvalues of $A$. We did not multiply $100$ matrices. These eigenvalues (here they are $1$ and $1/2$) show the core behavior of a matrix.

To explain eigenvalues, we first explain eigenvectors. Almost all vectors change direction when $A$ multiplies them. Certain special vectors $x$ stay in the same direction as $Ax$. Those vectors are the eigenvectors. Multiply an eigenvector by $A$, and the vector $Ax$ is a number $\lambda$ times the original $x$.

The Basic Equation

The basic equation is $Ax = \lambda x$. The number $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$.

The eigenvalue $\lambda$ tells if the special vector $x$ stretches, shrinks, reverses, or stays unchanged when $A$ multiplies it. We can find $\lambda = 2$, or $\frac{1}{2}$, or $-1$, or $1$. The eigenvalue $\lambda$ can also be zero! Then $Ax = 0x$ means that this eigenvector $x$ is in the nullspace.

If $A$ is the identity matrix $I$, every vector has $Ax = x$. All vectors are eigenvectors of $I$. All eigenvalues $\lambda$ ("lambda") are $\lambda = 1$. This is very unusual. Most $2 \times 2$ matrices have two eigenvector directions and two eigenvalues. We will show that $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.

This section explains how to compute the $x$'s and $\lambda$'s. It can come early in the course because we only need the determinant of a $2 \times 2$ matrix. First, we use $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ to find the eigenvalues in Example 1. Later, we derive this equation in equation (3).

Example 1

The matrix $A$ has two eigenvalues $\lambda = 1$ and $\lambda = 1/2$. Look at $\det(A - \lambda I)$:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} .8 & .3 \\ .2 & .7 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \det\begin{bmatrix} .8 - \lambda & .3 \\ .2 & .7 - \lambda \end{bmatrix} = \lambda^2 - \frac{3}{2}\lambda + \frac{1}{2} = (\lambda - 1)\left(\lambda - \frac{1}{2}\right).$$

Factor the quadratic into $(\lambda - 1)$ times $(\lambda - \frac{1}{2})$. This shows the two eigenvalues $\lambda = 1$ and $\lambda = \frac{1}{2}$. For those numbers, the matrix $A - \lambda I$ becomes singular (zero determinant). The eigenvectors $x_1$ and $x_2$ are in the nullspaces of $A - I$ and $A - \frac{1}{2}I$:

  • $(A - I)x_1 = 0$ is $Ax_1 = x_1$. The first eigenvector is $(.6, .4)$.
  • $(A - \frac{1}{2}I)x_2 = 0$ is $Ax_2 = \frac{1}{2}x_2$. The second eigenvector is $(1, -1)$.
$$x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} .6 \\ .4 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad Ax_1 = \begin{bmatrix} .8 & .3 \\ .2 & .7 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} .6 \\ .4 \end{bmatrix} = x_1 \quad (Ax = x \text{ means } \lambda_1 = 1)$$ $$x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad Ax_2 = \begin{bmatrix} .8 & .3 \\ .2 & .7 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} .5 \\ -.5 \end{bmatrix} \quad \left(\text{this is } \frac{1}{2}x_2, \text{ so } \lambda_2 = \frac{1}{2}\right).$$

If $A$ multiplies $x_1$ again, we still get $x_1$. Every power of $A$ gives $A^n x_1 = x_1$. When $A$ multiplies $x_2$, it gives $\frac{1}{2}x_2$. If we multiply again, we get $(\frac{1}{2})^2 x_2$.

Powers of $A$

When $A$ is squared, the eigenvectors stay the same. The eigenvalues are squared.

This pattern continues because the eigenvectors stay in their own directions (Figure 6.1) and never mix. The eigenvectors of $A^{100}$ are the same $x_1$ and $x_2$. The eigenvalues of $A^{100}$ are $1^{100} = 1$ and $(\frac{1}{2})^{100}$ (a very small number).

Other vectors do change direction. But all other vectors are combinations of the two eigenvectors. The first column of $A$ is the combination $x_1 + (.2)x_2$:

$$\textbf{Separate into eigenvectors} \qquad \begin{bmatrix} .8 \\ .2 \end{bmatrix} = x_1 + (.2)x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} .6 \\ .4 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} .2 \\ -.2 \end{bmatrix}.$$
(1)

Multiply by $A$ to get $(.7, .3)$, which is the first column of $A^2$. Do this separately for $x_1$ and $(.2)x_2$. Of course $Ax_1 = x_1$. And $A$ multiplies $x_2$ by its eigenvalue $\frac{1}{2}$:

$$\textbf{Multiply each } x_i \textbf{ by } \lambda_i \qquad A\begin{bmatrix} .8 \\ .2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} .7 \\ .3 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{is} \quad x_1 + \frac{1}{2}(.2)x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} .6 \\ .4 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} .1 \\ -.1 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Each eigenvector is multiplied by its eigenvalue when we multiply by $A$. We did not need these eigenvectors to find $A^2$. But eigenvectors are a good way to do $99$ multiplications. At every step, $x_1$ does not change, and $x_2$ is multiplied by $(\frac{1}{2})$. After $99$ steps, we have $(\frac{1}{2})^{99}$:

$$A^{99}\begin{bmatrix} .8 \\ .2 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{is really} \quad x_1 + (.2)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{99} x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} .6 \\ .4 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} \text{very} \\ \text{small} \\ \text{vector} \end{bmatrix}.$$

This is the first column of $A^{100}$. The number we first wrote as $.6000$ was not exact. We left out $(.2)(\frac{1}{2})^{99}$, which does not show up for $30$ decimal places.

The eigenvector $x_1$ is a "steady state" that does not change (because $\lambda_1 = 1$). The eigenvector $x_2$ is a "decaying mode" that almost disappears (because $\lambda_2 = .5$). Higher powers of $A$ bring its columns closer to the steady state.

Note that this $A$ is a Markov matrix. Its entries are positive, and every column adds to $1$. These facts guarantee that the largest eigenvalue is $\lambda = 1$. Its eigenvector $x_1 = (.6, .4)$ is the steady state—all columns of $A^k$ approach this vector. Section 8.3 shows how Markov matrices work in applications like Google.

For projections, we can see the steady state ($\lambda = 1$) and the nullspace ($\lambda = 0$).

Example 2

The projection matrix $P = \begin{bmatrix} .5 & .5 \\ .5 & .5 \end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $\lambda = 1$ and $\lambda = 0$.

Its eigenvectors are $x_1 = (1, 1)$ and $x_2 = (1, -1)$. For these vectors, $Px_1 = x_1$ (steady state) and $Px_2 = 0$ (nullspace). This example shows Markov matrices, singular matrices, and (most important) symmetric matrices. All three have special $\lambda$'s and $x$'s:

  1. Each column of $P = \begin{bmatrix} .5 & .5 \\ .5 & .5 \end{bmatrix}$ adds to $1$, so $\lambda = 1$ is an eigenvalue.
  2. $P$ is singular, so $\lambda = 0$ is an eigenvalue.
  3. $P$ is symmetric, so its eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ are perpendicular.

The only eigenvalues of a projection matrix are $0$ and $1$. The eigenvectors for $\lambda = 0$ ($Px = 0x$) fill the nullspace. The eigenvectors for $\lambda = 1$ ($Px = x$) fill the column space. The projection sends the nullspace to zero. It projects the column space onto itself. It keeps the column space and removes the nullspace:

$$\textbf{Project each part} \qquad v = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \quad \textbf{projects onto} \quad Pv = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Special properties of a matrix give special eigenvalues and eigenvectors. That is a main idea of this chapter (and it is shown in a table at the end of the chapter).

Projections have $\lambda = 0$ and $1$. Permutations have all $|\lambda| = 1$. The next matrix $R$ is both a reflection and a permutation.

Example 3

The reflection matrix $R = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $1$ and $-1$.

$R$ leaves the eigenvector $(1, 1)$ unchanged. The second eigenvector is $(1, -1)$—$R$ reverses its signs. A matrix with no negative entries can still have a negative eigenvalue!

The eigenvectors for $R$ are the same as for $P$, because $\text{reflection} = 2(\text{projection}) - I$:

$$R = 2P - I \qquad \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = 2\begin{bmatrix} .5 & .5 \\ .5 & .5 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$
(2)

Here is the main point. If $Px = \lambda x$, then $2Px = 2\lambda x$. Doubling the matrix doubles the eigenvalues. Now subtract $Ix = x$. The result is $(2P - I)x = (2\lambda - 1)x$. When you shift a matrix by $I$, each $\lambda$ shifts by $1$. The eigenvectors do not change.

Key idea: The eigenvalues of $R$ and $P$ have the same relation as the matrices:

  • The eigenvalues of $R = 2P - I$ are $2(1) - 1 = 1$ and $2(0) - 1 = -1$.
  • The eigenvalues of $R^2$ are $\lambda^2$. Here $R^2 = I$. Check: $(1)^2 = 1$ and $(-1)^2 = 1$.

The Equation for the Eigenvalues

For projections and reflections, we found $\lambda$'s and $x$'s by geometry: $Px = x$, $Px = 0$, $Rx = -x$. Now we use determinants and linear algebra. This is the main calculation in the chapter—almost every application starts by solving $Ax = \lambda x$.

First, move $\lambda x$ to the left side. Write $Ax = \lambda x$ as $(A - \lambda I)x = 0$. The matrix $A - \lambda I$ times the eigenvector $x$ gives the zero vector. The eigenvectors form the nullspace of $A - \lambda I$. When we know an eigenvalue $\lambda$, we find an eigenvector by solving $(A - \lambda I)x = 0$.

Find the eigenvalues first. If $(A - \lambda I)x = 0$ has a nonzero solution, $A - \lambda I$ is not invertible. The determinant of $A - \lambda I$ must be zero. This tells us how to find an eigenvalue $\lambda$:

Eigenvalues and the Characteristic Equation

Eigenvalues: The number $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ if and only if $A - \lambda I$ is singular.

$$\textbf{Equation for the eigenvalues} \qquad \det(A - \lambda I) = 0$$
(3)

This "characteristic polynomial" $\det(A - \lambda I)$ contains only $\lambda$, not $x$. When $A$ is $n \times n$, equation (3) has degree $n$. Then $A$ has $n$ eigenvalues (repeats are possible!). Each $\lambda$ gives an $x$:

For each eigenvalue $\lambda$, solve $(A - \lambda I)x = 0$ or $Ax = \lambda x$ to find an eigenvector $x$.

Example 4

$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ is already singular (zero determinant). Find its $\lambda$'s and $x$'s.

When $A$ is singular, $\lambda = 0$ is one of the eigenvalues. The equation $Ax = 0x$ has solutions. These solutions are the eigenvectors for $\lambda = 0$. But $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ finds all $\lambda$'s and $x$'s. Always subtract $\lambda I$ from $A$:

$$\textbf{Subtract } \lambda \textbf{ from the diagonal to find} \quad A - \lambda I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - \lambda & 2 \\ 2 & 4 - \lambda \end{bmatrix}.$$
(4)

Take the determinant "$ad - bc$" of this $2 \times 2$ matrix. From $1 - \lambda$ times $4 - \lambda$, the "$ad$" part is $\lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 4$. The "$bc$" part (without $\lambda$) is $2$ times $2$:

$$\det\begin{bmatrix} 1 - \lambda & 2 \\ 2 & 4 - \lambda \end{bmatrix} = (1 - \lambda)(4 - \lambda) - (2)(2) = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda.$$
(5)

Set this determinant $\lambda^2 - 5\lambda$ to zero. One solution is $\lambda = 0$ (as expected, because $A$ is singular). Factor it into $\lambda$ times $\lambda - 5$ to get the other root $\lambda = 5$:

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda = 0 \quad \text{gives the eigenvalues} \quad \lambda_1 = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \lambda_2 = 5.$$

Now find the eigenvectors. Solve $(A - \lambda I)x = 0$ separately for $\lambda_1 = 0$ and $\lambda_2 = 5$:

$$(A - 0I)x = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{gives an eigenvector} \quad \begin{bmatrix} y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{for } \lambda_1 = 0$$ $$(A - 5I)x = \begin{bmatrix} -4 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{gives an eigenvector} \quad \begin{bmatrix} y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{for } \lambda_2 = 5.$$

The matrices $A - 0I$ and $A - 5I$ are singular (because $0$ and $5$ are eigenvalues). The eigenvectors $(2, -1)$ and $(1, 2)$ are in the nullspaces: $(A - \lambda I)x = 0$ is $Ax = \lambda x$.

We must stress this point: There is nothing unusual about $\lambda = 0$. Like any other number, zero can be an eigenvalue or not. If $A$ is singular, zero is an eigenvalue, and its eigenvectors fill the nullspace: $Ax = 0x = 0$. If $A$ is invertible, zero is not an eigenvalue. We shift $A$ by a multiple of $I$ to make it singular. In Example 4, the shifted matrix $A - 5I$ is singular, so $5$ is the other eigenvalue.

Summary: How to Solve the Eigenvalue Problem for an $n \times n$ Matrix
  1. Compute the determinant of $A - \lambda I$. Because $\lambda$ is subtracted along the diagonal, this determinant starts with $\lambda^n$ or $-\lambda^n$. It is a polynomial in $\lambda$ of degree $n$.
  2. Find the roots of this polynomial by solving $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$. The $n$ roots are the $n$ eigenvalues of $A$. They make $A - \lambda I$ singular.
  3. For each eigenvalue $\lambda$, solve $(A - \lambda I)x = 0$ to find an eigenvector $x$.

Note on eigenvectors of $2 \times 2$ matrices: When $A - \lambda I$ is singular, both rows are multiples of a vector $(a, b)$. The eigenvector is any multiple of $(b, -a)$. In Example 4, $\lambda = 0$ and $\lambda = 5$ gave:

  • $\lambda = 0$: rows of $A - 0I$ in the direction $(1, 2)$; eigenvector in the direction $(2, -1)$.
  • $\lambda = 5$: rows of $A - 5I$ in the direction $(-4, 2)$; eigenvector in the direction $(2, 4)$.

Earlier we wrote that last eigenvector as $(1, 2)$. Both $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 4)$ are correct. There is a whole line of eigenvectors—any nonzero multiple of $x$ is valid. MATLAB's eig(A) divides by the vector length to make the eigenvector a unit vector.

Warning: Missing Eigenvectors

Some $2 \times 2$ matrices have only one line of eigenvectors. This happens only when two eigenvalues are equal. (On the other hand, $A = I$ has equal eigenvalues and many eigenvectors.) Similarly, some $n \times n$ matrices do not have $n$ independent eigenvectors. Without $n$ eigenvectors, we do not have a basis. We cannot write every $v$ as a combination of eigenvectors. In the terms of Section 6.2, we cannot diagonalize a matrix without $n$ independent eigenvectors.

Good News, Bad News

Bad News First

If you add a row of $A$ to another row, or exchange rows, the eigenvalues usually change. Elimination does not keep the $\lambda$'s. The triangular $U$ has its eigenvalues on its diagonal—they are the pivots. But they are not the eigenvalues of $A$! Eigenvalues change when row 1 is added to row 2:

$$U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \text{ has } \lambda = 0 \text{ and } \lambda = 1; \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \text{ has } \lambda = 0 \text{ and } \lambda = 7.$$
Good News Second

You can find the product $\lambda_1 \lambda_2$ and the sum $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2$ quickly from the matrix. For $A$ above, the product is $0 \cdot 7 = 0$. This equals the determinant (which is $0$). The sum of eigenvalues is $0 + 7 = 7$. This equals the sum down the main diagonal (the trace is $1 + 6$). These quick checks always work:

  • The product of the $n$ eigenvalues equals the determinant.
  • The sum of the $n$ eigenvalues equals the sum of the $n$ diagonal entries.

The sum of the entries on the main diagonal is called the trace of $A$:

$$\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \cdots + \lambda_n = \textbf{trace} = a_{11} + a_{22} + \cdots + a_{nn}.$$
(6)

These checks are very helpful. Problems 16–17 and Section 6.2 prove them. They do not remove the work of computing $\lambda$'s. But when a calculation is wrong, these checks usually show the error. To find the exact $\lambda$'s, use $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.

The determinant check makes the product of the $\lambda$'s equal to the product of the pivots (with no row exchanges). But the sum of the $\lambda$'s is not the sum of the pivots—as the example showed. The individual $\lambda$'s have almost no link to the pivots. In this part of linear algebra, the key equation is nonlinear: $\lambda$ multiplies $x$.

Question to check: Why do the eigenvalues of a triangular matrix lie on its diagonal?

Imaginary Eigenvalues

One more fact (and it is manageable): eigenvalues are not always real numbers.

Example 5

The $90^\circ$ rotation $Q = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ has no real eigenvectors. Its eigenvalues are $\lambda = i$ and $\lambda = -i$. Sum of $\lambda$'s $=$ trace $= 0$. Product $=$ determinant $= 1$.

After a rotation, no vector $Qx$ stays in the same direction as $x$ (except $x = 0$, which is not useful). There is no real eigenvector unless we use imaginary numbers—and we do.

To see how $i$ helps, look at $Q^2$, which is $-I$. If $Q$ rotates by $90^\circ$, then $Q^2$ rotates by $180^\circ$. Its eigenvalues are $-1$ and $-1$ (because $-Ix = -1x$). Squaring $Q$ squares each $\lambda$, so $\lambda^2 = -1$. The eigenvalues of the $90^\circ$ rotation matrix $Q$ are $+i$ and $-i$, because $i^2 = -1$.

These $\lambda$'s also come from $\det(Q - \lambda I) = 0$. This equation gives $\lambda^2 + 1 = 0$, with roots $i$ and $-i$. The imaginary number $i$ also appears in the eigenvectors:

$$\textbf{Complex eigenvectors} \qquad \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{bmatrix} = -i\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = i\begin{bmatrix} i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$

These complex vectors $x_1 = (1, i)$ and $x_2 = (i, 1)$ keep their direction during rotation. This example shows an important fact: real matrices can easily have complex eigenvalues and eigenvectors. The eigenvalues $i$ and $-i$ also show two special properties of $Q$:

  1. $Q$ is an orthogonal matrix, so the absolute value of each $\lambda$ is $|\lambda| = 1$.
  2. $Q$ is a skew-symmetric matrix, so each $\lambda$ is pure imaginary.

A symmetric matrix ($A^T = A$) corresponds to a real number. A skew-symmetric matrix ($A^T = -A$) corresponds to an imaginary number. An orthogonal matrix ($A^T A = I$) corresponds to a complex number with $|\lambda| = 1$. Section 6.4 proves these rules as theorems.

The eigenvectors for all these special matrices are perpendicular. Even $(i, 1)$ and $(1, i)$ are perpendicular (Chapter 10 explains the dot product of complex vectors).

Eigshow in MATLAB

MATLAB has a demo (type eigshow) that shows the eigenvalue problem for a $2 \times 2$ matrix. It starts with the unit vector $x = (1, 0)$. As you move the mouse, $x$ moves around the unit circle. At the same time, the screen shows $Ax$ moving in color. Sometimes $Ax$ is ahead of $x$. Sometimes $Ax$ is behind $x$. Sometimes $Ax$ is parallel to $x$. At that parallel moment, $Ax = \lambda x$ (at $x_1$ and $x_2$ in the second diagram of Figure 6.3).

The eigenvalue $\lambda$ is the length (with sign) of $Ax$ when the unit eigenvector $x$ lines up. The built-in choices for $A$ show three possible cases: $0$, $1$, or $2$ directions where $Ax$ crosses $x$.

  1. Zero real eigenvectors: $Ax$ stays behind or ahead of $x$. The eigenvalues and eigenvectors are complex, as for the rotation matrix $Q$.
  2. Only one line of eigenvectors (unusual): The moving directions $Ax$ and $x$ touch but do not cross. This happens for the last two $2 \times 2$ matrices below.
  3. Two independent eigenvector directions (typical): $Ax$ crosses $x$ at the first eigenvector $x_1$, and crosses back at the second eigenvector $x_2$. Then $Ax$ and $x$ cross again at $-x_1$ and $-x_2$.

Follow $x$ and $Ax$ mentally for these five matrices. The number under each matrix gives the count of its real eigenvector directions:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$2
$$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$2
$$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$0
$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$$1
$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$1

When $A$ is singular (rank one), its column space is a line. The vector $Ax$ moves back and forth along that line while $x$ moves around the circle. One eigenvector $x$ lies along that line. Another eigenvector appears when $Ax_2 = 0$. Zero is an eigenvalue of a singular matrix.

Review of the Key Ideas

  1. $Ax = \lambda x$ means eigenvectors $x$ keep the same direction when $A$ multiplies them.
  2. $Ax = \lambda x$ also gives $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$. This equation finds the $n$ eigenvalues.
  3. The eigenvalues of $A^2$ and $A^{-1}$ are $\lambda^2$ and $\lambda^{-1}$, with the same eigenvectors.
  4. The sum of the $\lambda$'s equals the sum down the main diagonal of $A$ (the trace). The product of the $\lambda$'s equals the determinant.
  5. Projections $P$, reflections $R$, and $90^\circ$ rotations $Q$ have special eigenvalues $1, 0, -1, i, -i$. Singular matrices have $\lambda = 0$. Triangular matrices have their $\lambda$'s on the diagonal.

Worked Examples

Worked Example 6.1 A

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A$, $A^2$, $A^{-1}$, and $A + 4I$:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{and} \qquad A^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -4 \\ -4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Check the trace $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2$ and the determinant $\lambda_1 \lambda_2$ for both $A$ and $A^2$.

Solution: The eigenvalues of $A$ come from $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$:

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = \begin{vmatrix} 2 - \lambda & -1 \\ -1 & 2 - \lambda \end{vmatrix} = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = 0.$$

This factors into $(\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 3) = 0$, so the eigenvalues of $A$ are $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_2 = 3$. Check the trace: $2 + 2 = 4$, which matches $1 + 3$. Check the determinant: $3$, which matches $\lambda_1 \lambda_2 = 1 \cdot 3 = 3$.

Find each eigenvector by solving $(A - \lambda I)x = 0$ (which is $Ax = \lambda x$):

$$\lambda = 1: \quad (A - I)x = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{gives the eigenvector } x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ $$\lambda = 3: \quad (A - 3I)x = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{gives the eigenvector } x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}.$$

$A^2$, $A^{-1}$, and $A + 4I$ keep the same eigenvectors as $A$. Their eigenvalues are $\lambda^2$, $\lambda^{-1}$, and $\lambda + 4$:

$$A^2 \text{ has eigenvalues } 1^2 = 1 \text{ and } 3^2 = 9 \qquad A^{-1} \text{ has } \frac{1}{1} \text{ and } \frac{1}{3} \qquad A + 4I \text{ has } \begin{matrix} 1 + 4 = 5 \\ 3 + 4 = 7 \end{matrix}$$

The trace of $A^2$ is $5 + 5 = 10$, which matches $1 + 9$. The determinant is $25 - 16 = 9$, which matches $1 \cdot 9$.

Notes for later sections: $A$ has orthogonal eigenvectors (Section 6.4 on symmetric matrices). $A$ can be diagonalized because $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$ (Section 6.2). $A$ is similar to any $2 \times 2$ matrix with eigenvalues $1$ and $3$ (Section 6.6). $A$ is a positive definite matrix (Section 6.5) because $A = A^T$ and the $\lambda$'s are positive.

Worked Example 6.1 B

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of this $3 \times 3$ matrix $A$:

$$\textbf{Symmetric, Singular, Trace } 1 + 2 + 1 = 4: \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Solution: All rows of $A$ add to zero, so the vector $x = (1, 1, 1)$ gives $Ax = 0$. This is an eigenvector for the eigenvalue $\lambda = 0$. To find $\lambda_2$ and $\lambda_3$, compute the $3 \times 3$ determinant:

$$\begin{aligned} \det(A - \lambda I) &= \begin{vmatrix} 1 - \lambda & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 - \lambda & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 - \lambda \end{vmatrix} \\ &= (1 - \lambda)(2 - \lambda)(1 - \lambda) - 2(1 - \lambda) \\ &= (1 - \lambda)\big[(2 - \lambda)(1 - \lambda) - 2\big] \\ &= (1 - \lambda)(-\lambda)(3 - \lambda). \end{aligned}$$

The factor $-\lambda$ confirms that $\lambda = 0$ is a root and an eigenvalue of $A$. The other factors $(1 - \lambda)$ and $(3 - \lambda)$ give the other eigenvalues $1$ and $3$. Their sum is $0 + 1 + 3 = 4$ (the trace). Each eigenvalue $0, 1, 3$ corresponds to an eigenvector:

$$x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad Ax_1 = 0x_1 \qquad\qquad x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \quad Ax_2 = 1x_2 \qquad\qquad x_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad Ax_3 = 3x_3.$$

Notice again that eigenvectors are perpendicular when $A$ is symmetric.

This $3 \times 3$ matrix gave a cubic (third-degree) polynomial $\det(A - \lambda I) = -\lambda^3 + 4\lambda^2 - 3\lambda$. Here we found simple roots $\lambda = 0, 1, 3$. Usually we use a software command like eig(A), which does not use determinants (Section 9.3 shows a better method for large matrices).

The full command [S, D] = eig(A) puts unit eigenvectors in the columns of the eigenvector matrix $S$. The first column may have three minus signs: $-(1, 1, 1)/\sqrt{3}$. The eigenvalues of $A$ appear on the diagonal of the eigenvalue matrix (typed as $D$, soon called $\Lambda$).

6.2 Diagonalizing a Matrix

When $x$ is an eigenvector, multiplication by $A$ is just multiplication by a number $\lambda$: $Ax = \lambda x$. All the difficulties of matrices disappear. Instead of a connected system, we can follow each eigenvector separately. It works like a diagonal matrix, with no off-diagonal links. The $100$th power of a diagonal matrix is easy to find.

The goal of this section is simple. The matrix $A$ turns into a diagonal matrix $\Lambda$ when we use the eigenvectors correctly. This is the matrix form of our main idea. We begin with that key calculation.

Diagonalization

Suppose the $n \times n$ matrix $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors $x_1, \dots, x_n$. Put them into the columns of an eigenvector matrix $S$. Then $S^{-1} A S$ is the eigenvalue matrix $\Lambda$:

$$\begin{matrix} \textbf{Eigenvector matrix } S \\ \textbf{Eigenvalue matrix } \Lambda \end{matrix} \qquad S^{-1} A S = \Lambda = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \lambda_n \end{bmatrix}.$$
(1)

The matrix $A$ is "diagonalized." We use capital lambda ($\Lambda$) for the eigenvalue matrix because the small $\lambda$'s (the eigenvalues) sit on its diagonal.

Proof: Multiply $A$ times its eigenvectors, which are the columns of $S$. The first column of $AS$ is $Ax_1$, which is $\lambda_1 x_1$. Each column of $S$ is multiplied by its eigenvalue $\lambda_i$:

$$\textbf{A times S} \qquad AS = A \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & \cdots & x_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 x_1 & \cdots & \lambda_n x_n \end{bmatrix}.$$

Now split this matrix $AS$ into $S$ times $\Lambda$:

$$\textbf{S times } \Lambda \qquad \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 x_1 & \cdots & \lambda_n x_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & \cdots & x_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \lambda_n \end{bmatrix} = S\Lambda.$$

Keep those matrices in the right order! Then $\lambda_1$ multiplies the first column $x_1$, as shown. Diagonalization is complete, and we can write $AS = S\Lambda$ in two useful forms:

$$AS = S\Lambda \quad \text{is} \quad S^{-1}AS = \Lambda \quad \text{or} \quad A = S\Lambda S^{-1}.$$
(2)

The matrix $S$ has an inverse because we assumed its columns (the eigenvectors of $A$) are linearly independent. Without $n$ independent eigenvectors, we cannot diagonalize.

$A$ and $\Lambda$ have the same eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$. Their eigenvectors are different. The job of the original eigenvectors $x_1, \dots, x_n$ was to diagonalize $A$. Those eigenvectors in $S$ give $A = S\Lambda S^{-1}$. You will soon see how simple and useful the $n$th power $A^n = S\Lambda^n S^{-1}$ is.
