In an n-type sample the electrons come from the donors, so $E_F$ moves up, about midway between $E_d$ and $E_c$. As the sample is heated $E_F$ falls back to mid-gap and the sample behaves intrinsically. Its conductivity is $\sigma\approx N_de\mu_n$ in the useful range. n-type

At 0 K every donor level is full and the conduction band empty, so $E_F$ lies between $E_d$ and $E_c$. Warming ionises some donors (electrons to the conduction band) and breaks a few bonds (holes). With enough donors the holes are suppressed by mass action, so $n\approx N_d^+$.

A direct link to the intrinsic level, from $n=N_ce^{-(E_c-E_F)/kT}$ and $n_i=N_ce^{-(E_c-E_i)/kT}$:

$$n=n_ie^{(E_F-E_i)/kT}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad E_F-E_i=kT\ln\frac{n}{n_i}$$

So $n$ approaches $n_i$ as $E_F$ approaches $E_i$, and grows exponentially as $E_F$ moves towards the conduction band. Each factor of 10 in $n$ moves $E_F$ by $kT\ln10=0.06$ eV at room temperature.

The classic curve, computed exactly from charge neutrality for silicon. Teal: n-type, copper: p-type, for $10^{20}$, $10^{22}$ and $10^{24}$ impurities per $\mathrm{m}^{3}$. Heavier doping pushes $E_F$ towards its band edge; heating pulls every curve back to $E_i$ as thermally made pairs swamp the dopants. The lightly doped sample turns intrinsic first. Conductivity of n-type Ge against temperature $$\sigma=ne\mu_n\approx eN_d\mu_n$$ The classic conductivity plot for n-type Ge, computed from the same neutrality condition with a mobility that rises with $T$ at first (impurity scattering) and then falls (lattice vibrations). The three regions are the classic 8-mark answer.
  1. A → B (about 10 K to 50 K), the ionisation region: $\sigma$ rises as more donors are ionised; maximum when all are ionised.
  2. B → C (up to about room temperature), the extrinsic plateau: $\sigma$ falls slightly: $n\approx N_d$ is fixed but the mobility drops as lattice vibrations grow. This is the working range of devices.
  3. C → D, the intrinsic region: steep rise: broken bonds (intrinsic carriers) overtake the donors, and the curve joins the intrinsic line.
p-type

Same argument with acceptors: $p\approx N_a^-=N_af(E_a)\approx N_ae^{-(E_a-E_F)/kT}$, and equating with $p=N_ve^{-(E_F-E_v)/kT}$:

$$E_F=\frac{E_v+E_a}{2}-\frac{kT}{2}\ln\frac{N_a}{N_v},\qquad p=n_ie^{(E_i-E_F)/kT},\qquad \sigma_p=ep\mu_p\approx eN_a\mu_p$$ $E_F$ lies about midway between $E_v$ and $E_a$, moves up with rising $T$ and finally meets $E_i$. Not valid at very high $T$ (then $n$ cannot be neglected).

More impurities give higher conductivity: for $10^{20}$–$10^{22}$ impurities per $\mathrm{m}^{3}$ the resistivity of Si and Ge falls to $10^{-3}$–$10^{-1}\ \Omega\,$m. Very heavy doping ($10^{23}$–$10^{24}$ $\mathrm{m}^{-3}$) makes degenerate semiconductors: $E_F$ moves into the band itself, the Boltzmann approximation fails, and the sample conducts nearly as well as a metal (high-power and high-frequency devices).

Mixed semiconductors

With both donors and acceptors, neutrality reads $N_d^++p=N_a^-+n$. If $N_d=N_a$, the acceptors capture the donors' electrons: $n=p=n_i$, and the sample behaves as intrinsic (compensated). If $N_d\ne N_a$ it is n-type or p-type according to whichever is larger, with an effective doping of $|N_d-N_a|$.

Summary of the Fermi level: intrinsic, at mid-gap; n-type, about midway between $E_d$ and $E_c$; p-type, about midway between $E_v$ and $E_a$. As $T$ rises it approaches the intrinsic level in every case.

 An n-type sample is heated a long way. What happens to its Fermi level and why?

It moves down from near $E_c$ towards mid-gap ($E_i$). At high $T$ the thermally generated electron–hole pairs ($n_i$ grows as $e^{-E_g/2kT}$) outnumber the donor electrons, so the sample behaves as intrinsic, with $n\approx p$ and $E_F$ near the middle of the gap.

 Can a semiconductor containing both n-type and p-type impurities behave as an intrinsic one?

Yes, when $N_d=N_a$: every donor electron drops into an acceptor, so the dopants cancel and only thermally made pairs remain, $n=p=n_i$. It is called compensated.
