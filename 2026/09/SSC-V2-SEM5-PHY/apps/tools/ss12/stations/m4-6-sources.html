Three mechanisms add up: the electron cloud shifts ($\alpha_e$, every atom), ions shift against each other ($\alpha_i$, ionic solids), permanent dipoles turn ($\alpha_d=p^2/3kT$, polar molecules). As the frequency rises they drop out one by one: dipolar first, then ionic, leaving the electronic part at optical frequencies. Three ways to polarise

The total polarisability is the sum of whatever mechanisms the material allows:

$$\alpha=\alpha_e+\alpha_i+\alpha_d$$ Choose a mechanism and switch the field on. Electronic: the cloud slides relative to the nucleus. Ionic: the + and − sublattices move apart. Dipolar: permanent dipoles turn partly towards the field, against thermal disorder. Every material has the first; ionic crystals add the second; polar molecules add the third. Electronic polarisability

The electron cloud is displaced relative to the nucleus until the restoring pull of the nucleus balances the field. Model the cloud as a uniformly charged sphere of radius $R$: the restoring force is linear in the displacement, like a spring, which gives $\alpha_e=4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3$, proportional to the atom's volume. At optical frequencies the polarisation is almost all electronic, and with $\varepsilon_r=n^2$ (Maxwell): $\dfrac{n^2-1}{n^2+2}=\dfrac{1}{3\varepsilon_0}\sum N_j\alpha_{ej}$. This is how the refractive index measures $\alpha_e$.

Ionic polarisability

In an ionic crystal (NaCl, KCl) a field pushes the positive ions one way and the negative ions the other, stretching some bonds and compressing others. The bonds act as springs of stiffness $\beta$, so each ion pair becomes a dipole $p=e\,\Delta x$ with $\Delta x=eE/\beta$:

The ions are thousands of times heavier than electrons, so this mechanism resonates at much lower frequency (infrared, the lattice vibration frequency, $\sim10^{13}$ Hz). It is comparable in size to $\alpha_e$: in NaCl it is about half the total.

Dipolar polarisability

Polar molecules ($\mathrm{H_{2}O}$, HCl, $\mathrm{NH_{3}}$) have a permanent dipole moment $p$. Without a field they point at random; a field turns them, thermal agitation resists, and the balance gives exactly the Langevin average of paramagnetism, with $pE$ in place of $\mu B$:

$$\alpha_e=4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3\ \ (\text{static}),\qquad \alpha_i=\frac{e^2}{\beta},\qquad \alpha_d=\frac{p^2}{3kT}$$ Only $\alpha_d$ depends on temperature. Plot $\alpha$ against $1/T$: slope $p^2/3k$ gives the dipole moment, intercept gives $\alpha_e+\alpha_i$ (the Debye method).

 Frequency dependence

Each process has a relaxation time. When the field changes much faster than that, the process cannot follow and stops contributing. Dipolar orientation is slowest (whole molecules turning, drops out by microwave frequencies, $\sim10^{10}$–$10^{11}$ Hz), ionic next (infrared, $\sim10^{13}$ Hz), electronic last (ultraviolet, $\sim10^{15}$ Hz). So $\alpha$ and $\varepsilon_r$ fall in steps as the frequency rises. (A fourth, space-charge polarisation at interfaces, matters only at very low frequency.)

Near each step the material absorbs energy strongly (the dielectric loss peaks): this is why water absorbs microwaves and ionic crystals absorb in the infrared. Microwave ovens work at 2.45 GHz, where water's dipoles lag the field.

Water's static $\varepsilon_r\approx80$ but its refractive index is 1.33 ($n^2=1.77$): the huge difference is the dipolar part, which is gone at optical frequencies. Never use $\varepsilon_r=n^2$ unless both are at the same frequency.

 NaCl has $\varepsilon_r=5.6$ (static) and refractive index 1.5. What share of its polarisability is ionic?

Static: $\dfrac{\varepsilon_r-1}{\varepsilon_r+2}=\dfrac{4.6}{7.6}=0.605\propto\alpha_e+\alpha_i$. Optical: $\dfrac{n^2-1}{n^2+2}=\dfrac{1.25}{4.25}=0.294\propto\alpha_e$. Electronic share $0.294/0.605=0.486$, so ionic $=1-0.486=$ 51.4%.

 Which polarisation mechanisms still work at (a) 1 kHz, (b) $10^{12}$ Hz, (c) visible light, for liquid water?

(a) all three, electronic, ionic and dipolar; (b) electronic and ionic (the molecules can no longer turn); (c) electronic only, so $\varepsilon_r=n^2\approx1.8$.
