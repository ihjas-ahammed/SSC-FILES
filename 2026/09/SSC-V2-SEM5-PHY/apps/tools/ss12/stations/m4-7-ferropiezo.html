A ferroelectric has spontaneous polarisation (the electric twin of ferromagnetism): a $P$–$E$ hysteresis loop, a Curie point, and $\varepsilon_r\propto1/(T-T_c)$ above it. A piezoelectric crystal (no centre of symmetry) polarises when squeezed, and strains in a field. Ferroelectricity

Spontaneous polarisation: the centres of positive and negative charge do not coincide even with no field. Everything you learnt about ferromagnets carries over with $P$ for $M$ and $E$ for $H$: domains, a $P$–$E$ hysteresis loop (remanent polarisation, coercive field), and a transition temperature (Curie point) $T_c$ above which the crystal is paraelectric. Examples: barium titanate $\mathrm{BaTiO_{3}}$, Rochelle salt ($\mathrm{NaKC_{4}H_{4}O_{6}\cdot 4H_{2}O}$), KDP.

Two groups:

  • Order–disorder: the transition is the ordering of individual ions, linked to protons in hydrogen bonds: $\mathrm{KH_{2}PO_{4}}$ (KDP), $\mathrm{RbH_{2}PO_{4}}$.
  • Displacive: a whole sublattice of one kind of ion shifts relative to another; perovskite and ilmenite structures: $\mathrm{BaTiO_{3}}$, $\mathrm{KNbO_{3}}$, $\mathrm{LiTaO_{3}}$.

$\mathrm{BaTiO_{3}}$ (perovskite): cubic cell above $T_c$ (about 393 K) with $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$ at the corners, $\mathrm{O^{2-}}$ at the face centres and $\mathrm{Ti^{4+}}$ at the body centre (octahedrally surrounded by six $\mathrm{O^{2-}}$); the charge centres coincide. Below $T_c$, $\mathrm{Ti^{4+}}$ (and $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$) shift slightly up and $\mathrm{O^{2-}}$ down: the cell becomes tetragonal and polarised. The polarisation comes from induced electronic and ionic dipoles.

A face of the $\mathrm{BaTiO_{3}}$ cell, with the shifts exaggerated so they show. Above $T_c$ the $\mathrm{Ti^{4+}}$ sits exactly at the centre of its oxygen cage. Below $T_c$ it moves 0.06 Å up while the oxygens move 0.06–0.08 Å down, and the cell stretches along $c$: a built-in dipole in every cell.

Why it happens: the polarisation catastrophe. From Clausius–Mossotti, $\varepsilon_r$ diverges when $N\alpha/3\varepsilon_0$ reaches 1: the local field from the polarised neighbours alone is then enough to keep each ion polarised, so the polarisation sustains itself with no applied field.

$\varepsilon_r$ of $\mathrm{BaTiO_{3}}$ against temperature: a sharp peak of about $10^4$ at the Curie point and the Curie–Weiss fall above it, the electric twin of $\chi=C/(T-T_c)$.

$$\varepsilon_r=\frac{C}{T-T_c}\quad(T>T_c)$$ Ferroelectric ↔ ferromagnetic: spontaneous $P$ ↔ $M$, $P$–$E$ loop ↔ $M$–$H$ loop, Curie point and Curie–Weiss law in both. Uses: capacitors (very high $\varepsilon_r$ near $T_c$), non-volatile memory (FeRAM), infrared detectors.

 Piezoelectricity

In some crystals a stress produces a dipole moment and so surface charges (direct effect); a field produces a strain (converse effect). Examples: quartz, Rochelle salt, tourmaline, $\mathrm{BaTiO_{3}}$ and PZT ceramics.

Why the crystal must lack a centre of symmetry. In the hexagonal ring the + and − charge centres coincide until a squeeze moves the ions on the line of force unequally; then they separate and a dipole appears. In a centrosymmetric arrangement every ion has a twin opposite it, and the squeeze moves the twins symmetrically: the centres never separate.

In one-dimensional notation the two effects read:

$$P=Zd+E\varepsilon_0\chi,\qquad e=Zs+Ed$$

$P$ polarisation, $Z$ stress, $d$ piezoelectric strain constant, $E$ field, $\chi$ dielectric susceptibility, $e$ strain, $s$ elastic compliance. The same constant $d$ appears in both equations: the direct and converse effects are two faces of one coupling. Large fields give small strains: in quartz $10^4$ V/m gives a strain of about $10^{-8}$.

The ions must be displaced so that the charge distribution loses its symmetry: piezoelectricity needs a crystal with no centre of inversion (20 of the 32 point groups). Every ferroelectric is piezoelectric ($\mathrm{BaTiO_{3}}$), but not every piezoelectric is ferroelectric (quartz).

Uses: electromechanical transducers: pick-ups, microphones, strain gauges, ultrasonic generators, gas lighters, and quartz oscillators that keep time in every watch and computer.

Why can a crystal with a centre of inversion never be piezoelectric?

A uniform stress keeps the centre of inversion. For every displaced charge there is an equal charge displaced the opposite way about the centre, so the centres of positive and negative charge stay together: no dipole moment, no polarisation.

 List three ways a ferroelectric resembles a ferromagnet and one way it differs.

Alike: spontaneous order below a Curie point; domains and a hysteresis loop; Curie–Weiss law ($\varepsilon_r$ or $\chi\propto1/(T-T_c)$) above $T_c$. Different: the order is of electric dipoles made by ion displacements (a structural change of the crystal, e.g. cubic to tetragonal), not of electron spins coupled by exchange.
