A semiconductor is an insulator with a small band gap (about 1 eV). Heat breaks a few bonds and makes electron–hole pairs (intrinsic). Adding a trace of Group V or Group III atoms adds electrons or holes on purpose (extrinsic), and that is how every device is made. What a semiconductor is

Put the three kinds of solid side by side by how well they conduct. Conductors have resistivities of $10^{-8}$–$10^{-6}\ \Omega\,$m, insulators $10^{7}$–$10^{8}\ \Omega\,$m and more, and semiconductors sit in between: $10^{-5}$ to $10^{4}\ \Omega\,$m. Ge and Si (Group IV) have resistivities of about 0.6 and $1.5\times10^{3}\ \Omega\,$m. Compound semiconductors come from Groups III–V (GaAs, InP) and II–VI (CdS).

The band picture from Module II explains the middle position: the valence band is full and the conduction band empty, exactly as in an insulator, but the gap $E_g$ between them is small enough for room-temperature heat to push a few electrons across.

Same column of the periodic table, same diamond-type bonding, and the gap shrinks steadily down the group. That single number decides insulator, semiconductor or conductor.

Two properties every exam asks for:

  • Negative temperature coefficient of resistance. A metal conducts worse when hot (more scattering). A semiconductor conducts better when hot, because heating makes new carriers faster than it slows the old ones.
  • Doping changes everything. Adding impurity atoms in a controlled way, about 1 in $10^{6}$ to $10^{8}$, changes the conductivity enormously. The impurity takes the place of a host atom, so the crystal structure does not change.

The small gap also fixes the wavelength of light the material emits or absorbs: that is why semiconductors make LEDs, lasers and solar cells.

Intrinsic semiconductors

In pure Si each atom shares its four valence electrons in four covalent bonds pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron (the diamond structure of Module I). Step through the bond picture:

The bond picture, the usual way to draw it. Two dots on a link are the two electrons of one covalent bond. Step 2 is the whole of intrinsic conduction; steps 4 and 5 are doping.

At 0 K every electron is in a bond: pure Si is an insulator at 0 K. The valence band is full and the conduction band empty. Above 0 K a few electrons get enough thermal energy to break free. Each one leaves a hole, an empty place in a bond, which also carries current: a neighbouring bond electron can jump into it, so the hole moves the other way. One broken bond gives one electron–hole pair, so in a pure sample

$$n=p=n_i\quad(\text{intrinsic carrier concentration})$$

Properties of a hole: charge $+e$; it moves in the valence band, in the direction of the field; it is not a particle but the absence of an electron in an otherwise full band; it has a larger effective mass and smaller mobility than an electron, because it moves in a nearly full band under the applied field and the ionic field of the lattice.

Generation and recombination. Pairs are made all the time by heat. A free electron that meets a hole drops back into the bond, giving out about $E_g$ (often as light). In equilibrium the two rates balance:

$$g=R=Cn_ip_i=Cn_i^2$$

where $C$ depends on the material (it is related to the densities of states at the band edges). Because the recombination rate is proportional to the product of the two concentrations, this balance is the first hint of the law of mass action, $np=n_i^2$.

Extrinsic semiconductors

n-type (donors). A Group V atom (P, As, Sb) uses four of its five valence electrons in bonds. The fifth is held only weakly, so a little energy frees it. Its energy level, the donor level $E_d$, sits in the gap just below $E_c$: about 0.01 eV below in Ge and 0.03 eV in Si. Against $kT\approx0.026$ eV at room temperature, almost every donor gives its electron to the conduction band and becomes an immobile positive ion. Electrons are the majority carriers, thermally made holes the minority carriers.

p-type (acceptors). A Group III atom (B, Al, Ga, In) completes only three bonds. It takes an electron from a neighbouring Si atom to complete the fourth, leaving a hole there. Its acceptor level $E_a$ is just above $E_v$, about 0.01 eV. The impurity becomes an immobile negative ion; holes are the majority carriers.

The same three cases as band diagrams. Switch between them: the dopant levels sit a hair inside the gap, they are almost all ionised at room temperature, and the Fermi level follows the majority carrier towards its band.

How big is the effect? Si has $5\times10^{28}$ atoms per $\mathrm{m}^{3}$. Doping at 1 in $10^{8}$ gives $N_d=5\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$ donors, against $n_i=1.5\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$ at 300 K: some 30 000 times more electrons than the pure crystal has, from one foreign atom in a hundred million.

In both types the law of mass action holds: $np=n_i^2$ at a given temperature. Doping raises one carrier and lowers the other in proportion. The crystal stays neutral: $n+N_a^-=p+N_d^+$.

A donor atom does not make a hole when it gives its electron away: what it leaves is a fixed positive ion, not a mobile hole. Only broken Si–Si bonds make holes. Likewise an acceptor makes a mobile hole and a fixed negative ion, not a free electron.

 Si is doped with $10^{22}$ donors per $\mathrm{m}^{3}$ (all ionised). With $n_i=1.5\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, find $n$ and $p$.

$n\approx N_d=10^{22}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$. Mass action: $p=n_i^2/n=(1.5\times10^{16})^2/10^{22}=2.25\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, a factor of $10^{12}$ below $n$.

 Why is pure silicon an insulator at 0 K but a (poor) conductor at room temperature, while copper is a good conductor at both?

At 0 K every valence electron of Si is locked in a bond: the valence band is full, the conduction band empty, nothing can carry current. At 300 K a few bonds break ($n_i\sim10^{16}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$), giving electrons and holes. Copper's conduction band is half full at any temperature ($\sim10^{28}$ free electrons per $\mathrm{m}^{3}$), so it never needs heat to make carriers.
