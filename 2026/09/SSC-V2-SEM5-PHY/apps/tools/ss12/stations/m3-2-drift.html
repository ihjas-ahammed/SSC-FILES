A field gives the carriers a small extra velocity on top of their random motion: $v_d=\mu E$. Electrons and holes both carry current, so $\sigma=e(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$. In a pure sample $\sigma$ rises as $e^{-E_g/2kT}$, and $\ln\sigma$ against $1/T$ measures the gap. Drift velocity and mobility

With no field the carriers move at random and give no current: for every electron going left there is one going right. A field accelerates electrons one way and holes the other, but they keep colliding (with vibrating atoms, impurities, defects) and lose the extra velocity each time. On average they gain a steady extra velocity, the drift velocity, proportional to the field:

$$v_d=\mu E$$

The constant $\mu$ is the mobility: drift velocity per unit field, in $\mathrm{m^2V^{-1}s^{-1}}$. It is the same idea as the Drude picture of Module II, with $\mu=e\tau/m^*$: a long time between collisions and a light effective mass make a mobile carrier.

One electron, the same random collisions twice: grey with no field, teal with a field. The thermal speed ($\sim10^5$ m/s) is enormous next to the drift ($\sim$ cm/s); the field only biases the walk. The red arrow is everything the field achieved. (The bias is exaggerated a great deal so you can see it.)

Why electrons beat holes. A hole's motion is really a relay of bond electrons in a nearly full band, so it has a larger effective mass and collides more effectively: $\mu_p<\mu_n$ (Si: 0.048 against 0.135). That is why devices that use electrons (n-type channels) are faster and preferred.

$$v_d=\mu E,\qquad J=\sigma E,\qquad \sigma=e(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$$ Intrinsic: $n=p=n_i$, so $\sigma_i=en_i(\mu_n+\mu_p)$. Electrons and holes drift opposite ways but, having opposite charges, their currents add.

Drift and diffusion. Carriers also move down a concentration gradient (diffusion current). In a sample with uniformly spread impurities there is no gradient, so only the drift current counts here. The two are linked by the Einstein relation $D/\mu=kT/e$ (0.026 V at room temperature).

Conductivity and temperature

Taking the mobilities as constant, all the temperature dependence comes from $n_i$ (derived in the next section): $n_i\propto T^{3/2}e^{-E_g/2kT}$. The exponential swamps the $T^{3/2}$, so a plot of $\ln\sigma$ against $1/T$ is very nearly a straight line, and its slope gives the gap. This is the standard laboratory way of measuring $E_g$.

Metal against semiconductor, with impurities. Impurities in a metal add scattering, so its conductivity falls. Impurities in a semiconductor add carriers, so its conductivity rises, by many orders of magnitude, even though they also scatter a little. Temperature works the same way round: a metal's $\sigma$ falls as $1/T$, a semiconductor's climbs exponentially.

The slope of $\ln\sigma$ against $1/T$ is $-E_g/2k$, not $-E_g/k$. Forgetting the 2 halves or doubles your band gap. Mobility data are often given in $\mathrm{cm^2V^{-1}s^{-1}}$: divide by $10^4$.

 Intrinsic Si at 300 K: $n_i=1.5\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, $\mu_n=0.135$, $\mu_p=0.048\ \mathrm{m^2V^{-1}s^{-1}}$. Find $\sigma_i$ and $\rho$.

$\sigma_i=n_ie(\mu_n+\mu_p)=1.5\times10^{16}\times1.6\times10^{-19}\times0.183=4.39\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}$. $\rho=1/\sigma=2.3\times10^{3}\ \Omega\,$m: about $10^{11}$ times copper's and $10^{13}$ times less than glass. That middle position is what "semiconductor" means.

 In a field of 100 V/m, how fast do electrons drift in Si ($\mu_n=0.135$)? Compare with their thermal speed of about $10^5$ m/s.

$v_d=\mu E=0.135\times100=13.5$ m/s, about $10^{-4}$ of the thermal speed. The current comes from a tiny, steady bias on a very fast random motion.
