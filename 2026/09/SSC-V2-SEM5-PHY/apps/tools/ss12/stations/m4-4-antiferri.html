Negative exchange makes neighbours antiparallel. Two equal sublattices cancel (antiferromagnetism: small $\chi$ with a cusp at the Néel temperature). Two unequal ones leave a net moment (ferrimagnetism: ferrite, magnetite), which behaves outwardly like a ferromagnet. Antiferromagnetism

Spins of neighbouring atoms antiparallel (exchange integral negative). Think of two interpenetrating sublattices A and B, magnetised in opposite directions: no net magnetisation. First seen in MnO ($T_N=116$ K); also FeO, NiO, CoO, $\mathrm{MnF_{2}}$, Cr. A field gives a small magnetisation that increases with temperature up to the Néel temperature $T_N$, then falls (paramagnetic). At $T_N$, $\chi$ is not infinite but has a weak cusp.

Néel's theory above $T_N$. Each sublattice is a paramagnet that feels the applied field minus molecular fields from the other sublattice ($\beta$) and from its own ($\alpha$). The minus signs are the whole story: the neighbours fight the field instead of helping it.

$$\chi=\frac{2C}{T+\theta}\quad(T>T_N)$$ Like Curie–Weiss with the sign of the intercept reversed: $1/\chi$ extrapolates to $-\theta$. In the simplest theory (only A–B interactions) $\theta=T_N$; real crystals have $\theta>T_N$ (MnO: $\theta\approx610$ K, $T_N=116$ K).

Below $T_N$: each sublattice is ordered along its preferred direction. For a field across that direction, $\chi_\perp$ stays almost constant (both sublattices simply cant a little towards the field). Along it, $\chi_\parallel$ falls to zero at 0 K: the internal field is too strong to let a perfectly ordered sublattice turn, and at 0 K both are perfectly ordered. At $T_N$, $\chi_\perp=\chi_\parallel=\chi$.

The dashed curve is a powder, $\tfrac13\chi_\parallel+\tfrac23\chi_\perp$, which is what a polycrystalline sample shows: the cusp. Right chip: the ferrimagnet. Ferrimagnetism

The same antiparallel arrangement, but the sublattice magnetisations are unequal, so a net magnetisation survives. Found in ferrites, double oxides of the form $\mathrm{MO\cdot Fe_{2}O_{3}}$ (M = Mn, Co, Ni, Cu, Mg, Zn, Fe) with the spinel structure: oxygen ions in a close-packed cubic array, metal ions in the gaps between them. Two kinds of gap: A (tetrahedral) sites with four $\mathrm{O^{2-}}$ neighbours and B (octahedral) sites with six. The A–B exchange is strongly negative, so the A and B sublattices point opposite ways.

Magnetite $\mathrm{Fe_{3}O_{4}}$ = $\mathrm{FeO\cdot Fe_{2}O_{3}}$: one $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ on an A site; one $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ and the $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ on B sites. The two $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ moments (5 $\mu_B$ each, $3d^{5}$) cancel, and the net moment per formula unit is that of one $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$, 4 $\mu_B$ ($3d^{6}$: four unpaired electrons). Experiment gives 4.1 $\mu_B$. A spinel unit cell holds 8 formula units.

Ferrimagnet in one line: antiparallel and unequal sublattices. Net moment = difference of the sublattice moments. Outwardly like a ferromagnet (spontaneous $M$, Curie point, domains, hysteresis), but with a much smaller saturation moment and a very high electrical resistivity.

Above the Curie temperature both sublattices follow Curie–Weiss-like laws and $1/\chi$ is a hyperbola rather than a straight line; below it, saturation sets in. Ferrites are ceramics with resistivities up to $10^{10}$ times that of iron, so eddy-current losses are tiny: the standard cores for high-frequency transformers, inductors, antenna rods and microwave devices.

$\mathrm{Fe_{3}O_{4}}$ has three iron ions per formula unit, yet its moment is only about 4 $\mu_B$. Why?

The $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ on the A site and the $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ on a B site are antiparallel and equal (5 $\mu_B$ each), so they cancel. What is left is the $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ on the other B site, 4 $\mu_B$ (four unpaired 3d electrons).

 An unknown oxide has a small positive $\chi$ that rises with temperature to a maximum at 200 K and then falls. Above 200 K, $1/\chi$ is a straight line that extrapolates to $-400$ K. What is it?

An antiferromagnet with $T_N=200$ K (the cusp) and $\theta=400$ K (the negative intercept of the Néel law). $\theta>T_N$ is normal: interactions within each sublattice add to the A–B ones.
