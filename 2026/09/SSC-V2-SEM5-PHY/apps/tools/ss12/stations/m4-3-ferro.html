Weiss: an internal field proportional to $M$ lines the moments up. It explains spontaneous magnetisation below $T_c$ and the Curie–Weiss law above it. The field is the quantum exchange interaction. A lump of iron can be unmagnetised because it is split into domains; moving and turning them gives the hysteresis loop. Ferromagnetism

Permanent moments, as in paramagnets, but neighbours line up parallel even without a field: spontaneous magnetisation, below the Curie temperature $T_c$. Above $T_c$ thermal agitation wins and the material is paramagnetic. Large, positive, field-dependent $\chi$; magnetisation against field traces a hysteresis curve. Examples: Fe ($T_c=1043$ K), Ni (631 K), Co (1388 K), Gd (292 K), Dy; alloys and oxides such as MnBi, MnAs, $\mathrm{CrO_{2}}$.

Two puzzles need explaining. Why do the moments line up at all, when a paramagnet needs an enormous field to do the same? And if iron is spontaneously magnetised, why is an ordinary iron nail not a magnet? Weiss answered both with two hypotheses.

Weiss theory

Two hypotheses: (i) the specimen is made of small spontaneously magnetised domains, whose moments add as vectors; (ii) inside a domain an exchange (molecular) field $B_E=\lambda M$ lines the moments up, $\lambda$ the Weiss constant (independent of $T$). For iron $B_E\approx1000$ T.

Above $T_c$: treat the material as a paramagnet that feels $B+\lambda M$ instead of $B$. Solving for $M$ gives the Curie–Weiss law:

$$\chi=\frac{C}{T-T_c},\qquad T_c=\frac{\lambda C}{\mu_0},\qquad C=\frac{\mu_0Ng^2J(J+1)\mu_B^2}{3k}$$ A strong molecular field (large $\lambda$) means a high Curie temperature. Internal field from measured numbers: $B_E=\lambda M_s=\dfrac{3kT_c}{g(J+1)\mu_B}$ (quantum) or $\dfrac{3kT_c}{\mu}$ (classical).

Below $T_c$: spontaneous magnetisation. With $B=0$, the magnetisation must satisfy two equations at once. The paramagnet formula says $M=M_s(0)B_J(x)$ with $x=gJ\mu_B\lambda M/kT$; turned round, the definition of $x$ says $M$ is proportional to $x$, with a slope proportional to $T$. The graphical solution (standard construction) for spin $\tfrac12$. Cold: the straight line is shallow and cuts the curve again away from the origin: a non-zero magnetisation exists with no field. The fall to zero at $T_c$ is continuous, with no latent heat: a second-order phase transition.

Origin of the Weiss field: exchange

A dipole–dipole field is about $10^3$ G (0.1 T), far too weak: iron needs about $10^7$ G. Heisenberg (1928): the field comes from the quantum exchange interaction, a consequence of the Pauli principle. Turning one spin changes the allowed spatial wavefunction and so the charge distribution: the energy is electrostatic, not magnetic. The energy of two atoms contains

$$U=-2J_e\,\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j$$

$J_e$, the exchange integral, depends on the overlap of the two atoms' charge clouds. $J_e>0$: parallel spins have lower energy (ferromagnetism). $J_e<0$: antiparallel (antiferromagnetism). Bethe–Slater: $J_e>0$ when $r_{ab}/r_{3d}$ (atomic separation ÷ radius of the 3d shell) is a little above 3: Fe, Co, Ni, Gd are ferromagnetic; Mn and Cr are not. Summing over $z$ nearest neighbours (Stoner) gives $B_E=\lambda M$ with $\lambda=\dfrac{2zJ_e}{Ng^2\mu_B^2}$.

Domains and hysteresis

Why iron is usually unmagnetised: each domain is fully magnetised but the domains point in different directions. Néel: the domain pattern minimises the total energy: exchange, magnetic field energy, anisotropy energy, wall energy and magnetostrictive energy. One big domain has a large external field; splitting lowers that, but every wall costs energy, so the split stops at a balance.

Domains from the energy argument, then the two ways a field magnetises them. Steps 1–3: splitting and closure domains remove the stray field. Step 4: wall movement (low fields). Step 5: rotation (high fields), up to saturation.

Two processes magnetise a specimen: (a) growth of favourably oriented domains (wall movement) and (b) rotation of domain magnetisation towards the field. They map onto the magnetisation curve:

  • OA (small fields): reversible wall movement.
  • AB: large, irreversible wall movements; $M$ rises steeply.
  • BC: domains rotate from their easy directions to the field; $M$ creeps up to saturation $M_s$.
  • Reduce the field: $M$ does not retrace the curve. At $H=0$ the remanence $M_r$ is left. The reverse field that removes it is the coercivity $H_c$. The closed loop is the hysteresis loop; its area is the energy lost per cycle.

Soft materials (small $H_c$, thin loop: transformer cores, electromagnets; soft iron, Si-steel) and hard materials (large $H_c$ and $M_r$: permanent magnets; alnico, ferrite magnets).

Sketch $1/\chi$ against $T$ above the critical temperature for a paramagnet, a ferromagnet and an antiferromagnet, and say what tells them apart.

All three are straight lines. Paramagnet: through the origin ($\chi=C/T$). Ferromagnet: cuts the $T$-axis at $+T_c$ ($\chi=C/(T-T_c)$). Antiferromagnet: extrapolates to $-\theta$ ($\chi=2C/(T+\theta)$). The sign of the intercept is the diagnosis.

 Iron has $T_c=1043$ K and an effective moment of $2.2\,\mu_B$ per atom. Estimate the internal field.

Classical Weiss: $B_E=\dfrac{3kT_c}{\mu}=\dfrac{3\times1.38\times10^{-23}\times1043}{2.2\times9.27\times10^{-24}}\approx2100$ T. No ordinary dipole field comes near this, which is why the field must be exchange.
