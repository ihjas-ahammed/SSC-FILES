9.2.1 The wave equation for E and B

Source: book-sources/m3-sec3-wave-eq.html and the end of m3-sec2-polar.html, Griffiths pp. 401–402. Equations 9.40–9.43 in full.

Before you start

  • Maxwell’s equations in vacuum with no charge or current (Module 2, Section 7.3).
  • Curl of a curl: $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf A)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)-\nabla^2\mathbf A$ (Beyond in Plus Two; pre-math M13).

1. Maxwell’s equations in empty space

With no charge and no current, Maxwell’s equations are four first-order equations:

$$\text{(i)}\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0,\qquad\text{(iii)}\ \nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},$$
$$\text{(ii)}\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad\text{(iv)}\ \nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}\tag{9.40}$$

These are coupled. $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$ appear in each other’s equations. The book decouples them.

2. Decoupling

Take the curl of (iii) and of (iv). For (iii):

$$\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\times\mathbf B)=-\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}$$

The last step uses (iv). Then (i) removes the divergence term, and the same method applied to (iv) gives the equation for $\mathbf B$:

$$\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2},\qquad\nabla^2\mathbf B=\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\mathbf B}{\partial t^2}\tag{9.41}$$

Each equation is second order and decoupled, but the price is that the equations are second order. Each Cartesian component of $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$ satisfies the three-dimensional wave equation $\nabla^2f=\frac{1}{v^2}\partial^2f/\partial t^2$, which generalises Eq. 9.2 with $\partial^2f/\partial z^2$ replaced by $\nabla^2f$.

Plus Two recall: speed of light from $\epsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$

Recall. From Class 12: electromagnetic waves travel in vacuum at $c=1/\sqrt{\epsilon_0\mu_0}$, the same as the speed of light.

Re-derive.

  1. Start from the wave equation Eq. 9.41 for $\mathbf E$: $\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu_0\epsilon_0\,\partial^2\mathbf E/\partial t^2$.
  2. For a wave $f(z-vt)$, the same equation with $\partial^2f/\partial z^2=(1/v^2)\partial^2f/\partial t^2$ (Eq. 9.2) gives $1/v^2=\mu_0\epsilon_0$.
  3. Therefore $v=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}$.

Saved. $c=1/\sqrt{\epsilon_0\mu_0}$ Eq. 9.43.

3. The speed

Comparing Eq. 9.41 with the wave equation gives the speed of the waves in vacuum:

Result 9.5: speed of electromagnetic waves in vacuum
$$v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon_0\mu_0}}=3.00\times10^8\ \text{m/s}\tag{9.42}$$
$$c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon_0\mu_0}}\tag{9.43}$$

This is the measured speed of light, so light is an electromagnetic wave (footnote 6).

4. Why this matters

Maxwell’s contribution to Ampère’s law, the term $\mu_0\epsilon_0\,\partial\mathbf E/\partial t$, is what produces the wave equation. Without it, the equations would not admit waves. The constants $\epsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$ are measured with charges, batteries and wires, and yet they give $c$. The book calls this the crucial role of displacement current in the theory of light.
