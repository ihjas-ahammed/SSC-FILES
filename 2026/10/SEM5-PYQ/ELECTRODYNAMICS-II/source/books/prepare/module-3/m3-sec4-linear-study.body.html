1. Maxwell’s equations in a linear medium

Inside matter, with no free charge or free current, Maxwell’s equations are Eq. 9.66:

$$\text{(i)}\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf D=0,\qquad\text{(iii)}\ \nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},$$
$$\text{(ii)}\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad\text{(iv)}\ \nabla\times\mathbf H=\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}\tag{9.66}$$

For a linear medium, $\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf H=\mathbf B/\mu$ Eq. 9.67. If the medium is also homogeneous, so $\epsilon$ and $\mu$ are constant, the equations take the vacuum form with $\mu_0\epsilon_0$ replaced by $\mu\epsilon$ Eq. 9.68:

$$\text{(i)}\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0,\qquad\text{(iii)}\ \nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},$$
$$\text{(ii)}\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad\text{(iv)}\ \nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}\tag{9.68}$$

Recall. From Class 12: the refractive index of a medium is $n=c/v$, the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to its speed in the medium.

Re-derive.

  1. From Eq. 9.68 the wave equation has $\mu\epsilon$ in place of $\mu_0\epsilon_0$.
  2. Comparing with the vacuum wave equation gives $v^2=1/\mu\epsilon$, so $v=1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$.
  3. Write $v=c/n$; then $n=c/v=\sqrt{\mu\epsilon/\mu_0\epsilon_0}$.

Saved. $v=c/n$ Eq. 9.69 with $n$ as in Eq. 9.70.

$$v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}}=\frac{c}{n},\qquad n\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\mu}{\epsilon_0\mu_0}}\tag{9.69}$$

$n$ is the index of refraction Eq. 9.70. Most materials have $\mu\approx\mu_0$, so

$$n\approx\sqrt{\epsilon_r}\tag{9.71}$$

where $\epsilon_r$ is the dielectric constant (Eq. 4.34). Since $\epsilon_r>1$ for almost all materials, light travels more slowly in matter.

Footnote 13 (book): the field inside a material is the sum of the original field and the fields of the molecular dipoles it induces. Together they make a single wave with the same frequency and a different speed. This “Ewald–Oseen extinction theorem” explains transparency. It depends on linearity.

Footnote 14 (book): $\epsilon_r$ is independent of the amplitude of $\mathbf E$, but it may depend on frequency. The static dielectric constant of water gives $n=8.9$, which is far from the visible-light value $n\approx1.33$.

3. Energy and the Poynting vector in a medium

All earlier results carry over with $\epsilon_0\to\epsilon$, $\mu_0\to\mu$, $c\to v$. The energy density (footnote 15: see Prob. 8.27) and the Poynting vector are

$$u=\frac12\left(\epsilon E^2+\frac1\mu B^2\right)\tag{9.72}$$
$$\mathbf S=\frac1\mu(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)\tag{9.73}$$

For a monochromatic plane wave, $\omega=kv$ Eq. 9.11; $B_0=E_0/v$ (9.48 with $c\to v$); and the intensity, the average power per unit area, is

$$I=\frac12\epsilon vE_0^2\tag{9.74}$$

Footnote 16: the momentum carried by a wave in matter is a matter of current debate.

4. Boundary conditions between two media

Chapter 7 gives the boundary conditions at an interface (Eq. 7.74). In the form used here Eq. 9.75: the normal component of $\epsilon E$ and the normal component of $B$ are continuous, and so are the tangential component of $\mathbf E$ and of $\mathbf H=\mathbf B/\mu$:

$$\text{(i)}\ \epsilon_1E_1^\perp=\epsilon_2E_2^\perp,\qquad\text{(iii)}\ \mathbf E_1^\parallel=\mathbf E_2^\parallel,$$
$$\text{(ii)}\ B_1^\perp=B_2^\perp,\qquad\text{(iv)}\ \frac{1}{\mu_1}\mathbf B_1^\parallel=\frac{1}{\mu_2}\mathbf B_2^\parallel\tag{9.75}$$

5. Normal incidence (§9.3.2)

Let the $xy$ plane be the interface, with medium 1 on the left ($z<0$) and medium 2 on the right. A plane wave of frequency $\omega$, polarized in $x$, travels in the $+z$ direction and meets the interface head on (Fig. 9.13):

$$\tilde{\mathbf E}_I=\tilde E_{0I}e^{i(k_1z-\omega t)}\hat x,\qquad\tilde{\mathbf B}_I=\frac{1}{v_1}\tilde E_{0I}e^{i(k_1z-\omega t)}\hat y\tag{9.76}$$
{{FIG:m3-sec4-normal}}

A reflected wave goes back into medium 1. Its $\mathbf B$ has the opposite sign, as Eq. 9.50 requires (the Poynting vector points along the direction of propagation):

$$\tilde{\mathbf E}_R=\tilde E_{0R}e^{i(-k_1z-\omega t)}\hat x,\qquad\tilde{\mathbf B}_R=-\frac{1}{v_1}\tilde E_{0R}e^{i(-k_1z-\omega t)}\hat y\tag{9.77}$$

A transmitted wave goes on into medium 2:

$$\tilde{\mathbf E}_T=\tilde E_{0T}e^{i(k_2z-\omega t)}\hat x,\qquad\tilde{\mathbf B}_T=\frac{1}{v_2}\tilde E_{0T}e^{i(k_2z-\omega t)}\hat y\tag{9.78}$$

At $z=0$ there are no components perpendicular to the surface, so (i) and (ii) are trivial. Condition (iii) gives

$$\tilde E_{0I}+\tilde E_{0R}=\tilde E_{0T}\tag{9.79}$$

and (iv) gives

$$\frac{1}{\mu_1}\left(\frac{1}{v_1}\tilde E_{0I}-\frac{1}{v_1}\tilde E_{0R}\right)=\frac{1}{\mu_2}\frac{1}{v_2}\tilde E_{0T}\tag{9.80}$$

i.e. $\tilde E_{0I}-\tilde E_{0R}=\beta\tilde E_{0T}$ Eq. 9.81, with

$$\beta\equiv\frac{\mu_1v_1}{\mu_2v_2}=\frac{\mu_1n_2}{\mu_2n_1}\tag{9.82}$$

Solving Eq. 9.79 and Eq. 9.81:

$$\tilde E_{0R}=\left(\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}\right)\tilde E_{0I},\qquad\tilde E_{0T}=\left(\frac{2}{1+\beta}\right)\tilde E_{0I}\tag{9.83}$$

For $\mu\approx\mu_0$, $\beta=v_1/v_2$, and these become the string results Eq. 9.84. The real amplitudes are then Eq. 9.85, or in terms of indices Eq. 9.86:

$$\tilde E_{0R}=\left(\frac{v_2-v_1}{v_2+v_1}\right)\tilde E_{0I},\qquad\tilde E_{0T}=\left(\frac{2v_2}{v_2+v_1}\right)\tilde E_{0I}\tag{9.84}$$
$$E_{0R}=\left|\frac{n_1-n_2}{n_1+n_2}\right|E_{0I},\qquad E_{0T}=\left(\frac{2n_1}{n_1+n_2}\right)E_{0I}\tag{9.86}$$
$$E_{0R}=\left|\frac{v_2-v_1}{v_2+v_1}\right|E_{0I},\qquad E_{0T}=\left(\frac{2v_2}{v_2+v_1}\right)E_{0I}\tag{9.85}$$

6. Reflection and transmission coefficients

The intensity is Eq. 9.74. With $\mu_1=\mu_2=\mu_0$, the reflection and transmission coefficients (fractions of incident intensity) are

$$R\equiv\frac{I_R}{I_I}=\left(\frac{E_{0R}}{E_{0I}}\right)^2=\left(\frac{n_1-n_2}{n_1+n_2}\right)^2\tag{9.87}$$
$$T\equiv\frac{I_T}{I_I}=\frac{\epsilon_2v_2}{\epsilon_1v_1}\left(\frac{E_{0T}}{E_{0I}}\right)^2=\frac{4n_1n_2}{(n_1+n_2)^2}\tag{9.88}$$

Energy conservation requires

$$R+T=1\tag{9.89}$$

For air ($n_1=1$) to glass ($n_2=1.5$): $R=0.04$ and $T=0.96$. Most of the light is transmitted.
