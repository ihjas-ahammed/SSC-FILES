9.1.1–9.1.2 The wave equation and sinusoidal waves

Source: book-sources/m3-sec2-wave1d.html, Griffiths pp. 390–396. Equations 9.1–9.20 are included in full. Problems 9.1–9.4 are listed but not worked.

Before you start

  • Partial derivatives and the chain rule (pre-math M7): if $f$ depends on $z-vt$, then $\partial f/\partial t=-v\,\partial f/\partial z$.
  • Euler’s formula $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ (pre-math M5). It is needed for Section 9.1.2(ii).
  • Newton’s second law for a small piece of string (Plus Two mechanics).

1. What a wave is

A wave is a disturbance that moves through a medium with its shape fixed and its speed constant. The book adds that real waves may spread, fade or change shape, but the simplest case is a fixed shape moving at constant speed in one dimension (Fig. 9.1).

{{FIG:m3-sec2-shift}}

Call the shape at $t=0$ $g(z)=f(z,0)$. At a later time $t$, each point has moved a distance $vt$ to the right. So the displacement at $z$ at time $t$ equals the displacement at $z-vt$ at time 0:

$$f(z,t)=f(z-vt,0)=g(z-vt)\tag{9.1}$$

A function of $z$ and $t$ that depends only on the combination $z-vt$ describes a wave of fixed shape moving to the right at speed $v$. The book’s examples $f_1$, $f_2$, $f_3$ are waves, because each depends on $z-vt$ alone. The examples $f_4$ and $f_5$ are not, because they do not.

2. Where the wave equation comes from

Take a taut string under tension $T$, with mass $\mu$ per unit length. A short piece between $z$ and $z+\Delta z$ feels a net transverse force from the tension. For small displacements the angles are small, so $\sin\theta\approx\tan\theta=\partial f/\partial z$. The net force is

$$\Delta F=T\sin\theta'-T\sin\theta\approx T\left(\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right|_{z+\Delta z}-\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right|_{z}\right)\approx T\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial z^2}\Delta z$$

Newton’s second law for that piece gives $\Delta F=\mu\,\Delta z\,\partial^2f/\partial t^2$. Equating the two and rearranging:

$$\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial z^2}=\frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial t^2}\tag{9.2}$$

with the speed of propagation

$$v=\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}\tag{9.3}$$

Equation 9.2 is the wave equation. Its solutions are the functions of $z-vt$.

3. Solutions of the wave equation

Let $u=z-vt$ and $f=g(u)$. The chain rule gives

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}=\frac{dg}{du},\qquad\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=-v\frac{dg}{du},\qquad\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z^2}=\frac{d^2g}{du^2},\qquad\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial t^2}=v^2\frac{d^2g}{du^2}$$

Substituting into Eq. 9.2 shows that $f=g(z-vt)$ satisfies it for any differentiable $g$. The same argument shows that $h(z+vt)$ satisfies it too:

$$f(z,t)=g(z-vt)\tag{9.4}$$
$$f(z,t)=h(z+vt)\tag{9.5}$$

The wave equation is linear, so the sum of two solutions is also a solution. The most general solution is therefore a wave to the right plus a wave to the left:

$$f(z,t)=g(z-vt)+h(z+vt)\tag{9.6}$$

The book notes that every solution has this form. The class of solutions is much larger than for an ordinary second-order equation, because the wave equation is a partial differential equation.

4. Sinusoidal waves

(i) Terminology. The most familiar wave is the cosine:

$$f(z,t)=A\cos[k(z-vt)+\delta]\tag{9.7}$$

$A$ is the amplitude, the maximum displacement. The argument of the cosine is the phase; $\delta$ is the phase constant. The wave is delayed by a distance $\delta/k$. The wave number $k$ gives the wavelength:

$$\lambda=\frac{2\pi}{k}\tag{9.8}$$

The period, the time for one complete oscillation at a fixed point, is

$$T=\frac{2\pi}{kv}\tag{9.9}$$

{{FIG:m3-sec2-sinusoid}}

Plus Two recall: frequency, wavelength and speed

Recall. From Class 12: for a wave, speed $=$ frequency $\times$ wavelength.

Re-derive.

  1. In one period $T$ the wave moves forward one wavelength $\lambda$.
  2. So $v=\lambda/T$, and the frequency is $\nu=1/T$.
  3. Substituting $T$ from Eq. 9.9 gives $\nu=kv/2\pi$.

Saved. $\nu=\dfrac{kv}{2\pi}=\dfrac{v}{\lambda}=\dfrac1T$ Eq. 9.10.

The frequency is the number of oscillations per second:

$$\nu=\frac{kv}{2\pi}=\frac{v}{\lambda}=\frac{1}{T}\tag{9.10}$$

It is more convenient to use the angular frequency $\omega=2\pi\nu$. It is the number of radians swept per second, by analogy with circular motion:

$$\omega=2\pi\nu=kv\tag{9.11}$$

Written in terms of $\omega$, the wave is

$$f(z,t)=A\cos(kz-\omega t+\delta)\tag{9.12}$$

A wave going to the left is

$$f(z,t)=A\cos(kz+\omega t-\delta)\tag{9.13}$$

Because the cosine is even, this can also be written $A\cos(-kz-\omega t+\delta)$ Eq. 9.14. Reversing the sign of $k$ therefore reverses the direction of travel and nothing else.

(ii) Complex notation. Euler’s formula $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ Eq. 9.15 lets the wave be written as the real part of an exponential:

$$f(z,t)=\mathrm{Re}\left[Ae^{i(kz-\omega t+\delta)}\right]\tag{9.16}$$

Define the complex wave function and the complex amplitude $\tilde A=Ae^{i\delta}$:

$$\tilde f(z,t)=\tilde Ae^{i(kz-\omega t)}\tag{9.17}$$
$$f(z,t)=\mathrm{Re}[\tilde f(z,t)]\tag{9.18}$$

Example 9.1. Two waves with the same $k$ and $\omega$ add by adding their complex amplitudes, $\tilde A_3=\tilde A_1+\tilde A_2$, which gives

$$A_3e^{i\delta_3}=A_1e^{i\delta_1}+A_2e^{i\delta_2}\tag{9.19}$$

The real part of the sum is the sum of the real parts, so this works. It does not work for products: $\mathrm{Re}(\tilde f_1\tilde f_2)\neq\mathrm{Re}(\tilde f_1)\,\mathrm{Re}(\tilde f_2)$. Quadratic quantities such as energy have to be handled with the real fields.

(iii) Linear combinations. Any wave can be written as a sum of sinusoidal waves over all $k$ (the book’s Fourier representation):

$$\tilde f(z,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde A(k)e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\,dk\tag{9.20}$$

The coefficient $\tilde A(k)$ comes from the initial shape and velocity (Prob. 9.34). Here $k$ runs over negative values as well, to include both directions. The book says that from here on only sinusoidal waves are needed: if you know how one sinusoidal wave behaves, you know how any wave behaves.
