9.2.1 The wave equation for E and B

Source: book-sources/m3-sec3-wave-eq.html and the end of m3-sec2-polar.html, Griffiths pp. 401–402. Equations 9.40–9.43 in full.

Prerequisites

  • Maxwell’s equations in vacuum (Module 2, Section 7.3).
  • Identity $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf A)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)-\nabla^2\mathbf A$ (pre-math M13).

1. Maxwell’s equations in empty space

$$\text{(i)}\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0,\qquad\text{(iii)}\ \nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},$$
$$\text{(ii)}\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad\text{(iv)}\ \nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}\tag{9.40}$$

2. Uncoupling

The curl of (iii), using (iv) and (i):

$$\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E=-\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}$$

The curl of (iv), using (iii) and (ii), gives the same form for $\mathbf B$. Hence

Result 9.6: decoupled wave equations
$$\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2},\qquad\nabla^2\mathbf B=\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\mathbf B}{\partial t^2}\tag{9.41}$$

These are second-order equations. Each component satisfies $\nabla^2f=(1/v^2)\,\partial^2f/\partial t^2$.

3. Speed

Plus Two recall: speed of light from $\epsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$

Recall. From Class 12: electromagnetic waves travel in vacuum at $c=1/\sqrt{\epsilon_0\mu_0}$.

Re-derive.

  1. For a wave $f(z-vt)$, Eq. 9.2 gives $\partial^2f/\partial z^2=(1/v^2)\partial^2f/\partial t^2$.
  2. Comparing with Eq. 9.41 gives $1/v^2=\mu_0\epsilon_0$.
  3. So $v=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}$.

Saved. $c=1/\sqrt{\epsilon_0\mu_0}$ Eq. 9.43.

Result 9.7: speed in vacuum
$$v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon_0\mu_0}}=3.00\times10^8\ \text{m/s}\tag{9.42}$$
$$c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon_0\mu_0}}\tag{9.43}$$

The number is the measured speed of light, so light is an electromagnetic wave (footnote 6 of the book).

4. Role of the displacement current

The term $\mu_0\epsilon_0\,\partial\mathbf E/\partial t$ in (iv) is what makes waves possible. Without it there is no wave equation. The constants $\epsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$ are measured with static charges and steady currents, which have nothing to do with light, yet they fix $c$.
