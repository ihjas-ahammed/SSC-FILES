8.1.2 Poynting’s theorem

Source: book-sources/m3-s812-poynting.html, Griffiths pp. 363–367. Equations 8.5–8.12 and Example 8.1 are included in full. Problems 8.1–8.4 are not included.

Before you start

  • The divergence theorem: $\oint_S\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf a=\int_{\mathcal V}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)\,d\tau$ (Beyond in Plus Two; see pre-math M13).
  • The product rule for a divergence: $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)=\mathbf B\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf E)-\mathbf E\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)$.
  • Faraday’s law and the Ampère–Maxwell law, in the forms given in Module 2 (Section 7.3).

1. The energy stored in the fields

Earlier in the book, two results were found for the work needed to build up a field. To assemble a static charge distribution, the work is $W_e=\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$ (Eq. 2.45). To get currents flowing against the back emf, the work is $W_m=\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int B^2\,d\tau$ (Eq. 7.44). Both are integrals of a density, so the energy per unit volume must be:

$$u=\frac12\left(\epsilon_0E^2+\frac{1}{\mu_0}B^2\right)\tag{8.5}$$

Section 8.1.2 checks this, and then builds the energy conservation law from it.

2. Work done on the charges

Take a charge and current distribution that makes fields $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$ at time $t$. In the next instant $dt$ the charges move a little. The Lorentz force does work on each charge. The magnetic part does no work, because $\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ is at right angles to $\mathbf v$. So the work on a charge $q$ is

$$dW=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v\,dt$$

To pass from one charge to the whole volume, replace $q$ by $\rho\,d\tau$ and $\rho\mathbf v$ by $\mathbf J$. The rate at which work is done on all the charges in $\mathcal V$ is then

$$\frac{dW}{dt}=\int_{\mathcal V}(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf J)\,d\tau\tag{8.6}$$

The quantity $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf J$ is the power per unit volume that the fields deliver to the charges.

3. Eliminating $\mathbf J$

Write $\mathbf J$ in terms of the fields, using the Ampère–Maxwell law. That gives

$$\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf J=\frac{1}{\mu_0}\mathbf E\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)-\epsilon_0\,\mathbf E\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}$$

The first term still contains $\nabla\times\mathbf B$. Use the product rule, and then Faraday’s law, to rewrite it:

$$\mathbf E\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)=-\mathbf B\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)$$

Two of the terms now have the form of a time derivative. Since $\mathbf B\cdot\partial\mathbf B/\partial t=\tfrac12\partial(B^2)/\partial t$, and similarly for $\mathbf E$:

$$\mathbf B\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}=\frac12\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(B^2),\qquad\mathbf E\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}=\frac12\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(E^2)\tag{8.7}$$

Putting these together:

$$\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf J=-\frac12\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\epsilon_0E^2+\frac{1}{\mu_0}B^2\right)-\frac{1}{\mu_0}\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)\tag{8.8}$$

The first term is minus the time rate of change of the energy density $u$ (Eq. 8.5). The second term is a divergence. So part of the power delivered to the charges comes from a fall in the stored energy, and part comes from a divergence term that describes flow through the surface.

4. Poynting’s theorem

Substitute Eq. 8.8 into Eq. 8.6. Apply the divergence theorem to the second term, which turns the volume integral into a surface integral over the boundary $S$ of $\mathcal V$:

$$\frac{dW}{dt}=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal V}u\,d\tau-\frac{1}{\mu_0}\oint_S(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\mathbf a\tag{8.9}$$

In words: the work done on the charges equals the fall in energy stored in the fields, minus the energy that flows out through the surface. This is Poynting’s theorem. It is the work–energy theorem for electrodynamics.

Plus Two recall: power in a circuit

Recall. From Class 12: the power delivered to a resistor is $P=VI=I^2R$, the rate of Joule heating.

Re-derive.

  1. Power is energy per unit time. For a resistor, the energy per unit time that becomes heat is the work done on the charges per unit time.
  2. Using $V=IR$, $VI=I^2R$ is the same quantity written in two ways.
  3. So the power of Poynting’s theorem, which the fields deliver to the charges in a wire, must reproduce $VI$ when we check it with a real wire (Example 8.1 below).

Saved. $P=VI$. This is the result that Example 8.1 reproduces from the Poynting vector.

5. The Poynting vector

The surface integral in Eq. 8.9 is the energy that leaves the volume per unit time. Define the vector

$$\mathbf S\equiv\frac{1}{\mu_0}(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)\tag{8.10}$$

$\mathbf S$ is the Poynting vector. It is the energy per unit time crossing unit area: the energy flux density. Then $\mathbf S\cdot d\mathbf a$ is the energy crossing an element of area $d\mathbf a$ in unit time.

Two cautions from the book (footnote 4). First, Eq. 8.9 shows that $\oint\mathbf S\cdot d\mathbf a$ is the power through a closed surface. It does not prove that $\int\mathbf S\cdot d\mathbf a$ is the power through an open surface, because a term could integrate to zero over every closed surface. Second, where energy is located is not fixed in electrodynamics (Section 2.4.4). The Poynting vector is the natural interpretation, not a proof of location.

With Eq. 8.10, Poynting’s theorem takes a compact form:

$$\frac{dW}{dt}=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal V}u\,d\tau-\oint_S\mathbf S\cdot d\mathbf a\tag{8.11}$$

6. Local conservation of energy

Take a region with no charge, so $dW/dt=0$. Then Eq. 8.11 gives $\partial_t\!\int u\,d\tau=-\oint\mathbf S\cdot d\mathbf a$. Apply the divergence theorem to the right side, and then, because the volume is arbitrary, the integrands must match:

Result 8.1: local energy conservation in empty space
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf S\tag{8.12}$$

This has the same form as the continuity equation for charge (Eq. 8.4). $u$ plays the part of $\rho$, and $\mathbf S$ plays the part of $\mathbf J$.

In general, electromagnetic energy by itself is not conserved. The fields do work on the charges, and the charges make fields. Energy moves back and forth between them, so the total energy of matter plus fields is what is conserved.

7. Example 8.1: a resistive wire

Current flows down a resistive wire of length $L$ and radius $a$. The book uses the Poynting vector to find the power delivered to the wire, and checks it against $VI$.

Step 1. The potential difference between the ends is $V$. The field inside the wire is uniform and points along the wire: $E=V/L$.

Step 2. The current $I$ produces a circumferential magnetic field. At the surface, $B=\mu_0I/2\pi a$ (Ampère’s law).

Step 3. The two fields are at right angles. So the magnitude of the Poynting vector is

$$S=\frac{1}{\mu_0}\cdot\frac{V}{L}\cdot\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi a}=\frac{VI}{2\pi aL}$$

Step 4. $\mathbf E$ runs along the wire and $\mathbf B$ goes round it, so $\mathbf E\times\mathbf B$ points radially. Its direction is radially inward, toward the axis of the wire.

Step 5. The surface of the wire has area $2\pi aL$. The energy per unit time crossing it inward is

$$\int\mathbf S\cdot d\mathbf a=S(2\pi aL)=VI$$

This is exactly the Joule heating $VI$ found in Section 7.1.1. The energy comes into the wire through its surface, from the fields outside it, rather than along the wire.
