9.4 Absorption and dispersion (background)

Source: book-sources/m3-sec4-oblique.html (the first sheet, p. 418), m3-bg-absorption.html, Griffiths pp. 418–430. Background: beyond the syllabus list. Equations 9.119–9.176 are given in full. Before you start

  • Complex numbers, $\tilde k=k+i\kappa$, and the real part (Section 9.1.2).
  • Ohm’s law $\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E$ (Module 1 and Plus Two current electricity).
  • Binomial expansion $\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}\approx1+\tfrac12\varepsilon$ (pre-math M2).

1. Conductors (§9.4.1)

In a conductor the free current is not zero. With Ohm’s law,

$$\mathbf J_f=\sigma\mathbf E\tag{9.119}$$

Maxwell’s equations for a linear conductor take the form Eq. 9.120, after using $\rho_f$ and Gauss’s law:

$$\text{(i)}\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\rho_f,\qquad\text{(iii)}\ \nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},$$
$$\text{(ii)}\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad\text{(iv)}\ \nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu\sigma\mathbf E+\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}\tag{9.120}$$

The continuity equation for free charge is $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J_f=-\partial\rho_f/\partial t$ Eq. 9.121. With Ohm’s law and Gauss’s law it gives $\rho_f(t)=e^{-(\sigma/\epsilon)t}\rho_f(0)$ Eq. 9.122. So any free charge disappears in a time $\tau=\epsilon/\sigma$, and once it has gone, $\rho_f=0$. Footnote 20 explains why $\tau$ for good conductors is not the real timescale.

With $\rho_f=0$ the equations reduce to Eq. 9.123, and the extra term in (iv) is the only difference from the non-conducting case:

$$\text{(i)}\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0,\qquad\text{(iii)}\ \nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},$$
$$\text{(ii)}\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad\text{(iv)}\ \nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}+\mu\sigma\mathbf E\tag{9.123}$$

Applying the curl to (iii) and (iv) gives modified wave equations Eq. 9.124:

$$\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}+\mu\sigma\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t},\qquad\nabla^2\mathbf B=\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf B}{\partial t^2}+\mu\sigma\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}\tag{9.124}$$

Plane waves $\tilde{\mathbf E}=\tilde{\mathbf E}_0e^{i(\tilde kz-\omega t)}$ Eq. 9.125 solve these, provided

$$\tilde k^2=\mu\epsilon\omega^2+i\mu\sigma\omega\tag{9.126}$$

Writing $\tilde k=k+i\kappa$ Eq. 9.127, the real and imaginary parts are Eq. 9.128:

$$k\equiv\omega\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\mu}{2}}\left[\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon\omega}\right)^2}+1\right]^{1/2},\qquad\kappa\equiv\omega\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\mu}{2}}\left[\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon\omega}\right)^2}-1\right]^{1/2}\tag{9.128}$$

Check: for $\sigma=0$, $\kappa=0$ and $k=\omega\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}$, the vacuum-type result of §9.3.1.

The imaginary part gives attenuation Eq. 9.129:

$$\tilde{\mathbf E}(z,t)=\tilde{\mathbf E}_0e^{-\kappa z}e^{i(kz-\omega t)},\qquad\tilde{\mathbf B}(z,t)=\tilde{\mathbf B}_0e^{-\kappa z}e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\tag{9.129}$$

The skin depth is $d=1/\kappa$ Eq. 9.130, the distance over which the amplitude falls by $1/e$. The wavelength, speed and index are Eq. 9.131:

$$\lambda=\frac{2\pi}{k},\qquad v=\frac{\omega}{k},\qquad n=\frac{ck}{\omega}\tag{9.131}$$

Maxwell’s equations fix the polarization: $\mathbf E$ along $x$ Eq. 9.132, and $\mathbf B$ along $y$ with amplitude $\tilde k/\omega$ times that of $\mathbf E$ Eq. 9.133. Writing $\tilde k=Ke^{i\phi}$ Eq. 9.134 with

$$K\equiv|\tilde k|=\sqrt{k^2+\kappa^2}=\omega\sqrt{\epsilon\mu\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon\omega}\right)^2}} \tag{9.135}$$
$$\phi\equiv\tan^{-1}(\kappa/k)\tag{9.136}$$

the complex amplitudes are related by $B_0e^{i\delta_B}=(Ke^{i\phi}/\omega)E_0e^{i\delta_E}$ Eq. 9.137. Hence the magnetic field lags the electric field by $\phi$, and

$$\delta_B-\delta_E=\phi\tag{9.138}$$
$$\frac{B_0}{E_0}=\frac{K}{\omega}=\sqrt{\epsilon\mu\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon\omega}\right)^2}}\tag{9.139}$$
$$\mathbf E(z,t)=E_0e^{-\kappa z}\cos(kz-\omega t+\delta_E)\hat x,\qquad\mathbf B(z,t)=B_0e^{-\kappa z}\cos(kz-\omega t+\delta_E+\phi)\hat y\tag{9.140}$$

Figure 9.18 shows these fields. Problems 9.20–9.22 (skin depth in water and in metal, the phase lag, and the energy density) are listed, not worked.

2. Reflection at a conducting surface (§9.4.2)

At a surface with free charges and currents the boundary conditions are Eq. 9.141:

$$\text{(i)}\ \epsilon_1E_1^\perp-\epsilon_2E_2^\perp=\sigma_f,\qquad\text{(iii)}\ \mathbf E_1^\parallel-\mathbf E_2^\parallel=0,$$
$$\text{(ii)}\ B_1^\perp-B_2^\perp=0,\qquad\text{(iv)}\ \frac{1}{\mu_1}\mathbf B_1^\parallel-\frac{1}{\mu_2}\mathbf B_2^\parallel=\mathbf K_f\times\hat{\mathbf n}\tag{9.141}$$

For an ohmic conductor there is no free surface current (footnote 21). Take the incident, reflected and transmitted waves Eq. 9.142–Eq. 9.144, with the transmitted wave using $\tilde k_2$:

$$\tilde{\mathbf E}_I=\tilde E_{0I}e^{i(k_1z-\omega t)}\hat x,\qquad\tilde{\mathbf B}_I=\frac{1}{v_1}\tilde E_{0I}e^{i(k_1z-\omega t)}\hat y\tag{9.142}$$
$$\tilde{\mathbf E}_R=\tilde E_{0R}e^{i(-k_1z-\omega t)}\hat x,\qquad\tilde{\mathbf B}_R=-\frac{1}{v_1}\tilde E_{0R}e^{i(-k_1z-\omega t)}\hat y\tag{9.143}$$
$$\tilde{\mathbf E}_T=\tilde E_{0T}e^{i(\tilde k_2z-\omega t)}\hat x,\qquad\tilde{\mathbf B}_T=\frac{\tilde k_2}{\omega}\tilde E_{0T}e^{i(\tilde k_2z-\omega t)}\hat y\tag{9.144}$$

Conditions (iii) and (iv) at $z=0$ give Eq. 9.145 and Eq. 9.146, and then Eq. 9.147 with $\tilde\beta$ defined in Eq. 9.148:

$$\tilde E_{0I}+\tilde E_{0R}=\tilde E_{0T}\tag{9.145}$$
$$\frac{1}{\mu_1v_1}(\tilde E_{0I}-\tilde E_{0R})-\frac{\tilde k_2}{\mu_2\omega}\tilde E_{0T}=0\tag{9.146}$$
$$\tilde E_{0I}-\tilde E_{0R}=\tilde\beta\tilde E_{0T},\qquad\tilde\beta\equiv\frac{\mu_1v_1}{\mu_2\omega}\tilde k_2\tag{9.147}$$

Eq. 9.147 has the form of Eq. 9.81 with a complex $\tilde\beta$. Solving:

$$\tilde E_{0R}=\left(\frac{1-\tilde\beta}{1+\tilde\beta}\right)\tilde E_{0I},\qquad\tilde E_{0T}=\left(\frac{2}{1+\tilde\beta}\right)\tilde E_{0I}\tag{9.149}$$

For a perfect conductor, $\tilde k_2\to\infty$ and $\tilde\beta\to\infty$, so $\tilde E_{0R}=-\tilde E_{0I}$ and $\tilde E_{0T}=0$ Eq. 9.150. The wave is totally reflected with a $180^\circ$ phase shift; this is why good conductors make mirrors. Problem 9.23 (reflection from silver) is listed, not worked.

3. Frequency dependence of permittivity (§9.4.3)

Dispersion means the speed depends on frequency. The phase velocity is $v=\omega/k$ Eq. 9.151, and the group velocity of a packet is $v_g=d\omega/dk$ Eq. 9.152. The group velocity is the speed of the envelope and of the energy. Footnotes 23 and 24 give references, and the book notes that $v_g$ can exceed $c$ in special cases.

Model: each electron in a molecule is a damped oscillator with spring force $-m\omega_0^2x$ Eq. 9.153 and damping force $-m\gamma\,dx/dt$ Eq. 9.154. Under the wave’s field $E_0\cos\omega t$, the driving force is

$$F_{\rm driving}=qE=qE_0\cos(\omega t)\tag{9.155}$$

and the electron obeys Eq. 9.156. In complex form Eq. 9.157, the steady state is $\tilde x=\tilde x_0e^{-i\omega t}$ Eq. 9.158, so

$$\tilde x_0=\frac{q/m}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega}E_0\tag{9.159}$$

The dipole moment is $\tilde p=q\tilde x$ Eq. 9.160. Summing over $f_j$ electrons per molecule at frequency $\omega_j$ and damping $\gamma_j$, with $N$ molecules per unit volume, gives the polarization Eq. 9.161, the complex susceptibility Eq. 9.162, and

$$\tilde\epsilon_r=1+\frac{Nq^2}{m\epsilon_0}\sum_j\frac{f_j}{\omega_j^2-\omega^2-i\gamma_j\omega}\tag{9.163}$$

The complex wave equation Eq. 9.164 has the plane-wave solution Eq. 9.165 with $\tilde k=\sqrt{\tilde\epsilon\mu_0}\,\omega$ Eq. 9.166, and writing $\tilde k=k+i\kappa$ Eq. 9.167 gives the attenuated wave Eq. 9.168. The absorption coefficient is $\alpha=2\kappa$ Eq. 9.169, and $n=ck/\omega$ Eq. 9.170.

For a dilute gas, using $\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}\approx1+\tfrac12\varepsilon$, the complex wave number is Eq. 9.171, and the real and imaginary parts give Eq. 9.172 and Eq. 9.173:

$$n\approx1+\frac{Nq^2}{2m\epsilon_0}\sum_j\frac{f_j(\omega_j^2-\omega^2)}{(\omega_j^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma_j^2\omega^2}\tag{9.172}$$
$$\alpha\approx\frac{Nq^2\omega^2}{m\epsilon_0c}\sum_j\frac{f_j\gamma_j}{(\omega_j^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma_j^2\omega^2}\tag{9.173}$$

Away from resonance, $n$ rises with frequency (normal dispersion). Near a resonance $n$ falls sharply (anomalous dispersion), and the absorption is largest there (Fig. 9.22). Far from resonance, with damping ignored, $n=1+\frac{Nq^2}{2m\epsilon_0}\sum_j f_j/(\omega_j^2-\omega^2)$ Eq. 9.174. For $\omega<\omega_j$ this becomes Eq. 9.175, and in terms of the vacuum wavelength, Cauchy’s formula Eq. 9.176: $n=1+A(1+B/\lambda^2)$.
