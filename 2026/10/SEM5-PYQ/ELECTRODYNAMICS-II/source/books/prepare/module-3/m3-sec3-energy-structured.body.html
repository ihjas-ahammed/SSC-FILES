9.2.3 Energy and momentum in electromagnetic waves

Source: book-sources/m3-sec3-energy.html, Griffiths pp. 406–408. Equations 9.54–9.65 in full; footnotes 9–11. Problem 9.10 described; Problems 9.11–9.13 listed, not worked.

Prerequisites

  • $u$ and $\mathbf S$ from Section 8.1.2 (Eqs. 8.5, 8.10); the momentum density result (Eq. 8.29).
  • Average of $\cos^2$ over a period, $\tfrac12$ (pre-math M4).

1. Energy density

$$u=\frac12\left(\epsilon_0E^2+\frac{1}{\mu_0}B^2\right)\tag{9.54}$$

For a monochromatic plane wave, $B^2=E^2/c^2=\mu_0\epsilon_0E^2$ Eq. 9.55. The two terms are equal:

Plus Two recall: electric energy density

Recall. From Class 12: a field $E$ in vacuum stores $\tfrac12\epsilon_0E^2$ per unit volume.

Re-derive.

  1. The magnetic term of Eq. 9.54 is $\tfrac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2$.
  2. With $B=E/c$ and $c^2=1/\epsilon_0\mu_0$, this is $\tfrac12\epsilon_0E^2$.
  3. The two halves are equal.

Saved.

$$u=\epsilon_0E^2=\epsilon_0E_0^2\cos^2(kz-\omega t+\delta)\tag{9.56}$$

2. Poynting vector of a plane wave

Result 9.9: energy flux
$$\mathbf S=\frac{1}{\mu_0}(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)\tag{9.57}$$
$$\mathbf S=c\epsilon_0E_0^2\cos^2(kz-\omega t+\delta)\hat z=cu\,\hat z\tag{9.58}$$

The flux equals $cu$ because in time $\Delta t$ the energy $uAc\Delta t$ crosses an area $A$.

3. Momentum density

$$\mathbf g=\frac{1}{c^2}\mathbf S\tag{9.59}$$
$$\mathbf g=\frac{1}{c}\epsilon_0E_0^2\cos^2(kz-\omega t+\delta)\hat z=\frac{1}{c}u\,\hat z\tag{9.60}$$

Footnote 9: $\mathrm{Re}(\tilde u)\neq u$ for the complex form, so energy densities are always computed from the real fields.

4. Averages and intensity

Because the period (about $10^{-15}$ s) is much shorter than any measurement, the averages over a cycle are used (footnote 10: $\langle\cos^2\rangle=\tfrac12$):

$$\langle u\rangle=\frac12\epsilon_0E_0^2\tag{9.61}$$
$$\langle\mathbf S\rangle=\frac12c\epsilon_0E_0^2\hat z\tag{9.62}$$
$$\langle\mathbf g\rangle=\frac{1}{2c}\epsilon_0E_0^2\hat z\tag{9.63}$$
Result 9.10: intensity
$$I\equiv\langle S\rangle=\frac12c\epsilon_0E_0^2\tag{9.64}$$

5. Radiation pressure

$$P=\frac{1}{A}\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}=\frac12\epsilon_0E_0^2=\frac{I}{c}\tag{9.65}$$

With $\Delta p=\langle g\rangle Ac\,\Delta t$ for a perfect absorber. A perfect reflector gives $2I/c$. The qualitative account with the Lorentz force is incomplete (footnote 11; Problem 9.11).
