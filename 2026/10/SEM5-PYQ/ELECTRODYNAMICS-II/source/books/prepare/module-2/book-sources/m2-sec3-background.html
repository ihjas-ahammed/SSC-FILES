I have written Maxwell’s equations in the traditional way, which emphasizes that they specify the divergence and curl of $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$. In this form, they reinforce the notion that electric fields can be produced either by charges ($\rho$) or by changing magnetic fields ($\partial\mathbf B/\partial t$), and magnetic fields can be produced either by currents ($\mathbf J$) or by changing electric fields ($\partial\mathbf E/\partial t$). Actually, this is misleading, because $\partial\mathbf B/\partial t$ and $\partial\mathbf E/\partial t$ are themselves due to charges and currents. I think it is logically preferable to write

(i)  $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\dfrac{1}{\epsilon_0}\rho,$(iii)  $\nabla\times\mathbf E+\dfrac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}=\mathbf 0,$(7.52)
(ii)  $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,$(iv)  $\nabla\times\mathbf B-\mu_0\epsilon_0\dfrac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}=\mu_0\mathbf J.$

This notation emphasizes that all electromagnetic fields are ultimately attributable to charges and currents. Maxwell’s equations tell you how charges produce fields; reciprocally, the force law tells you how fields affect charges.

7.3.4 Magnetic Charge

There is a pleasing symmetry to Maxwell’s equations; it is particularly striking in free space, where $\rho$ and $\mathbf J$ vanish:

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0,\qquad\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\qquad\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}.$$

7.4 The Field of a Rotating Magnet

Imagine an axially symmetric magnet rotating at constant angular velocity about its axis – a cylindrical bar magnet, if you like, or for that matter the Earth itself (pretending that the magnetic axis coincides with the rotation axis). It is natural to wonder whether its magnetic field is stationary, or rotates with the magnet (or, as Arlo Guthrie would say, “Maybe it doesn’t even matter”). Are the field lines “dragged along” by the rotation of the source? Faraday did a series of experiments (variations on the disk experiment of Ex. 7.4) that appeared to settle the issue. Mounting the magnet along the axis of the disk he first held the magnet still while he rotated the disk (that’s the original version), and a current was generated in the circuit (Fig. 7.51). Then he allowed the magnet to rotate at the same rate as the disk; again a current was generated. Finally, he held the disk at rest and rotated the magnet. This time no current flowed. He concluded

that the field of the rotating magnet is the same as that of the stationary magnet: that’s why the first two experiments give the same result, and the third is just what you’d get if the magnet were at rest. Apparently a magnet rotating about its axis of symmetry does not drag the field around as it spins; the field lines are stationary.34

And yet, if the magnet rotates about an axis perpendicular to its axis of symmetry, then it certainly does drag the field along with it. The field lines loop around from the north pole to the south pole, and that direction keeps changing. So not everyone accepted Faraday’s conclusion, until 1912 when experiments by S. J. Barnett finally led to a consensus that Faraday was right – in particular, the Earth’s magnetic field (or more precisely the part of it associated with rotation about the geographic north-south axis) does not rotate with the Earth.35

But what exactly does it mean to say that the field lines do (or do not) rotate with their source? Faraday had a very concrete conception of field lines as a network of “wires” (or cobwebs) existing “out there” in space. In that case, to say that they are moving (or not) makes perfect sense. But in Maxwell’s theory it is fields that have real physical existence, and field lines are just constructs we devise (by joining up the arrows that represent the fields themselves) to help us visualize (and draw) the fields. And we have already learned that even static magnetic field lines can be problematic (Section 5.3.4).

That would still be a perfectly good field-line pattern, representing exactly the same field. The labeling of the field lines is completely arbitrary, with no physical content at all. It’s not wrong, exactly (it describes the field correctly at every moment), it’s just dumb – a complicated and confusing way to think about something that is intrinsically simple and straightforward.

But suppose instead of a point charge we have a uniformly charged sphere, and it’s (uniformly) rotating. The electric field is exactly the same (for points outside), and it’s still static (because the charge density is constant in time). It is now accompanied by a (static) magnetic field, but that doesn’t alter the electric field. Now, however, it is not quite so dumb to wonder whether the electric field lines rotate with the sphere. Well, let ’em rotate, if it pleases you (you can even have them rotate at some completely different rate, about some other axis, if you want to be really perverse); this has nothing to do with physics – they represent the same electric field – it just has to do with how you choose to direct the field-line movie. Arlo was right: it doesn’t even matter. Field lines tell you two things: their density tells you the magnitude of the field, and the arrow tells you its direction. As long as they get those two things right, you can’t ask for more, because that’s all there is to know about the fields. Which field line at one moment corresponds to which field line at some other moment … that’s a metaphysical question; it has no “right” answer, because it is devoid of physical content. And for electric fields that’s pretty much the end of the story.

But the magnetic case is more pernicious, because of the $\mathbf v$ in the Lorentz force law. That’s the velocity of the charge … but relative to what? Nowadays we would answer “relative to whatever inertial reference frame you choose to work in – the frame in which the magnetic field is $\mathbf B$.” But if you conceive of magnetic field lines as “wires” out there in space, moving around in time, it is easy to persuade yourself (at any rate, it was in the nineteenth century) that $\mathbf v$ must be the velocity of the charge relative to the field line at that location. (Remember those electrical engineers talking about “cutting” field lines.) And if that is the case (which, I hasten to say, it is not36) then it matters critically whether the field lines are dragged along with the rotation of the magnet.
