7.2.3 Inductance

Source: book-sources/m2-sec2-inductance.html, Griffiths pp. 325–330. Equations 7.31–7.38; Examples 7.11, 7.12 and 7.13 in full. Problems not included.

Prerequisites

  • Biot–Savart law; Ampère’s law; the field of a long solenoid $B=\mu_0nI$ (Plus Two).
  • The vector potential $\mathbf A$, with $\nabla\times\mathbf A=\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf A=\dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\dfrac{\mathbf J}{r}\,d\tau$ (Eq. 5.66).
  • Faraday’s law (Section 7.2.1); Stokes’ theorem.

1. Mutual inductance

Take two loops of wire at rest (Fig. 7.30). A steady current $I_1$ in loop 1 makes a magnetic field $\mathbf B_1$. Some of its field lines pass through loop 2. Let $\Phi_2$ be the flux of $\mathbf B_1$ through loop 2.

{{FIG:m2-sec2-two-loops}}
Plus Two recall: mutual inductance

Recall. From Class 12: a changing current in one coil induces an emf in a second coil, through the mutual inductance $M$.

Re-derive.

  1. Biot–Savart: $\mathbf B_1=\dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}I_1\oint\dfrac{d\mathbf l_1\times\hat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}$.
  2. Every factor except $I_1$ depends only on geometry, so $\mathbf B_1\propto I_1$.
  3. Then $\Phi_2=\int\mathbf B_1\cdot d\mathbf a_2\propto I_1$. Write the constant of proportionality as $M_{21}$.

Saved. $\Phi_2=M_{21}I_1$ (Eq. 7.31).

The Biot–Savart law shows that $\mathbf B_1$ is proportional to $I_1$. The flux through loop 2 is then also proportional to $I_1$:

$$\Phi_2=M_{21}I_1 \tag{7.31}$$

$M_{21}$ is the mutual inductance of the two loops.

2. The Neumann formula

Write the flux through the vector potential, and use Stokes’ theorem:

$$\Phi_2=\int\mathbf B_1\cdot d\mathbf a_2=\int(\nabla\times\mathbf A_1)\cdot d\mathbf a_2=\oint\mathbf A_1\cdot d\mathbf l_2$$

The vector potential of loop 1 is $\mathbf A_1=\dfrac{\mu_0I_1}{4\pi}\oint\dfrac{d\mathbf l_1}{r}$ (Eq. 5.66). Substituting,

$$\Phi_2=\frac{\mu_0I_1}{4\pi}\oint\left(\oint\frac{d\mathbf l_1}{r}\right)\cdot d\mathbf l_2$$

Comparing with Eq. 7.31 gives the Neumann formula:

Result 2.4: Neumann formula
$$M_{21}=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint\oint\frac{d\mathbf l_1\cdot d\mathbf l_2}{r} \tag{7.32}$$

The double integral runs once round each loop. It depends only on the geometry, so $M_{21}$ is purely geometric. Swapping the roles of the loops leaves the integral unchanged, so $M_{21}=M_{12}$ (Eq. 7.33). From now on write $M$.

{{FIG:m2-sec2-neumann}}

The Neumann formula is rarely used for calculation. It shows that the mutual inductance is fixed by the shapes and positions, and that it is symmetric. Swapping the loops gives the same flux per ampere.

3. Example 7.11: a short solenoid inside a long one

A short solenoid (length $l$, radius $a$, $n_1$ turns per unit length) lies on the axis of a very long solenoid (radius $b$, $n_2$ turns per unit length). Find $M$.

{{FIG:m2-sec2-short-in-long}}
  1. The field of the short solenoid is complicated, and it puts a different flux through each turn of the long solenoid. Computing directly is a miserable task.
  2. Use the symmetry $M_{21}=M_{12}$. Run the current $I$ through the long solenoid instead.
  3. The field inside a long solenoid is $B=\mu_0n_2I$, uniform across the cross-section.
  4. The flux through one turn of the short solenoid is $B\pi a^2=\mu_0n_2I\pi a^2$. The short solenoid has $n_1l$ turns in all.
  5. Total flux: $\Phi=\mu_0\pi a^2n_1n_2Il$.
  6. So $M=\mu_0\pi a^2n_1n_2l$.

If the current in loop 1 changes, Faraday’s law gives an emf in loop 2:

$$\mathcal E_2=-\frac{d\Phi_2}{dt}=-M\frac{dI_1}{dt} \tag{7.34}$$

This holds in the quasistatic regime. No wires join the loops; the emf is induced across the gap.

4. Self-inductance

A changing current also induces an emf in its own loop. The field, and therefore the flux through the loop, is proportional to the current:

Plus Two recall: field of a solenoid and of a toroid

Recall. From Class 12: $B=\mu_0nI$ inside a long solenoid of $n$ turns per unit length.

Re-derive (toroid, used in Example 7.12).

  1. Choose a circle of radius $s$ inside the toroid, centred on its axis. By symmetry $\mathbf B$ is tangent to the circle and constant on it.
  2. Ampère’s law: $B(2\pi s)=\mu_0I_{\text{enc}}=\mu_0NI$, because the circle links all $N$ turns.
  3. So $B=\dfrac{\mu_0NI}{2\pi s}$ inside the toroid, and $B=0$ outside.

Saved. $B=\mu_0NI/2\pi s$.

$$\Phi=LI \tag{7.35}$$

$L$ is the self-inductance (or simply the inductance) of the loop. It depends on the geometry and is positive. The emf is

Result 2.5: self-inductance emf
$$\mathcal E=-L\frac{dI}{dt} \tag{7.36}$$

The unit is the henry, H = V·s/A. The minus sign makes the emf a back emf: it opposes any change in current.

5. Example 7.12: toroid

A toroid has a rectangular cross-section (inner radius $a$, outer radius $b$, height $h$) and $N$ turns. Find $L$.

{{FIG:m2-sec2-toroid}}
  1. From the box above, $B=\dfrac{\mu_0NI}{2\pi s}$.
  2. Flux through one turn: $\displaystyle\int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a=\int_a^b\frac{\mu_0NI}{2\pi s}\,h\,ds=\frac{\mu_0NIh}{2\pi}\ln\frac{b}{a}$.
  3. Total flux through all $N$ turns: $N$ times that, $\Phi=\dfrac{\mu_0N^2Ih}{2\pi}\ln\dfrac{b}{a}$.
  4. Using $\Phi=LI$ (Eq. 7.35): $L=\dfrac{\mu_0N^2h}{2\pi}\ln\dfrac{b}{a}$ (Eq. 7.37).

Inductance plays for currents the role that mass plays for velocities. A large $L$ makes the current hard to change. Lenz’s law, through the minus sign in Eq. 7.36, makes the back emf oppose any change.

6. Example 7.13: the RL circuit

Example 7.13 has two cases. Cutting a current suddenly makes $dI/dt$ huge, and the back emf produces a spark. Connecting a battery to a resistor and an inductor makes the current grow smoothly, because induction opposes the sudden increase (Fig. 7.35).

{{FIG:m2-sec2-rl}}
Plus Two recall: growth of current in an LR circuit

Recall. From Class 12: in an LR circuit the current grows exponentially, with time constant $L/R$.

Re-derive.

  1. Loop rule round the circuit: battery emf $\mathcal E_0$, minus the back emf $L\,dI/dt$, equals the drop $IR$.
  2. So $\mathcal E_0-L\dfrac{dI}{dt}=IR$. This is a first-order equation.
  3. Rearrange: $\dfrac{dI}{dt}=\dfrac{\mathcal E_0-IR}{L}$. Let $I_\infty=\mathcal E_0/R$. Then $\dfrac{dI}{I-I_\infty}=-\dfrac{R}{L}dt$.
  4. Integrate from $I=0$ at $t=0$: $\ln\dfrac{I_\infty-I}{I_\infty}=-\dfrac{R}{L}t$, so $I=I_\infty(1-e^{-Rt/L})$.

Saved. Eq. 7.38 below.

Result 2.6: current growth in an RL circuit
$$I(t)=\frac{\mathcal E_0}{R}\left[1-e^{-(R/L)t}\right] \tag{7.38}$$

The time constant is $\tau=L/R$. At $t=\tau$ the current has reached about two-thirds of its final value $\mathcal E_0/R$. Without inductance the current would jump to $\mathcal E_0/R$ at once.

{{FIG:m2-sec2-rl-graph}}

Footnote 21: the term $-L\,dI/dt$ belongs on the left of the loop equation, because it is part of the emf that drives the voltage across the resistor.
