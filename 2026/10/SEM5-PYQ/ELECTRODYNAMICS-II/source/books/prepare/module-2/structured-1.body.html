Prerequisites

  • Ohm’s law $\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E$ (Eq. 7.3); resistivity $\rho=1/\sigma$.
  • Faraday’s law and the flux rule (Eqs. 7.25, 7.26), used in Sections 1.4 and 2.1.
  • Vector operations: line, surface and volume integrals; divergence, curl, gradient; Stokes’ theorem; the divergence theorem.

Scope: Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics (5th ed.), Ch. 7, sections 7.1.1–7.1.3, 7.2.1–7.2.4, 7.3.1–7.3.3 and 7.3.5–7.3.6 (syllabus items 6–8). Sections 7.3.4 and 7.4 are outside the syllabus and are given as background.

1. Electromotive force (§7.1)

1.1 Ohm’s law (§7.1.1)

The force per unit charge $\mathbf f$ drives the current density $\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf f$ (Eq. 7.1). With the electromagnetic force, $\mathbf f=\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ (Eq. 7.2). Neglecting $\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ (small drift velocity):

Result 1.1 (Ohm’s law)
$$\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E \tag{7.3}$$

Properties:

  • A perfect conductor has $\sigma=\infty$, and in it $\mathbf E=\mathbf J/\sigma=\mathbf 0$ even when a current flows.
  • An insulator has $\sigma\approx0$.

Examples.

  • Ex. 7.1: uniform cylinder of area $A$ and length $L$; $\mathbf E$ uniform (Ex. 7.3); $I=\sigma AV/L$, $R=L/\sigma A$.
  • Ex. 7.2: coaxial cylinders of radii $a$, $b$ and length $L$; $I=2\pi\sigma LV/\ln(b/a)$, $R=\ln(b/a)/2\pi\sigma L$.
  • Ex. 7.3: $V=V_0z/L$ satisfies Laplace’s equation and the boundary conditions; by uniqueness it is the solution, so $\mathbf E$ is uniform.

Steady currents. For steady current and uniform $\sigma$:

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac1\sigma\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0 \tag{7.5}$$

Hence the charge density in the conductor vanishes, excess charge resides on the surface, and $V$ obeys Laplace’s equation within a homogeneous ohmic medium carrying a steady current.

Result 1.2 (circuit form)
$$V=IR \tag{7.4}$$

$R$ depends on geometry and $\sigma$. The unit of resistance is the ohm, $\Omega$ (1 Ω = 1 V/A).

Result 1.3 (Joule heating law)
$$P=VI=I^2R \tag{7.7}$$

$P$ is the power delivered to the resistor, the work of the electric force converted to heat by collisions.

Microscopic model (background to Eq. 7.6). With $n$ molecules per unit volume, $f$ free electrons per molecule, charge $q$ and mass $m$, and a mean free path $\lambda$, the classical (Drude) estimate is $\mathbf J=nfq\,\mathbf v_{\text{ave}}=\left(\frac{nf\lambda q^2}{2mv_{\text{thermal}}}\right)\mathbf E$ (Eq. 7.6). The model is qualitative only (footnote 2).

1.2 Electromotive force (§7.1.2)

Current is equal at all points of a series circuit because any inequality produces a charge accumulation whose field restores equality (Figs. 7.7, 7.8). The total force per unit charge is split into a source part and an electrostatic part:

$$\mathbf f=\mathbf f_s+\mathbf E \tag{7.8}$$

The source part $\mathbf f_s$ acts only in the source region. The electromotive force is defined as

Result 1.4 (definition of emf)
$$\mathcal E\equiv\oint\mathbf f\cdot d\mathbf l=\oint\mathbf f_s\cdot d\mathbf l \tag{7.9}$$

The two forms agree because $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=0$ for electrostatic fields.

Ideal source. Within an ideal source ($\sigma=\infty$), $\mathbf E=-\mathbf f_s$. The terminal potential difference is therefore

$$V=-\int_a^b\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=\int_a^b\mathbf f_s\cdot d\mathbf l=\oint\mathbf f_s\cdot d\mathbf l=\mathcal E \tag{7.10}$$

A real source has internal resistance $r$ and terminal voltage $\mathcal E-Ir$ (footnote 4). Footnote 5 gives the hydraulic analogy: gravity plays the role of $\mathbf E$, and the pump plays the role of the source.

1.3 Motional emf (§7.1.3)

Setup. A loop with a side of length $h$ moves with speed $v$ to the right through a uniform field $\mathbf B$ into the page (Fig. 7.10). A charge in the moving side experiences $\mathbf f_{\text{mag}}=\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$, with component $vB$ along the wire.

Result 1.5 (motional emf)
$$\mathcal E=\oint\mathbf f_{\text{mag}}\cdot d\mathbf l=vBh \tag{7.11}$$

The line integral is evaluated at one instant (a snapshot of the loop). The horizontal sides contribute nothing.

Energy balance. The magnetic force does no work. A charge carries an additional drift velocity $\mathbf u$ along the wire, so the magnetic force has a component $quB$ opposing the motion. The external agent exerts $f_{\text{pull}}=uB$ to maintain the current. Along the actual path of length $h/\cos\theta$,

$$\int\mathbf f_{\text{pull}}\cdot d\mathbf l=(uB)\frac{h}{\cos\theta}\sin\theta=vBh=\mathcal E$$

The work per unit charge equals the emf, although the path of integration and the force responsible are different.

1.4 The flux rule (§7.1.3, continued)

The magnetic flux is

$$\Phi\equiv\int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a \tag{7.12}$$

For the loop of Fig. 7.10, $\Phi=Bhx$, so $d\Phi/dt=Bh\,dx/dt=-Bhv$ (since $dx/dt<0$).

Result 1.6 (flux rule for motional emf)
$$\mathcal E=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt} \tag{7.13}$$

Valid for loops of arbitrary shape and motion, and for non-uniform fields.

Proof. Let $\mathcal S$ be a surface bounded by the loop at time $t$, and $\mathcal S'$ the surface bounded by the loop at $t+dt$. The change in flux is $d\Phi=\int_{\text{ribbon}}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a$, where the ribbon is the strip joining the old and new loops. A point on the ribbon moves with velocity $\mathbf v$, so $d\mathbf a=(\mathbf v\times d\mathbf l)\,dt$. Hence

$$\frac{d\Phi}{dt}=\oint\mathbf B\cdot(\mathbf v\times d\mathbf l)=\oint\mathbf B\cdot(\mathbf w\times d\mathbf l),\qquad\mathbf w=\mathbf v+\mathbf u,$$

using $\mathbf u\parallel d\mathbf l$. Since $\mathbf B\cdot(\mathbf w\times d\mathbf l)=-(\mathbf w\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\mathbf l$ and $\mathbf w\times\mathbf B=\mathbf f_{\text{mag}}$, we get $d\Phi/dt=-\oint\mathbf f_{\text{mag}}\cdot d\mathbf l=-\mathcal E$.

Sign convention. The direction of $d\mathbf l$ around the loop and that of $d\mathbf a$ are linked by the right-hand rule. A negative $\mathcal E$ means the current flows in the negative sense.

Limitation (flux-rule paradox, Fig. 7.14). The rule assumes a single conductor loop. Switches, sliding contacts or extended conductors with several current paths invalidate its use: a switch thrown in Fig. 7.14 doubles the flux but produces no emf.

Example 7.4 (Faraday disk). Disk of radius $a$, angular velocity $\omega$, field $\mathbf B$ along $\hat{\mathbf z}$. The speed at radius $s$ is $\omega s$, so $\mathcal E=\int_0^a\omega Bs\,ds=\omega Ba^2/2$ and $I=\omega Ba^2/2R$. The current spreads over the disk, so the flux rule does not apply in the form used above.

Example 7.5 (eddy currents in a spinning shell). Spherical shell of conductivity $\sigma$, radii $R_1$, $R_2$, angular velocity $\boldsymbol\omega$ in a uniform field $\mathbf B$.

  • Charge density in the steady state: $\rho=-\epsilon_0\nabla\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$ (Eq. 7.14). For $\boldsymbol\omega\parallel\mathbf B$, $\rho=-2\epsilon_0\omega B$ (Eq. 7.16).
  • Case (a), $\boldsymbol\omega\parallel\mathbf B$: $\mathbf E=-\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ (Eq. 7.22) and $\mathbf J=\mathbf 0$.
  • Case (b), $\boldsymbol\omega\perp\mathbf B$: $\rho=0$; $\nabla^2V=0$ within the shell, with $\partial V/\partial r=-\omega BR_{1,2}\sin^2\theta\sin\phi\cos\phi$ at $R_1$ and $R_2$ (the case-(b) boundary data); the eddy current follows from $\mathbf J=\sigma[\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B]$.

Eddy-current demonstrations (Fig. 7.16): a solid aluminium pendulum is retarded on entering a magnet’s field, a slotted pendulum is not, because the slots interrupt the large-scale current loops.
