7.3.5 Maxwell’s equations in matter

Source: book-sources/m2-sec3-matter.html, Griffiths pp. 342–344. Equations 7.56–7.68 in full; Problem 7.42 not included.

Prerequisites

  • Bound charge $\rho_b=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P$ (Eq. 7.56); bound current $\mathbf J_b=\nabla\times\mathbf M$ (Eq. 7.57).
  • Continuity equation (Section 7.3.3).

1. Bound charges and currents

Inside polarized or magnetized matter there are bound charges and currents. They are not controlled directly, so it is cleaner to write Maxwell’s equations in terms of free charges and currents only.

  • Electric polarization $\mathbf P$ gives bound charge density $\rho_b=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P$ (Eq. 7.56).
  • Magnetization $\mathbf M$ gives bound current $\mathbf J_b=\nabla\times\mathbf M$ (Eq. 7.57).

2. Polarization current

When the fields change in time there is a third current: a changing polarization moves bound charge. Take a short tube of polarized material with $\mathbf P$ along its axis (Fig. 7.48).

{{FIG:m2-sec3-polarized-tube}}
Plus Two recall: polarization and bound surface charge

Recall. From Class 12: a dielectric carries bound charge on its surfaces, and its polarization is the dipole moment per unit volume.

Re-derive.

  1. The polarization gives a surface charge $\sigma_b=\mathbf P\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}$ on each end. For $\mathbf P$ along the axis, $\sigma_b=+P$ at one end and $-P$ at the other.
  2. If $P$ increases by $\partial P/\partial t$ per unit time, the charge on each end changes at that rate per unit area.
  3. So a net current per unit area flows along the tube: $\mathbf J_p=\partial\mathbf P/\partial t$.

Saved. Eq. 7.58.

$$\mathbf J_p=\frac{\partial\mathbf P}{\partial t} \tag{7.58}$$

This polarization current is different from the bound current $\mathbf J_b$. $\mathbf J_b$ comes from magnetization (spin and orbital motion of electrons). $\mathbf J_p$ is the motion of charge when $\mathbf P$ changes.

Check. $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J_p=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf P)=-\dfrac{\partial\rho_b}{\partial t}$. So $\mathbf J_p$ keeps bound charge conserved. A changing magnetization gives no analogous charge accumulation, because $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf M)\equiv0$.

3. Total charge and current

Split the total charge and current into free and bound parts:

$$\rho=\rho_f+\rho_b=\rho_f-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P \tag{7.59}$$
$$\mathbf J=\mathbf J_f+\mathbf J_b+\mathbf J_p=\mathbf J_f+\nabla\times\mathbf M+\frac{\partial\mathbf P}{\partial t} \tag{7.60}$$

4. Gauss’s law and Ampère’s law in matter

Gauss’s law becomes $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\dfrac{1}{\epsilon_0}(\rho_f-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P)$. Rearranging, $\nabla\cdot(\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P)=\rho_f$. Define

$$\mathbf D\equiv\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P \tag{7.62}$$

so that $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f$ (Eq. 7.61). Here $\mathbf D$ is the electric displacement.

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f \tag{7.61}$$

Ampère–Maxwell becomes $\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\left(\mathbf J_f+\nabla\times\mathbf M+\partial\mathbf P/\partial t\right)+\mu_0\epsilon_0\,\partial\mathbf E/\partial t$. Rearranging, $\nabla\times\left(\dfrac{\mathbf B}{\mu_0}-\mathbf M\right)=\mathbf J_f+\partial\mathbf D/\partial t$. Define

$$\mathbf H\equiv\frac{1}{\mu_0}\mathbf B-\mathbf M \tag{7.64}$$
$$\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t} \tag{7.63}$$

Faraday’s law and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$ are unchanged, since neither contains $\rho$ or $\mathbf J$.

Result 3.4: Maxwell’s equations in terms of free charge and current
$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f,\quad\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\quad\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\quad\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t} \tag{7.65}$$

These are a division of the sources into free and bound parts, not a more general theory than Eq. 7.49. Their mixed notation ($\mathbf E$ with $\mathbf D$, $\mathbf B$ with $\mathbf H$) needs constitutive relations.

5. Linear media

For linear media the constitutive relations are

Result 3.5: linear media
$$\mathbf P=\epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E,\qquad\mathbf M=\chi_m\mathbf H \tag{7.66}$$
$$\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E,\qquad\mathbf H=\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf B \tag{7.67}$$

with $\epsilon=\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)$ and $\mu=\mu_0(1+\chi_m)$.

In matter the displacement current is $\mathbf J_d=\partial\mathbf D/\partial t$ (Eq. 7.68), so the name “displacement current” of Section 7.3.2 is extended to $\partial\mathbf D/\partial t$. Problem 7.42 compares conduction and displacement current in sea water (not included).
