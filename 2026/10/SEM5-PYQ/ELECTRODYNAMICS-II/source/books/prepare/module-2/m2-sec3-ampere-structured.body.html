7.3.1 Electrodynamics before Maxwell

Source: book-sources/m2-sec3-ampere.html, Griffiths pp. 335–336. Equation 7.45 and the capacitor argument (Fig. 7.43) in full.

Prerequisites

  • $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf A)\equiv0$ for any vector field $\mathbf A$.
  • Continuity equation $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=-\partial\rho/\partial t$ (Eq. 5.29 in the book).
  • Ampère’s circuital law in integral form (Plus Two).

1. The four laws before Maxwell

Electromagnetic theory before Maxwell consisted of four laws, one for the divergence and one for the curl of each field:

  • (i) $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0$ (Gauss’s law);
  • (ii) $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$ (no name);
  • (iii) $\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial\mathbf B/\partial t$ (Faraday’s law);
  • (iv) $\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J$ (Ampère’s law).
Plus Two recall: Ampère’s circuital law

Recall. From Class 12: $\oint\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf l=\mu_0I_{\text{enc}}$, where $I_{\text{enc}}$ is the current through the surface bounded by the loop.

Re-derive (field of a long straight wire).

  1. Take a circle of radius $s$ about a long straight wire carrying current $I$. By symmetry $\mathbf B$ is tangent to the circle and has the same size all round.
  2. $\oint\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf l=B\cdot2\pi s$, and the enclosed current is $I$.
  3. So $B\cdot2\pi s=\mu_0I$, which gives $B=\dfrac{\mu_0I}{2\pi s}$.

Saved. $\mathbf B=\dfrac{\mu_0I}{2\pi s}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$, the field used in Section 7.2.2.

2. The inconsistency

Take the divergence of (iii). The left side is $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=0$. The right side is $-\partial(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)/\partial t$, which is zero by (ii). So (iii) is consistent with (ii).

Now take the divergence of (iv). Again the left side is zero, because $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\equiv0$:

$$\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)=\mu_0(\nabla\cdot\mathbf J) \tag{7.45}$$

The right side is zero only if $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0$, which holds for steady currents. For a charge that builds up somewhere, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=-\partial\rho/\partial t\neq0$. So (iv) contradicts the identity, and Ampère’s law cannot be right beyond magnetostatics.

3. The capacitor test (Fig. 7.43)

Charge a capacitor through a wire. Apply Ampère’s law to a loop round the wire. The loop bounds more than one surface:

{{FIG:m2-sec3-capacitor-loop}}
  • The plane surface spanning the loop is pierced by the wire, so $I_{\text{enc}}=I$.
  • A balloon-shaped surface that passes between the capacitor plates is not pierced by any conduction current, so $I_{\text{enc}}=0$.

Both surfaces have the same boundary, so the law gives two answers for one loop. “The current enclosed by the loop” is therefore ill-defined when currents are not steady. In magnetostatics this never arises, because charge does not accumulate on capacitor plates.

4. Where the flaw lies

Ampère’s law was derived from the Biot–Savart law, so there was no reason to expect it to hold outside magnetostatics. In Maxwell’s time there was no experimental evidence against it, so the flaw was purely theoretical. Section 7.3.2 gives Maxwell’s repair.
