6.1 Magnetization: my reading notes

Book pp. 268–276. Pages 209–267 are not in the excerpt.

Big picture

The book opens with a surprise: magnets are not a separate thing from moving charges. Inside an atom there are tiny currents, electrons going round the nucleus and spinning. Each one acts like a small magnet, which the book calls a magnetic dipole. Normally they point every which way and cancel. A field lines them up a little, and the material is then magnetized.

1. Three kinds of response

  • Paramagnet: magnetization points along B.
  • Diamagnet: magnetization points against B.
  • Ferromagnet: keeps its magnetization after the field is off. Its state depends on its history, so the book leaves it to the end.

The book warns that the classical picture is incomplete. Real electron spin is a fixed point dipole, not a spinning ball.

2. Torque on a current loop

Take a rectangular loop of sides $a$ and $b$, carrying current $I$, in a uniform field B. The forces on the sloping sides cancel and do not turn the loop. The forces on the horizontal sides are equal and opposite, but they are a little apart, so they make a turning effect:

$$\mathbf N = IabB\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf x} = \mathbf m\times\mathbf B \quad(6.1)$$

This is the magnetic version of $\mathbf p\times\mathbf E$ from §4.1. The loop turns to line up with B. Any loop can be cut into little rectangles, so the result is general.

3. Why paramagnetism is usually weak

Electrons pair up with opposite spins, and the pairs cancel each other's dipoles. So it is mostly atoms with an odd number of electrons that feel the torque. Heat then upsets the alignment, so it is never complete.

4. Force on a magnet

  • In a uniform field a loop feels no net force, because $\oint d\mathbf l = 0$.
  • In a non-uniform field a ring above a solenoid feels a force $F = 2\pi IRB\cos\theta$ (6.2).
  • For a tiny dipole: $\mathbf F = \nabla(\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B)$ (6.3). This is the same form as $\nabla(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E)$.

Two models are in use. The Gilbert model has separate north and south "charges", which makes the algebra easy. The Ampère model uses current loops, which is the real one, because magnetic monopoles do not exist. Use Gilbert for a quick picture, and never for exact close-range answers.

5. Orbiting electrons and diamagnetism

Model an electron as a circle of radius $R$ and speed $v$. Its current is $I = -ev/2\pi R$, and its dipole is

$$\mathbf m = -\tfrac12 evR\,\hat{\mathbf z} \quad(6.4)$$

Without a field, the electric pull alone supplies the centripetal force (6.5). With a field B perpendicular to the orbit, the Lorentz force $-e\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ adds to it (6.6). Subtracting (6.5) from (6.6) and taking the change to be small:

Result
$\Delta v = \dfrac{eRB}{2m_e} \quad(6.7)$. The electron speeds up.

A faster electron has a changed dipole:

Result
$\Delta\mathbf m = -\dfrac{e^2R^2}{4m_e}\mathbf B \quad(6.8)$. The change is against B, whichever way the electron goes round.

Every atom gets a small extra dipole against B. That is diamagnetism. It exists in all atoms but is weaker than paramagnetism, so it shows up mostly in atoms with an even number of electrons.

The book says this classical model is flawed, and that diamagnetism is really quantum. Also, the speed change is caused by the induced electric field of a changing B (Chapter 7), not by B doing work.

6. Magnetization M

Definition
$\mathbf M \equiv$ magnetic dipole moment per unit volume (6.9). It plays the role P played for dielectrics.

The book takes M as given and asks what field it makes (next section). It does not matter whether M comes from spin, from orbits, or from ferromagnetism.

Forces on samples in a non-uniform field are very small. Paramagnets are drawn in; diamagnets are pushed out. Iron feels $10^4$ to $10^5$ times more than a comparable sample of a weak material, which is why we could ignore magnetization in Chapter 5.
