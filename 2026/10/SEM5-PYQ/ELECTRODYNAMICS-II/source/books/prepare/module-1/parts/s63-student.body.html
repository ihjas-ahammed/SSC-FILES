6.3 The auxiliary field H: my reading notes

Book pp. 281–286.

Why do we need a new field?

Ampère's law (Chapter 5) says the curl of B equals $\mu_0$ times the current. But now there are two kinds of current: the free current from the wires, and the bound current from the magnetization. We want a rule that only mentions the current we control.

1. Splitting the current

The total current is $\mathbf J = \mathbf J_b + \mathbf J_f$ (6.17). The free current comes from the battery. The bound current comes from the atoms lining up. Put $\mathbf J_b = \nabla\times\mathbf M$ into Ampère's law and collect the curls:

$$\nabla\times\left(\frac{1}{\mu_0}\mathbf B - \mathbf M\right) = \mathbf J_f$$

The bracket is called H:

Definition and result
$\mathbf H \equiv \dfrac{1}{\mu_0}\mathbf B - \mathbf M \quad(6.18)$, and then $\nabla\times\mathbf H = \mathbf J_f \quad(6.19)$, or $\oint\mathbf H\cdot d\mathbf l = I_{f,\text{enc}} \quad(6.20)$.

This is the magnetic twin of D. Gauss's law used D so that you only need the free charge; Ampère's law uses H so that you only need the free current. Practical people prefer H, because they set a current on a dial and not a field.

2. Example 6.2: a wire with current

Take a long rod of copper with a uniform current I. Copper is weakly diamagnetic, so the bound currents oppose I inside and follow I on the surface. The field is azimuthal, and a loop of radius $s$ inside the rod encloses the fraction $s^2/R^2$ of the current:

Results
Inside: $\mathbf H = \dfrac{I}{2\pi R^2}s\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi} \quad(6.21)$.
Outside: $\mathbf H = \dfrac{I}{2\pi s}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi} \quad(6.22)$. Here $\mathbf B = \mu_0\mathbf H$, the same as for an ordinary wire.

Inside, I cannot get B, because I do not know M yet.

3. Two traps

  • Eq. 6.19 looks like Ampère's law, but a curl alone does not decide a field. You also need the divergence.
  • Unlike B, the divergence of H is not zero: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf H = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf M \quad(6.23)$.
  • Bar magnet: no free current at all, so a careless use of (6.20) gives $\mathbf H = 0$. That is wrong. The divergence of M at the ends is what makes H non-zero there.

Rule: look for symmetry first. Only then trust (6.20) on its own.

4. Boundary conditions (beyond our list)

Across a boundary, H's normal part jumps by the negative of M's jump (6.24), and its tangential part jumps by the free surface current (6.25). The book suggests checking them against Ex. 6.2 and Prob. 6.14.
