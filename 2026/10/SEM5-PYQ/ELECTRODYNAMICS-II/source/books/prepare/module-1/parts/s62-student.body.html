6.2 The field of a magnetized object: my reading notes

Book pp. 276–281.

The idea

Last section we found that a magnetized lump is a bunch of tiny loops. This section asks: can we replace all those loops with simpler currents on the surface and inside, and get the same field? Yes. That is what the book means by bound currents.

1. Summing the loops

Each tiny piece of the object is one dipole, $\mathbf M\,d\tau'$. The field of one dipole is in terms of its vector potential (Eq. 6.10). Adding them all up gives (6.11). The integral is messy, so we rewrite it.

The trick is the identity $\nabla'(1/\mathcal R) = \hat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal R}}/\mathcal R^2$. Using integration by parts and the divergence theorem, the messy integral splits into two simpler ones (6.12). One is over the volume, one over the surface.

Two new currents
Inside: $\mathbf J_b = \nabla\times\mathbf M$ (6.13). On the surface: $\mathbf K_b = \mathbf M\times\hat{\mathbf n}$ (6.14).
Result
The field of the magnetized object is the same as the field of $\mathbf J_b$ inside and $\mathbf K_b$ on the surface (6.15). This is the magnetic version of the bound charge trick in §4.2.

2. Example 6.1: magnetized sphere

Let M point along $z$. Inside, $\nabla\times\mathbf M = 0$, so there is no volume current. On the surface, $K_b = M\sin\theta$, flowing around the sphere. That is exactly the current on a spinning uniformly charged shell. So the magnetized sphere is equivalent to a spinning shell.

Result
Inside, $\mathbf B = \tfrac23\mu_0\mathbf M$ (6.16), which is uniform. Outside, it is a perfect dipole with $\mathbf m = \tfrac43\pi R^3\mathbf M$.

The electric version (uniformly polarized sphere) also gives a uniform field inside, but its factor is $-\tfrac13$. The factors differ, but the shape of the answer matches.

3. Why these currents are real

Picture a slab made of tiny loops all pointing the same way. Inside, each loop's right side cancels its neighbour's left side. At the edge there is no neighbour, so one current is left running around the boundary. Its strength is $K_b = M$, because one loop of area $a$ and thickness $t$ has $m = Mat = Ia$, so $I = Mt$.

{{FIG:s62-bound-surface-current}}

If M varies from place to place, the cancellation fails inside too. The result is $\mathbf J_b = \nabla\times\mathbf M$. Check: $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf M) = 0$, so this current is steady, as it should be.

{{FIG:s62-nonuniform-bound}}

Note: each electron only moves within its own atom. The current is "bound" to the atoms, but over the whole object it is a real current.

4. Inside matter

The true field inside matter changes wildly from place to place. The field we use is the average over many atoms. The methods above give that average. Prob. 6.11 asks for the proof.
