4.3 The electric displacement: my reading notes

Book pp. 181–186. Pages 209 onward are outside this section.

Why a new field?

Inside a dielectric there are two kinds of charge: the free charge that someone put there, and the bound charge that comes from polarization. Gauss's law needs both, but we only control the free charge. So we look for a quantity that depends on the free charge alone.

1. Free and bound together

$$\rho = \rho_b + \rho_f \quad(4.20)$$

Put $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf P$ into Gauss's law: $\epsilon_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf P + \rho_f$. Move the P term across and you get one divergence:

Definition and result
$\mathbf D \equiv \epsilon_0\mathbf E + \mathbf P \quad(4.21)$. Then $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D = \rho_f \quad(4.22)$, or in words $\oint\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf a = Q_{f,\text{enc}} \quad(4.23)$.

Now you only need the free charge to find D. Bound charge follows once the free charge is in place.

The surface is a special case. Right at the edge, the polarization drops to zero suddenly, so $\rho_b$ becomes infinite. The book's way out: imagine a thin edge where P fades to zero. Then there is no surface term, and Gauss's law holds everywhere. The integral form (4.23) avoids the issue altogether.

2. Example 4.4: a wire with insulation

A long wire has uniform line charge $\lambda$ and rubber insulation out to radius $a$. Draw a cylinder of radius s and length L. By symmetry D is radial. The free charge inside is $\lambda L$:

Result
$D(2\pi sL) = \lambda L$, so $\mathbf D = \dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi s}\hat{\mathbf s} \quad(4.24)$. This holds inside and outside the insulation.

Outside, P is zero, so $\mathbf E = \mathbf D/\epsilon_0$. Inside, E cannot be found yet, because P is not known.

3. Careful: D is not just E with other charges

Eq. 4.22 looks like Gauss's law, so it is tempting to say "D is E, but sourced by free charge". The book says this is false. There is no Coulomb's law for D. A divergence does not fix a vector field; you need the curl too.

$$\nabla\times\mathbf D = \nabla\times\mathbf P \quad(4.25)$$

The curl of P is not always zero. Example: a bar electret with no free charge. If D came only from free charge, D would be zero everywhere, which would wrongly give $\mathbf E = -\mathbf P/\epsilon_0$ inside and zero outside. So the curl term matters.

Advice: check symmetry first. With spherical, cylindrical or plane symmetry, Gauss's law gives D directly, and the curl of P is automatically zero.

4. Boundary conditions

Across a boundary, the normal part of D jumps by the free surface charge (4.26). The tangential part of D jumps by the tangential part of P (4.27). This follows from the curl equation. For comparison, E jumps by the total surface charge over $\epsilon_0$ in the normal direction (4.28), and its tangential part is continuous (4.29). Use the D versions when P is known.

5. Crystals: the choice is not unique (background)

In salt, each sodium ion can be paired with a chlorine ion above, below, left or right. Each choice gives a different P and a different split between free and bound charge. So the split is not uniquely defined for crystals. What is physical is the total charge, which is zero, and the change in P when a field is switched on.
