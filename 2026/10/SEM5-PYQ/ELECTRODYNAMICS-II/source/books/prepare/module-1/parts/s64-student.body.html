6.4 Linear and nonlinear media: my reading notes

Book pp. 286–294.

The rule for most materials

For most materials, M is proportional to the field, as long as the field is not too strong. Writing it in terms of B is correct but awkward, so the book uses H:

Rule
$\mathbf M = \chi_m\mathbf H \quad(6.29)$. The number $\chi_m$ is the magnetic susceptibility. It is positive for paramagnets and negative for diamagnets. Typical size: $10^{-5}$.

Then B follows: $\mathbf B = \mu_0(1+\chi_m)\mathbf H$ (6.30). Define the permeability $\mu = \mu_0(1+\chi_m)$, so $\mathbf B = \mu\mathbf H$ (6.31, 6.32). In vacuum $\chi_m = 0$, so $\mu = \mu_0$.

Example 6.3: solenoid with a core

An infinite solenoid with n turns per metre and current I. H comes from the free current alone, so $\mathbf H = nI\hat{\mathbf z}$. Then $\mathbf B = \mu_0(1+\chi_m)nI\hat{\mathbf z}$. A paramagnetic core makes B bigger, a diamagnetic core makes it smaller. The reason: the bound surface current runs along I for $\chi_m > 0$ and against it for $\chi_m < 0$.

Why M can have a divergence in a linear medium

For the electric field we said B has zero divergence, and that is always true. But M can have a divergence at a boundary between two materials. Take a pillbox across the boundary: M is zero on one side and not on the other, so the flux through the box is not zero. So the divergence of M is not zero there.

Inside a homogeneous linear material, the bound current is a fixed fraction of the free current (6.33): $\mathbf J_b = \chi_m\mathbf J_f$. So unless free current flows through the material, the bound current sits on the surface.

Ferromagnets are not linear

A ferromagnet keeps its magnetization with no field. Its alignment is frozen in. In a ferromagnet, neighbouring dipoles like to point the same way. This is quantum, and the book does not explain it. So for a ferromagnet, the susceptibility is not a constant.

Domains

Iron is not a magnet in everyday life, because the aligned regions (domains) point in random directions. A field grows the domains that point along it, by moving the walls between them. If the field is strong enough, one domain takes over. That is saturation. When the field is switched off, most of the domains stay where they are, so you have a permanent magnet.

Hysteresis: why history matters

Run a current through a coil around a piece of iron. Follow the path: from zero, to saturation (b), back to zero current with some magnetization left (c), reversed current until M is zero (d), reversed saturation (e), back to zero with reversed residual (f), and so on. The closed path is the hysteresis loop. Notice that zero current does not mean zero magnetization: at a, c and f the current is zero but M differs.

Plots usually use B against H. For a long solenoid H = nI, and B is almost $\mu_0 M$ because M is much bigger than H in practice.

Curie point

Heat destroys the alignment. For iron the threshold is 770 °C. Below it, iron is ferromagnetic. Above it, iron is only paramagnetic. The change is sudden, like water boiling.
