12.1 GROWTH OF CURRENT IN A CIRCUIT CONTAINING A RESISTANCE AND INDUCTANCE

Consider a circuit having an inductance $L$ and a resistance $R$ connected in series to a cell of steady emf $E$ (Fig. 12.1). When the key $K$ is pressed, there is a gradual growth of current in the circuit from zero to maximum value $I_0$. Let $I$ be the instantaneous current at any instant.

FIG. 12.1: circuit with R, L, cell E and key K in series

Then, the induced back emf $\varepsilon = -L\dfrac{dI}{dt}$.

$$E = RI + L\frac{dI}{dt}\qquad(1)$$

When the current reaches the maximum value $I_0$, the back emf is $L\dfrac{dI}{dt}=0$. Hence

$$E = RI_0\qquad(2)$$

Substituting this value for $E$ in Eq. (1),

$$RI_0 = RI + L\frac{dI}{dt}\quad\text{or}\quad R(I_0 - I) = L\frac{dI}{dt}$$
$$\frac{dI}{I_0 - I} = \frac{R}{L}\,dt$$

Integrating,

$$-\log(I_0 - I) = \frac{R}{L}t + C\qquad(3)$$

where $C$ is the constant of integration. When $t = 0$, $I = 0$, so $-\log_e I_0 = C$. Substituting this value of $C$ in Eq. (3),

$$-\log(I_0 - I) = \frac{R}{L}t - \log_e I_0\quad\text{or}\quad \log_e(I_0 - I) - \log_e I_0 = -\frac{R}{L}t$$
$$\log_e\frac{(I_0 - I)}{I_0} = -\frac{R}{L}t$$
$$\frac{I_0 - I}{I_0} = e^{-(R/L)t}\quad\text{or}\quad 1 - \frac{I}{I_0} = e^{-(R/L)t}$$
$$\therefore\quad I = I_0\left(1 - e^{-(R/L)t}\right)\qquad(4)$$

Eq. (4) gives the value of the instantaneous current in the $LR$ circuit. The quantity $(L/R)$ is called the time constant of the circuit.

$$\text{If}\quad \frac{L}{R} = t,\quad I = I_0\left(1 - e^{-1}\right) = I_0\left(1 - \frac{1}{e}\right) = 0.632\,I_0$$

Thus, the time constant $L/R$ of a L-R circuit is the time taken by the current to grow from zero to 0.632 times the steady maximum value of current in the circuit. Similarly, when $t = 2L/R,\ 3L/R\ \ldots$, the value of current will be 0.8647, 0.9502, $\ldots$ of the final maximum current.

When $t = 0$, $I = 0$ and when $t \to \infty$, $I = I_0$. Greater the value of $L/R$, longer is the time taken by the current $I$ to reach its maximum value (Fig. 12.2).

12.2 DECAY OF CURRENT IN A CIRCUIT CONTAINING L AND R

When the circuit is broken, an induced emf, equal to $-L\dfrac{dI}{dt}$, is again produced in the inductance $L$ and it slows down the rate of decay of the current. The current in the circuit decays from maximum value $I_0$ to zero. During the decay, let $I$ be the current at time $t$. In this case $E = 0$. The emf equation for the decay of current is

$$0 = RI + L\frac{dI}{dt}\qquad(1)$$
$$\therefore\quad \frac{dI}{I} = -\frac{R}{L}\,dt$$

Integrating,

$$\log_e I = -\frac{R}{L}t + C\qquad\text{where } C \text{ is a constant}$$

When $t = 0$, $I = I_0$, so $\log_e I_0 = C$.

$$\log_e I = -\frac{R}{L}t + \log_e I_0,\quad\text{or}\quad \log_e\frac{I}{I_0} = -\frac{R}{L}t$$
$$\frac{I}{I_0} = e^{-(R/L)t}$$
$$\therefore\quad I = I_0 e^{-(R/L)t}\qquad(2)$$

Eq. (2) represents the current at any instant $t$ during decay. A graph between current and time is shown in Fig. 12.3.

$$t = L/R,\quad I = I_0 e^{-1} = \frac{1}{e}I_0 = 0.365\,I_0$$
$$t = 2L/R,\quad I = I_0 e^{-2} = 0.1035\,I_0$$
$$t = 3L/R,\quad I = I_0 e^{-3} = 0.05\,I_0$$

Therefore, the time constant $L/R$ of a R-L circuit may also be defined as the time in which the current in the circuit falls to $1/e$ of its maximum value when external source of e.m.f. is removed.

FIG. 12.3: decay of current I (from I0) against time t

The rate of decay of current is

$$\frac{dI}{dt} = -\frac{R}{L}I_0 e^{-(R/L)t} = -\frac{R}{L}I$$

Thus it is clear that greater the ratio $R/L$, or smaller the time constant $L/R$, the more rapidly does the current die away (Fig. 12.4).

Example 1. An e.m.f. 10 volts is applied to a circuit having a resistance of 10 ohms and an inductance of 0.5 henry. Find the time required by the current to attain 63.2% of its final value. What is the time constant of the circuit?

Solution. $I = I_0(1 - e^{-Rt/L})$. Given:

$$\frac{I}{I_0} = \frac{63.2}{100},\qquad \frac{R}{L} = \frac{10}{0.5} = 20$$
$$\frac{63.2}{100} = 1 - e^{-20t}$$
$$e^{-20t} = 1 - 0.632 = 0.368$$
$$e^{20t} = \frac{1}{0.368} = 2.717$$
$$20t = \log_e 2.717$$
$$t = \frac{1}{20}\times 2.3026\times\log_{10}2.717 = \frac{2.3026\times 0.4341}{20} = 0.05\ \text{sec.}$$

The time constant of the circuit is

$$\frac{L}{R} = \frac{0.5}{10} = \frac{1}{20}\ \text{sec.}$$

Example 2. An inductance of 500 mH and a resistance of 5 ohms are connected in series with an e.m.f of 10 volts. Find the final current. If now the cell is removed and the two terminals are connected together, find the current after (i) 0.05 sec. and (ii) 0.2 sec.

Solution. Final current

$$I_0 = \frac{E}{R} = \frac{10}{5} = 2\ \text{A}$$

During discharge, $I = I_0 e^{-Rt/L}$. Now

$$\frac{R}{L} = \frac{5}{500\times 10^{-3}} = 10$$

(i) When $t = 0.05\ \text{sec.}$, $I = 2e^{-10\times 0.05} = 1.213\ \text{A}$

12.3 GROWTH AND DECAY OF CHARGE IN A CIRCUIT CONTAINING RESISTANCE AND CAPACITOR (CHARGING AND DISCHARGING OF A CAPACITOR THROUGH A RESISTOR)

(a) Growth of Charge. A capacitor $C$ and a resistance $R$ are connected to a cell of emf $E$ through a Morse key $K$ (Fig. 12.6). When the key is pressed, a momentary current $I$ flows through $R$. At any instant $t$, let $Q$ be the charge on the capacitor of capacitance $C$.

FIG. 12.6: capacitor C, resistance R, Morse key K and cell E in series

P.D. across capacitor $= Q/C$; P.D. across resistor $= RI$.

The emf equation of the circuit is

$$E = \frac{Q}{C} + RI\qquad(1)$$
$$E = \frac{Q}{C} + R\frac{dQ}{dt}\qquad\because\ I = \frac{dQ}{dt}$$

The capacitor continues getting charged till it attains the maximum charge $Q_0$. At that instant $I = dQ/dt = 0$. The P.D. across the capacitor is $E = Q_0/C$. i.e., when $Q = Q_0$, $\dfrac{dQ}{dt} = 0$ and $E = \dfrac{Q_0}{C}$.

$$\therefore\quad \frac{Q_0}{C} = \frac{Q}{C} + R\frac{dQ}{dt}$$
$$(Q_0 - Q) = CR\frac{dQ}{dt}$$
$$\frac{dQ}{Q_0 - Q} = \frac{dt}{CR}\qquad(2)$$

Integrating, $-\log_e(Q_0 - Q) = \dfrac{t}{CR} + K$, where $K$ is a constant. When $t = 0$, $Q = 0$, so $-\log_e Q_0 = K$.

$$-\log_e(Q_0 - Q) = \frac{t}{CR} - \log_e Q_0$$
$$\log_e(Q_0 - Q) = -\frac{t}{CR} + \log_e Q_0$$
$$\log_e(Q_0 - Q) - \log_e Q_0 = -\frac{t}{CR}$$
$$\log_e\left(\frac{Q_0 - Q}{Q_0}\right) = -\frac{t}{CR}$$
$$\frac{Q_0 - Q}{Q_0} = e^{-t/CR}\quad\text{or}\quad 1 - \frac{Q}{Q_0} = e^{-t/CR}$$
316Electricity and Magnetism
$$\therefore\quad Q = Q_0\left(1 - e^{-t/CR}\right)\qquad(3)$$

The term $CR$ is called the time constant of the circuit. At the end of time $t = CR$, $Q = Q_0(1 - e^{-1}) = 0.632\,Q_0$.

Thus, the time constant may be defined as the time taken by the capacitor to get charged to 0.632 times its maximum value.

The growth of charge is shown in Fig. 12.7. The rate of growth of charge is

$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = \frac{Q_0}{CR}e^{-\frac{t}{CR}} = \frac{I}{CR}(Q_0 - Q)$$

Thus it is seen that smaller the product $CR$, the more rapidly does the charge grow on the capacitor.

The rate of growth of the charge is rapid in the beginning and it becomes less and less as the charge approaches nearer and nearer the steady value.

FIG. 12.7: charge against time; growth curve rising to Q0 and discharge curve falling from Q0

(b) Decay of charge (Discharging of a Capacitor through Resistance). Let the capacitor having charge $Q_0$ be now discharged by releasing the Morse key $K$ (Fig. 12.6). The charge flows out of the capacitor and this constitutes a current. In this case $E = 0$.

$$R\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{Q}{C} = 0\qquad(1)$$
$$\text{or}\quad \frac{dQ}{Q} = -\frac{1}{CR}\,dt$$

Integrating, $\log_e Q = -\dfrac{t}{CR} + K$, where $K$ is a constant.

When $t = 0$, $Q = Q_0$, so $\log_e Q_0 = K$.

$$\log_e Q = -\frac{t}{CR} + \log_e Q_0$$
$$\text{or}\quad \log_e\frac{Q}{Q_0} = -\frac{t}{CR}\quad\text{or}\quad \frac{Q}{Q_0} = e^{-t/CR}$$
$$\therefore\quad Q = Q_0 e^{-t/CR}\qquad(2)$$

This shows that the charge in the capacitor decays exponentially and becomes zero after infinite interval of time (Fig. 12.7). The rate of discharge is

$$I = \frac{dQ}{dt} = -\frac{Q_0}{CR}e^{-t/CR} = -\frac{Q}{CR}\qquad(3)$$

Thus, smaller the time-constant $CR$, the quicker is the discharge of the capacitor.

In Eq. (2), if we put $t = CR$, then $Q = Q_0 e^{-1} = 0.368\,Q_0$. Hence time constant may also be defined as the time taken by the current to fall from maximum to 0.368 of its maximum value.

Example 1. A capacitor is charged by DC supply through a resistance of 2 megaohms. If it takes 0.5 seconds for the charge to reach three quarters of its final value, what is the capacitance of the capacitor?

Solution. Here, $R = 2\times 10^6\ \Omega$, $t = 0.5$ s, $Q/Q_0 = 3/4$.

$$Q = Q_0\left(1 - e^{-t/CR}\right)\quad\text{or}\quad \frac{Q}{Q_0} = \left(1 - e^{-t/CR}\right)$$
$$\frac{3}{4} = \left(1 - e^{-\frac{0.5}{C\times(2\times 10^6)}}\right)$$
$$e^{\frac{0.5}{C\times(2\times 10^6)}} = 4\quad\text{or}\quad \frac{0.5}{C\times(2\times 10^6)} = \log_e 4$$
$$C = \frac{0.5}{(2.3026\log_{10}4)(2\times 10^6)} = 0.18\times 10^{-6}\ \text{F} = 0.18\ \mu\text{F.}$$

Example 2. A capacitor of capacitance 0.1 $\mu$F is first charged and then discharged through a resistance of 10 megaohm. Find the time, the potential will take to fall to half its original value.

Solution. $Q = Q_0 e^{-t/CR}$, or $\dfrac{Q}{Q_0} = e^{-t/CR}$, or $\ln\left(\dfrac{Q_0}{Q}\right) = \dfrac{t}{CR}$.

$$\therefore\quad t = CR\ln\left(\frac{Q_0}{Q}\right)$$
$$Q = CV\ \text{and}\ Q_0 = CV_0.\qquad\therefore\ \frac{Q_0}{Q} = \frac{V_0}{V}$$
$$\therefore\quad t = CR\ln\left(\frac{V_0}{V}\right)$$

Here, $C = 10^{-7}$ F; $R = 10^7\ \Omega$; $V_0/V = 2$.

$$\therefore\quad t = 10^{-7}\times 10^7\times\ln 2 = 0.6931\ \text{s.}$$

Example 3. A resistance $R$ and a $2\mu$F capacitor in series are connected to a 200 volt direct supply. Across the capacitor is a neon lamp that strikes at 120 volts. Calculate the value of $R$ to make the lamp strike 5 seconds after switch has been closed.

Solution. The resistance $R$ must be such that the p.d. across the capacitor should rise to 120 volt in 5 seconds after the switch is closed. The lamp would then strike. The equation of charging is

$$Q = Q_0\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right)\ \text{or}\ CV = CV_0\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right)\ \text{or}\ V = V_0\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right)$$

Here, $V = 120$ volt, $V_0 = 200$ volt, $t = 5$ sec and $C = 2\times 10^{-6}$ farad,

$$120 = 200\left(1 - e^{-5/(2\times 10^{-6}R)}\right)$$
$$e^{5/2\times 10^{-6}R} = \frac{5}{2}$$
$$\frac{5}{2\times 10^{-6}R} = \log_e 5 - \log_e 2 = 1.6094 - 0.6931 = 0.9163$$
$$R = \frac{5}{2\times 10^{-6}\times 0.9163} = 2.73\times 10^6\ \text{ohm} = \mathbf{2.73\ megaohm}$$

12.4 MEASUREMENT OF HIGH RESISTANCE BY LEAKAGE (background, not in the listed sections)

Principle. When a capacitor of capacitance $C$ and initial charge $Q_0$ is allowed to discharge through a resistance $R$ for a time $t$, the charge remaining on the capacitor is given by

$$Q = Q_0 e^{-t/CR}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{Q_0}{Q} = e^{t/CR}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \log_e\frac{Q_0}{Q} = \frac{t}{CR}$$
$$\therefore\quad R = \frac{t}{C\log_e(Q_0/Q)} = \frac{t}{2.3026\,C\log_{10}(Q_0/Q)}$$

If $R$ is high, $CR$ will be high and the rate of discharge of capacitor will be very slow. Thus if we determine $Q_0/Q$ from experiment, then $R$ can be calculated.

Circuit diagram. Connections are made as shown in Fig. 12.8. $C$ is a capacitor of known capacitance, $R$ is the high resistance to be measured, B.G. is a ballistic galvanometer, $E$ is a cell, and $K_1$, $K_2$, $K_3$ are tap keys.

FIG. 12.8: capacitor C, resistance R, ballistic galvanometer B.G., cell E and keys K1, K2, K3

Procedure (i) Keeping $K_2$ and $K_3$ open, the capacitor is charged by depressing the key $K_1$. $K_1$ is then opened and at once $K_3$ is closed. The capacitor discharges through the galvanometer which records a throw $\theta_0$. The throw $\theta_0$ is proportional to $Q_0$ i.e., $Q_0 \propto \theta_0$.

(ii) The capacitor is again charged to the maximum value keeping $K_2$ and $K_3$ open and closing $K_1$. $K_1$ is then opened and $K_2$ is closed for a known time $t$. Some of the charge leaks through $R$. $K_2$ is opened and at once $K_3$ is closed. The charge $Q$ remaining on the capacitor then discharges through the galvanometer. The resulting throw $\theta$ is noted. Then $Q \propto \theta$.

Now,

$$\frac{Q_0}{Q} = \frac{\theta_0}{\theta}\qquad\therefore\quad R = \frac{t}{2.3026\,C\log_{10}(\theta_0/\theta)}$$

A series of values of $t$ and $\theta$ are obtained.

Graph. A graph is plotted between $t$ and $\log_{10}(\theta_0/\theta)$ which is a straight line (Fig. 12.9). Its slope gives the mean value of $\dfrac{t}{\log_{10}(\theta_0/\theta)}$. As $C$ is known, the value of $R$ can be calculated.

FIG. 12.9: straight-line graph of log10(θ0/θ) against t, with a slope triangle

Example 1. If the charge on a capacitor of capacitance 2 $\mu$F in leaking through a high resistance of 100 megaohms is reduced to half its maximum value, calculate the time of leakage.

Solution.

$$R = \frac{t}{2.3026\,C\log_{10}(\theta_0/\theta)}$$

Here, $C = 2\times 10^{-6}$ F, $R = 10^8\ \Omega$, $Q_0/Q = 2$.

$$\therefore\quad t = 2.3026\,CR\log_{10}(Q_0/Q)$$
$$= 2.3026\,(2\times 10^{-6})\,10^8\log_{10}2 = 138.7\ \text{s.}$$

The observed kick was 12 cm on the scale. The capacitor was fully charged again and allowed to leak through $R$ for 2 sec. 12.5 CHARGING AND DISCHARGING OF A CAPACITOR THROUGH AN INDUCTANCE

(i) Charging of a capacitor through an inductance. A capacitance $C$ and an inductance $L$ are joined to a battery through a Morse key (Fig. 12.10).

  • When the key is pressed, a momentary current flows through the inductance $L$ and the capacitor starts getting charged.
  • $E$ is the emf of the cell.

$Q$ is the charge on the capacitor at any instant $t$. The emf equation of the circuit is

FIG. 12.10: capacitor C, inductance L, Morse key and cell E
$$L\frac{dI}{dt} + \frac{Q}{C} = E\qquad(1)$$
$$\text{But}\quad I = \frac{dQ}{dt}\quad\text{and}\quad \frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{d^2Q}{dt^2}$$
$$\therefore\quad L\frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + \frac{Q}{C} = E$$
$$\text{or}\quad \frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + \frac{Q - CE}{LC} = 0$$
$$\text{OR}\quad \frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + \frac{1}{LC}(Q - Q_0) = 0\qquad(2)$$

($\because\ CE = Q_0$, the maximum charge associated with the capacitor.)

Eq. (2) is of the form $d^2x/dt^2 + p^2x = 0$, which is a differential equation for undamped simple harmonic motion of period, $T = 2\pi/p$. The time period of undamped oscillations is given by

$$T = \frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{LC}}} = 2\pi\sqrt{LC}\qquad(3)$$

Thus, the charging is oscillatory and the frequency of oscillation is given by

$$v = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}\qquad(4)$$

(ii) Discharge of a Capacitor through an Inductance. A capacitor $C$ and inductance $L$ are joined to a cell through a morse key (Fig. 12.11). The capacitor is initially charged to its maximum value $Q_0$ by pressing the morse key (with inductance out of the circuit).

  • When the key is now released, the capacitor discharges itself through the inductance $L$.
  • Let $I$ be the current and $Q$ charge left on the capacitor at any instant $t$.
  • There is no external source of emf during discharge.
FIG. 12.11: capacitor C, inductance L, Morse key and cell E (inductance out of the circuit while charging)

The equation of emf's is

$$\frac{Q}{C} + L\frac{dI}{dt} = 0\quad\text{or}\quad \frac{Q}{C} + L\frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} = 0\qquad\left[\because\ I = \frac{dQ}{dt}\ \text{and}\ \frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{d^2Q}{dt^2}\right]$$
$$\therefore\quad \frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + \frac{Q}{LC} = 0\qquad(1)$$

This is the differential equation representing undamped simple harmonic motion. Taking $\dfrac{1}{LC} = \omega^2$, we have

$$\frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + \omega^2 Q = 0$$

The general solution of this equation is

$$Q = A\sin(\omega t + \theta),\qquad(2)$$
$$\text{Here,}\quad \omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$$

When, $t = 0$, $Q = Q_0$, $\therefore\ Q_0 = A\sin\theta$.

When $\theta = \pi/2$, $A = Q_0$. $\therefore$ Eq. (2) becomes

$$Q = Q_0\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{LC}} + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\qquad(3)$$

Thus, $Q$ decreases sinusoidally or the discharge is oscillatory. The natural frequency $v$ of the circuit is given by

$$v = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}\qquad(4)$$

Importance in wireless telegraphy. The discharge of capacitor through an inductance is oscillatory and simple harmonic in nature.

  • When the capacitor is charged, the entire energy of the circuit is stored in the capacitor as electrostatic energy (Fig. 12.12 (a)).
  • During discharge of the capacitor, a current flows through the inductance coil for a very small time. This sets up a magnetic field within the coil and the whole of the energy is magnetic (Fig. 12.12 (b)).
  • When the oscillations are set up, the energy in the circuit is stored alternately in the electric field of capacitor and the magnetic field of the coil.

  • When the resistance of the circuit is low, a large number of oscillations take place per second. The changes in electric and magnetic fields give rise to electromagnetic radiations, which are propagated through space with the velocity of light.
  • The frequency of these oscillations is given by $v = \dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$.
  • These electromagnetic oscillations form the basis of wireless telegraphy. With the help of codes, the messages can be transmitted from one place to another.

13.1 EMF INDUCED IN A COIL ROTATING IN A MAGNETIC FIELD

Consider a rectangular coil of $N$ turns and of length $a$ and width $b$ rotating with uniform angular velocity $\omega$ about its axis in a uniform magnetic field $\mathbf B$ (Fig. 13.1).

FIG. 13.1: (a) coil between N and S poles with slip rings and resistance R; (b) coil at angle θ to B, with normal

The axis of rotation is at right angles to the field. As the coil rotates, the magnetic flux passing through it changes. Hence an emf is induced in the coil.

Suppose we start timing from the instant when the plane of the coil is at right angles to the field $\mathbf B$, i.e., when the angle between the normal to the plane of the coil and the direction of the field is zero. Then, at an instant $t$, the normal to the plane of the coil will make angle $\theta\ (= \omega t)$ with the direction of $\mathbf B$.

The magnetic flux linked with $N$ turns of the coil is

$$\phi = NBA\cos\theta = NBA\cos\omega t$$

where $A\ (= ab)$ is the area of the coil. The instantaneous induced emf,

$$E = -\frac{d\phi}{dt} = -\frac{d}{dt}(NBA\cos\omega t) = NBA\,\omega\sin\omega t = E_0\sin\omega t$$

Here, $E_0 = NBA\omega$, called the peak value of the e.m.f.

Now, $\omega = 2\pi\nu$ where $\nu$ = frequency of alternating voltage.

Thus, when $\omega t = 0$, $\sin\omega t = 0$ and $E = 0$;

$$\omega t = \pi/2,\ \sin\omega t = 1\ \text{and}\ E = E_0$$
$$\omega t = \pi,\ \sin\omega t = 0\ \text{and}\ E = 0$$
$$\omega t = 3\pi/2,\ \sin\omega t = -1,\ \text{and}\ E = -E_0$$
$$\text{and}\quad \omega t = 2\pi,\ \sin\omega t = 0\ \text{and}\ E = 0\ \text{again,}$$

A graph of $E$ against $\omega t$ is a sine curve (Fig. 13.2). Such an e.m.f. is called an 'alternating e.m.f.' The resulting current in the coil, if the coil is part of a closed circuit, is the 'alternating current.'

Peak Value of Alternating Current or emf. The maximum value of alternating current or emf in the positive or negative direction is called peak value of alternating current or emf. It is denoted by $I_0$ or $E_0$.

Mean Value of Alternating Current. Mean value of alternating current is defined as its average over half a cycle.

$$I_{mean} = \frac{\int_0^{T/2} 1\,dt}{T/2} = \frac{\int_0^{\pi/\omega} I_0\sin\omega t\,dt}{\pi/\omega} = \frac{I_0\omega}{\pi}\left[\frac{-\cos\omega t}{\omega}\right]_0^{\pi/\omega}$$

Root mean square value of an alternating current. It is defined as the square root of the average of $I^2$ during a complete cycle.

$$\overline{I^2} = \frac{\int_0^{2\pi/\omega} I^2\,dt}{2\pi/\omega} = \frac{\int_0^{2\pi/\omega} I_0^2\sin^2\omega t\,dt}{2\pi/\omega}$$

Similarly, $E_{rms} = \dfrac{E_0}{\sqrt 2} = 0.707\,E_0$.

The potential across the plates of the capacitor $= Q/C$.

$$\therefore\quad L\frac{dI}{dt} + RI + \frac{Q}{C} = E_0\sin\omega t.$$

Differentiating with respect to $t$,

$$L\frac{d^2I}{dt^2} + R\frac{dI}{dt} + \frac{1}{C}\frac{dQ}{dt} = E_0\,\omega\cos\omega t\qquad(1)$$

Let the trial solution be of the form

$$I = I_0\sin(\omega t - \phi)\qquad(2)$$

where $I_0$ and $\phi$ are constants to be determined.

$$\frac{dI}{dt} = I_0\omega\cos(\omega t - \phi)\quad\text{and}\quad \frac{d^2I}{dt^2} = -I_0\omega^2\sin(\omega t - \phi)$$

$$-LI_0\omega^2\sin(\omega t - \phi) + RI_0\omega\cos(\omega t - \phi) + \frac{I_0}{C}\sin(\omega t - \phi) = E_0\,\omega\cos\omega t$$

$$\left(-L\omega^2 + \frac{1}{C}\right)I_0 = -E_0\omega\sin\phi\qquad(3)$$

Acceptor Circuit. The series resonant circuit is often called an 'acceptor' circuit. By offering minimum impedance to currents at the resonant frequency, it is able to select or accept most readily the current of this one frequency from among those of many frequencies.

Voltage Magnification. At resonance, the (peak) current through the circuit is

LCR Circuit (Series Resonance Circuit) [Second Method]. Consider a circuit containing an inductance $L$, a capacitance $C$ and a resistance $R$ joined in series. This series circuit is connected to an AC supply given by

The total complex impedance is

$$Z = Z_R + Z_L + Z_C = R + j\left(\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}\right)$$
$$= \sqrt{R^2 + (\omega L - 1/\omega C)^2}\,e^{j\phi}\qquad\text{where}\ \tan\phi = \frac{(\omega L - 1/\omega C)}{R}\qquad(2)$$

Using Ohm's law in complex form, the 'complex' current in the circuit is

$$I = \frac{E}{Z} = \frac{E_0 e^{j\omega t}}{\sqrt{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2}\,e^{j\phi}}$$
$$\therefore\quad I = \frac{E_0}{\sqrt{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2}}\,e^{j(\omega t - \phi)}\qquad(3)$$

The actual emf is the imaginary part of the equivalent complex emf. Hence the actual current in the circuit is obtained by taking the imaginary part of the above 'complex' current.

$$\therefore\quad i = \operatorname{Im}(I) = \frac{E_0}{\sqrt{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2}}\sin(\omega t - \phi)\qquad(5)$$

The equivalent impedance of the series LCR circuit is

$$\sqrt{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2}$$

The current 'lags' behind the voltage by an angle

$$\phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{(\omega L - 1/\omega C)}{R}$$

13.4 PARALLEL RESONANT CIRCUIT

Here, capacitor $C$ is connected in parallel to the series combination of resistance $R$ and inductance $L$. The combination is connected across the AC source (Fig. 13.22). The applied voltage is sinusoidal, represented by

$$E = E_0 e^{j\omega t}$$

Complex impedance of $L$-branch: $Z_1 = R + j L\omega$. Complex impedance of $C$-branch: $Z_2 = \dfrac{1}{jC\omega}$. $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ are in parallel.

$$\frac{1}{Z} = \frac{1}{R + j\omega L} + \frac{1}{1/j\omega C} = \frac{1}{R + j\omega L} + j\omega C$$
$$= \frac{R - j\omega L}{(R + j\omega L)\times(R - j\omega L)} + j\omega C = \frac{R}{R^2 + (L\omega)^2} + j\left[C\omega - \frac{L\omega}{R^2 + (L\omega)^2}\right]$$

The current $I = E/Z = E\times\dfrac{1}{Z}$,

$$\therefore\quad I = E\left[\frac{R}{R^2 + (L\omega)^2} + j\left(C\omega - \frac{L\omega}{R^2 + (L\omega)^2}\right)\right]$$
$$\text{Let}\ A\cos\phi = \frac{R}{R^2 + (L\omega)^2};\quad A\sin\phi = C\omega - \frac{L\omega}{R^2 + (L\omega)^2}$$
$$\therefore\quad I = E(A\cos\phi + jA\sin\phi) = EAe^{j\phi} = E_0 A\,e^{j(\omega t + \phi)}$$
$$\text{where}\quad \phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{C\omega - \dfrac{L\omega}{R^2 + (L\omega)^2}}{\dfrac{R}{R^2 + (L\omega)^2}};$$
346Electricity and Magnetism
$$A^2 = \frac{R^2}{(R^2 + \omega^2L^2)^2} + \left(C\omega - \frac{L\omega}{R^2 + \omega^2L^2}\right)^2$$

The magnitude of the admittance

$$Y = \frac{1}{Z} = \frac{\sqrt{\left[R^2 + (\omega CR^2 + \omega^3L^2C - \omega L)^2\right]}}{R^2 + \omega^2L^2}$$

The admittance will be minimum, when

$$\omega CR^2 + \omega^3L^2C - \omega L = 0$$
$$\text{or}\quad \omega = \omega_0 = \sqrt{\left[\frac{1}{LC} - \frac{R^2}{L^2}\right]}$$
$$\text{or}\quad \nu_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{1}{LC} - \frac{R^2}{L^2}}$$

This is the resonant frequency of the circuit.

If $R$ is very small so that $\dfrac{R^2}{L^2}$ is negligible compared to $\dfrac{1}{LC}$,

$$\nu_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(LC)}}$$

At such a minimum admittance, i.e., maximum impedance, the circuit current is minimum.

Impedance at Resonance. At resonance, $Z = \dfrac{R^2 + (L\omega)^2}{R}$. But $R^2 + (L\omega)^2 = \dfrac{L}{C}$ at resonance. $\therefore\ Z = \dfrac{L}{RC}$.

Comparison between series and parallel resonant circuits. The behaviour of a parallel resonant circuit is strikingly different from that of a series resonant circuit. In both cases the impedance is resistive but, whereas parallel resonance implies maximum impedance, series resonance implies minimum impedance.

13.5 POWER IN AC CIRCUIT CONTAINING RESISTANCE, INDUCTANCE AND CAPACITANCE

Consider an ac circuit containing resistance, inductance and capacitance. $E$ and $I$ vary continuously with time. Therefore power is calculated at any instant and then its mean is calculated over a complete cycle.

The instantaneous values of the voltage and current are given by

$$E = E_0\sin\omega t,\qquad I = I_0\sin(\omega t - \phi)$$

where $\phi$ is the phase difference between current and voltage. Hence power at any instant is

$$E\times I = E_0 I_0\sin\omega t\,\sin(\omega t - \phi)$$
348Electricity and Magnetism
$$= \frac{1}{2}E_0 I_0\left[\cos\phi - \cos(2\omega t - \phi)\right]\qquad(1)$$

Average power consumed over one complete cycle is

$$P = \frac{\int_0^T E I\,dt}{\int_0^T dt} = \frac{\int_0^T \frac{1}{2}E_0 I_0[\cos\phi - \cos(2\omega t - \phi)]\,dt}{T}$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}\frac{E_0 I_0}{T}\left[(\cos\phi)\,t - \frac{\sin(2\omega t - \phi)}{2\omega}\right]_0^T$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}\frac{E_0 I_0}{T}\left[(\cos\phi)\,T - 0 - \frac{\sin(2\omega T - \phi)}{2\omega} + \frac{\sin(-\phi)}{2\omega}\right]$$

Now, $T = \dfrac{2\pi}{\omega}$ and $\sin(4\pi - \phi) = \sin(-\phi)$.

$$P = \frac{1}{2}\frac{E_0 I_0\omega}{2\pi}\left[(\cos\phi)\frac{2\pi}{\omega} - \frac{\sin(-\phi)}{2\omega} + \frac{\sin(-\phi)}{2\omega}\right]$$
$$= \frac{1}{2}E_0 I_0\cos\phi = \frac{E_0}{\sqrt 2}\times\frac{I_0}{\sqrt 2}\times\cos\phi = E_{r.m.s.}\,I_{r.m.s.}\cdot\cos\phi\qquad(2)$$

average power $=$ (virtual volts) $\times$ (virtual amperes) $\times\ \cos\phi$.

The term (Virtual volts $\times$ Virtual amperes) is called apparent power and $\cos\phi$ is called the power factor. Thus

$$\text{True power} = \text{apparent power}\times\text{power factor.}$$

Evidently, the power factor is the ratio of the true power to the apparent power.

As $\cos\phi$ is the factor by which the product of the r.m.s. values of the voltage and current must be multiplied to give the power dissipated, it is known as the 'power factor' of the circuit. For a circuit containing resistance, capacitance and inductance in series,

$$\tan\phi = \frac{\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}}{R}$$

From Fig. 13.24, the expression for the power factor is

$$\cos\phi = \frac{R}{\sqrt{\left\{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2\right\}}}$$
FIG. 13.24: power-factor triangle with sides R, (ωL − 1/ωC) and impedance Z, angle φ

Special cases.

  1. In a purely resistive circuit, $\phi = 0$ or $\cos\phi = 1$, $\therefore$ true power $= E_v\times I_v$.
349Alternating Current
  1. In a purely inductive circuit, current lags behind the applied emf by $90°$ so that $\phi = 90°$ or $\cos\phi = 0$. Thus true power consumed $= 0$.
  2. In a purely capacitive circuit, current leads the applied voltage by $90°$ so that $\phi = -90°$ or $\cos(-90°) = \cos 90° = 0$. $\therefore$ true power $= 0$.
  3. In an ac circuit containing a resistance and inductance in series, $\text{Power factor},\ \cos\phi = \dfrac{R}{\sqrt{R^2 + (L\omega)^2}}$.
  4. In an ac circuit containing a capacitance $C$ and a resistance $R$ in series, $\cos\phi = \dfrac{R}{\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{1}{C^2\omega^2} + R^2\right)}}$.

Wattless current. The average power dissipated during a complete cycle is $E_v I_v\cdot\cos\phi$. The current in A.C. circuit is said to be wattless when the average power consumed in the circuit is zero.

If an ac circuit is purely inductive or purely capacitive with no ohmic resistance, phase angle $\phi = \pi/2$ so that $\cos\phi = 0$ or the power consumed is zero. The current in such a circuit does not perform any useful work and is rightly called the wattless or idle current. In this situation, the circuit does not consume any power, though it offers a resistance to the flow of alternating current in it. It is the principle of choke coil.

Example 1. An alternating voltage of 10 volts at 100 Hz is applied to a choke of inductance 5 henry and of resistance of 200 ohms. Find the power factor of the coil and the power absorbed.

Solution. Here, $E_v = 10\ V$, $\nu = 100\ Hz$, $L = 5\ H$, and $R = 200\ \Omega$.

$$\text{Power factor}, \cos\phi = \frac{R}{\sqrt{R^2 + \omega^2 L^2}} = \frac{200}{\sqrt{(200)^2 + (2\pi\times 100\times 5)^2}} = 0.062$$
$$\text{Power absorbed} = E_v\cdot I_v\cdot\cos\phi = E_v\cdot\frac{E_v}{Z}\cos\phi = \frac{(E_v)^2}{\sqrt{R^2 + \omega^2 L^2}}\cos\phi = \frac{10\times 10\,(0.062)}{\sqrt{(200)^2 + (2\pi\times 100\times 5)^2}} = 0.00189\ \text{W}$$
