$$I_L = \frac{E_0}{\omega L}\sin\left(\omega t - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$$

Here $\omega L$ is the reactance of the inductance. The current through the capacitor leads the applied emf by $\pi/2$ and is given by

$$I_C = \frac{E_0}{(1/\omega C)}\sin\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$$

Here, $(1/\omega C)$ is the reactance of the capacitance. The total current through the circuit is given by

$$I = I_L + I_C = \frac{E_0}{\omega L}\sin\left(\omega t - \frac{\pi}{2}\right) + \frac{E_0}{1/\omega C}\sin\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$$
$$= -\frac{E_0}{\omega L}\cos\omega t + \frac{E_0}{(1/\omega C)}\cos\omega t$$
$$\text{or}\quad I = E_0\left(\omega C - \frac{1}{\omega L}\right)\cos\omega t\qquad(1)$$

If for a particular frequency $\nu_0$, $\omega C = \dfrac{1}{\omega L}$, then from Eq. (1), the current becomes zero.

The circuit now offers an infinite impedance. Such a circuit which offers an infinite impedance to the A.C. is called a parallel resonant circuit. The frequency is called the resonant frequency. Thus at $\nu_0$, we have,

$$\omega C = \frac{1}{\omega L}\quad\text{or}\quad \omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}\quad\text{or}\quad 2\pi\nu_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}\quad\therefore\quad \nu_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}\qquad(2)$$

13.3 AC CIRCUIT CONTAINING RESISTANCE, INDUCTANCE AND CAPACITANCE IN SERIES (SERIES RESONANCE CIRCUIT)

Let an alternating emf $E = E_0\sin\omega t$ be applied to a circuit containing a resistance $R$, inductance $L$ and capacitance $C$ in series (Fig. 13.16). Let at any instant, $I$ be the current in the circuit and $Q$ be the charge on the capacitor.

The potential drop across the resistance $= RI$. The E.M.F. induced in the inductance $= L\dfrac{dI}{dt}$.

FIG. 13.16: series L, R and C across E = E0 sin ωt
339Alternating Current

The potential across the plates of the capacitor $= Q/C$.

$$\therefore\quad L\frac{dI}{dt} + RI + \frac{Q}{C} = E_0\sin\omega t.$$

Differentiating with respect to $t$,

$$L\frac{d^2I}{dt^2} + R\frac{dI}{dt} + \frac{1}{C}\frac{dQ}{dt} = E_0\,\omega\cos\omega t\qquad(1)$$

Let the trial solution be of the form

$$I = I_0\sin(\omega t - \phi)\qquad(2)$$

where $I_0$ and $\phi$ are constants to be determined.

$$\frac{dI}{dt} = I_0\omega\cos(\omega t - \phi)\quad\text{and}\quad \frac{d^2I}{dt^2} = -I_0\omega^2\sin(\omega t - \phi)$$

Substituting these value of $I$, $\dfrac{dI}{dt}$ and $\dfrac{d^2I}{dt^2}$, in Eq. (1), we get

$$-LI_0\omega^2\sin(\omega t - \phi) + RI_0\omega\cos(\omega t - \phi) + \frac{I_0}{C}\sin(\omega t - \phi) = E_0\,\omega\cos\omega t$$
$$\text{or}\quad \left(-L\omega^2 + \frac{1}{C}\right)I_0\sin(\omega t - \phi) + R\omega I_0\cos(\omega t - \phi) = E_0\omega[\cos(\omega t - \phi) + \phi]$$
$$= E_0\omega[\cos(\omega t - \phi)\cos\phi - \sin(\omega t - \phi)\sin\phi]$$

Equating the coefficients of $\sin(\omega t - \phi)$ and $\cos(\omega t - \phi)$ on either side,

$$\left(-L\omega^2 + \frac{1}{C}\right)I_0 = -E_0\omega\sin\phi\qquad(3)$$
$$\text{and}\quad R\omega I_0 = E_0\omega\cos\phi.\qquad(4)$$

Dividing Eq. (3) by Eq. (4), we get

$$\tan\phi = -\frac{\left(-L\omega^2 + \dfrac{1}{C}\right)}{R\omega} = \frac{\omega L - \dfrac{1}{C\omega}}{R}\qquad(5)$$

Squaring and adding Eqs. (3) and (4), we get

$$I_0^2\left[\left(-L\omega^2 + \frac{1}{C}\right)^2 + R^2\omega^2\right] = E_0^2\omega^2$$
$$\text{or}\quad I_0^2\left[R^2 + \left(\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2\right] = E_0^2$$
$$\text{or}\quad I_0 = \frac{E_0}{\sqrt{\left[R^2 + \left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2\right]}}\qquad(6)$$
340Electricity and Magnetism

Substituting the value of $I_0$ in Eq. (2), we get

$$I = \frac{E_0}{\sqrt{\left[R^2 + \left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2\right]}}\sin(\omega t - \phi)\qquad(7)$$

where $\phi = \tan^{-1}\dfrac{\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}}{R}$.

Eq. (7) represents the current at any instant.

The quantity $\sqrt{\left[R^2 + \left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2\right]}$ is the impedance $Z$ of the circuit.

$L\omega$ and $1/\omega C$ respectively represent inductive reactance $X_L$ and capacitive reactance $X_C$. Thus

$$Z = \sqrt{\left[R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2\right]}$$

The current lags in phase behind e.m.f. by an angle

$$\phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}}{R} = \tan^{-1}\frac{X_L - X_C}{R}$$

The following three cases arise:

  • (i) When $X_L > X_C$, $\phi$ is positive so that the current lags behind the applied emf.
  • (ii) When $X_L < X_C$, $\phi$ is negative, so that the current leads the applied emf.
  • (iii) When $X_L = X_C$, $\phi = 0$, and the current is in phase with the emf.

Series Resonant Circuit. The value of current at any instant in a series $LCR$ circuit is given by

$$I = \frac{E_0}{\sqrt{\left\{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{C\omega}\right)^2\right\}}}\sin(\omega t - \phi)\qquad(1)$$

where $\sqrt{\left\{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{C\omega}\right)^2\right\}} = Z$ is called the impedance of the circuit.

At a particular frequency, $\omega L = \dfrac{1}{\omega C}$ so that the impedance becomes minimum being given by $Z = R$. This particular frequency $\nu_0$ at which the impedance of the circuit becomes minimum and, therefore the current becomes maximum, is called the resonant frequency of the circuit. Such a circuit which admits maximum current is called series resonant circuit.

Thus at $\nu_0$, we have

$$\omega L = \frac{1}{\omega C}\quad\text{or}\quad 2\pi\nu_0 L = \frac{1}{2\pi\nu_0 C}\quad\text{or}\quad \nu_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$$

The maximum current in the circuit $= I_0 = E_0/R$. The variation of current with frequency of applied voltage is shown in Fig. 13.17. The sharpness of peak depends upon the resistance $R$ of the circuit. For low resistance, the peak is sharp.

Acceptor Circuit. The series resonant circuit is often called an 'acceptor' circuit. By offering minimum impedance to currents at the resonant frequency, it is able to select or accept most readily the current of this one frequency from among those of many frequencies.

In radio receivers, the resonant frequency of the circuit is tuned (by changing $C$) to the frequency of the signal desired to be detected.

Voltage Magnification. At resonance, the (peak) current through the circuit is

$$I_0 = \frac{E_0}{R}$$

The (peak) voltage across the inductance is

$$V_L = X_L I_0 = \omega L\times\frac{E_0}{R} = \frac{\omega L}{R}(E_0) = Q\,E_0,$$

where $Q$ is known as the quality factor of the circuit. Thus at resonance the voltage drop across $L$ (which is also the voltage across $C$) is $Q$ times the applied voltage. Hence the chief characteristic of the series resonant circuit is 'voltage magnification'. This voltage magnification does not increase the power in the circuit as the reactive component of the power is wattless.

The Q-factor.

$$\text{Q-factor} = \frac{\text{Reactance of the coil at resonance}}{\text{Resistance of the circuit}} = \frac{L\omega_0}{R}$$

Q-factor determines the degree of selectivity of the circuit while tuning. This is because, for larger values of Q-factor the frequency response curve of the circuit is a steep narrow peak. For smaller values of Q-factor, the frequency response curve is quite flat (Fig. 13.18).

Sharpness of resonance of a series L-C-R circuit. The sharpness of resonance is defined as the rapidity with which the current falls from its resonant value $(E_0/R)$ with change in applied frequency.

A curve with low value of $R$ falls very sharply on either side than that with high value of $R$ (Fig. 13.18). Resonance in the first case is said to be sharper than in the second.

j operator method.

Use of operator $j$ in study of A.C. Circuits. The operator $j$ is defined as a quantity which is numerically equal to $\sqrt{(-1)}$ and which represents the rotation of a vector through $90°$ in anticlockwise direction, $-j$ represents the rotation of a vector through $90°$ in clockwise direction. The above facts about $j$ are helpful in studying the A.C. circuits. We know that in A.C. circuits, $E_L$ and $E_C$ always lie at $90°$ in anticlockwise and clockwise direction respectively with respect to $E_R$ (Fig. 13.20). Hence total emf of a circuit having $L$, $C$, $R$ will be

$$E = E_R + jE_L - jE_C$$

Ordinarily a source of alternating e.m.f. $E$ is denoted by $E_0\sin\omega t$.

This is actually the imaginary part of the complex form of alternating e.m.f.,

$$E = E_0 e^{j\omega t}$$

The instantaneous current in the A.C. circuit has been expressed as $I = I_0\sin(\omega t - \phi)$. This expression is the imaginary part of the complex current given by

$$I = I_0 e^{j(\omega t - \phi)}$$

Since the voltage across the inductor leads the current passing through it by $90°$, the inductive reactance $\omega L$ can be written as $j\omega L$. $Z_L = j\omega L = jX_L$.

Since the voltage across the capacitor lags the current passing through it by $90°$, the capacitive reactance $1/\omega C$ can be written as

$$-\frac{j}{\omega C} = \frac{1}{j\omega C},\qquad Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C} = -\frac{j}{\omega C} = -jX_C$$

LCR Circuit (Series Resonance Circuit) [Second Method]. Consider a circuit containing an inductance $L$, a capacitance $C$ and a resistance $R$ joined in series. This series circuit is connected to an AC supply given by

$$Z = Z_R + Z_L + Z_C = R + j\left(\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}\right)$$

$$I = \frac{E}{Z} = \frac{E_0 e^{j\omega t}}{\sqrt{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2}\,e^{j\phi}}$$

Example 1. A circuit consists of a non-inductive resistance of 50 ohms, an inductance of 0.3 henry, and a resistance of 2 ohms and a capacitor of 40 micro-farad in series and is supplied with 200 volts at 50 Hz. Find the impedance, the current, lag or lead, and the power in the circuit.

Solution. Total resistance $= R = 50 + 2 = 52\ \Omega$.

$$C = 40\times 10^{-6}\ \text{F},\ L = 0.3\ \text{H},\ \omega = 2\pi\nu = 2\pi\times 50 = 100\pi$$
$$E_{r.m.s.} = 200\ \text{V.}$$
$$\text{Impedance} = Z = \sqrt{R^2 + \left(L\omega - \frac{1}{C\omega}\right)^2} = \sqrt{(52)^2 + \left(0.3\times 100\pi - \frac{1}{40\times 10^{-6}\times 100\pi}\right)^2} = 53.97\ \Omega$$
$$\therefore\quad \text{current}\ I_{r.m.s.} = \frac{E_{rms}}{Z} = \frac{200}{53.97} = 3.71\ \text{A.}\qquad I_{max} = 3.71\times\sqrt 2\ \text{A}$$
$$\text{Now,}\quad X_L = L\omega = (100\pi)\times 0.3 = 94.2\ \Omega$$
$$X_C = \frac{-1}{C\omega} = \frac{1}{(40\times 10^{-6})\times 100\pi} = 79.6\ \Omega$$

Since $X_L > X_C$, the current lags behind the applied e.m.f.

$$\phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{L\omega - 1/C\omega}{R} = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{94.2 - 79.6}{52}\right) = \tan^{-1}(0.28) = 15°\,39'$$
$$\text{Power factor}\ \cos\phi = \frac{R}{\sqrt{[R^2 + (L\omega - 1/C\omega)^2]}} = \frac{52}{[(52)^2 + (14.6)^2]^{1/2}} = 0.964$$
$$\text{Apparent power} = E_{rms}\times I_{rms} = 200\times 3.71 = 742\ \text{V.A.}$$
$$\text{True power} = \text{Apparent power}\times\text{Power factor} = 742\times 0.964 = 716\ \text{watt}$$

13.4 PARALLEL RESONANT CIRCUIT

Here, capacitor $C$ is connected in parallel to the series combination of resistance $R$ and inductance $L$. The combination is connected across the AC source (Fig. 13.22). The applied voltage is sinusoidal, represented by

$$E = E_0 e^{j\omega t}$$

Complex impedance of $L$-branch: $Z_1 = R + j L\omega$. Complex impedance of $C$-branch: $Z_2 = \dfrac{1}{jC\omega}$. $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ are in parallel.

$$\frac{1}{Z} = \frac{1}{R + j\omega L} + \frac{1}{1/j\omega C} = \frac{1}{R + j\omega L} + j\omega C$$
$$= \frac{R - j\omega L}{(R + j\omega L)\times(R - j\omega L)} + j\omega C = \frac{R}{R^2 + (L\omega)^2} + j\left[C\omega - \frac{L\omega}{R^2 + (L\omega)^2}\right]$$

The current $I = E/Z = E\times\dfrac{1}{Z}$,

$$\therefore\quad I = E\left[\frac{R}{R^2 + (L\omega)^2} + j\left(C\omega - \frac{L\omega}{R^2 + (L\omega)^2}\right)\right]$$
$$\text{Let}\ A\cos\phi = \frac{R}{R^2 + (L\omega)^2};\quad A\sin\phi = C\omega - \frac{L\omega}{R^2 + (L\omega)^2}$$
$$\therefore\quad I = E(A\cos\phi + jA\sin\phi) = EAe^{j\phi} = E_0 A\,e^{j(\omega t + \phi)}$$
$$\text{where}\quad \phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{C\omega - \dfrac{L\omega}{R^2 + (L\omega)^2}}{\dfrac{R}{R^2 + (L\omega)^2}};$$
346Electricity and Magnetism
$$A^2 = \frac{R^2}{(R^2 + \omega^2L^2)^2} + \left(C\omega - \frac{L\omega}{R^2 + \omega^2L^2}\right)^2$$

The magnitude of the admittance

$$Y = \frac{1}{Z} = \frac{\sqrt{\left[R^2 + (\omega CR^2 + \omega^3L^2C - \omega L)^2\right]}}{R^2 + \omega^2L^2}$$

The admittance will be minimum, when

$$\omega CR^2 + \omega^3L^2C - \omega L = 0$$
$$\text{or}\quad \omega = \omega_0 = \sqrt{\left[\frac{1}{LC} - \frac{R^2}{L^2}\right]}$$
$$\text{or}\quad \nu_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{1}{LC} - \frac{R^2}{L^2}}$$

This is the resonant frequency of the circuit.

If $R$ is very small so that $\dfrac{R^2}{L^2}$ is negligible compared to $\dfrac{1}{LC}$,

$$\nu_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(LC)}}$$

At such a minimum admittance, i.e., maximum impedance, the circuit current is minimum.

Impedance at Resonance. At resonance, $Z = \dfrac{R^2 + (L\omega)^2}{R}$. But $R^2 + (L\omega)^2 = \dfrac{L}{C}$ at resonance. $\therefore\ Z = \dfrac{L}{RC}$.

Thus smaller the resistance $R$, larger is the impedance. If $R$ is negligible, the impedance is infinite at resonance.

Rejector Circuit. The parallel resonant circuit does not allow the current of the same frequency as the natural frequency of the circuit. Thus it can be used to suppress the current of this particular frequency out of currents of many other frequencies. Hence the circuit is known as a 'rejector' or 'filter' circuit.

Comparison between series and parallel resonant circuits. The behaviour of a parallel resonant circuit is strikingly different from that of a series resonant circuit. In both cases the impedance is resistive but, whereas parallel resonance implies maximum impedance, series resonance implies minimum impedance.
