7.1 Introduction

Semiconductors have conductivities between those of conductors and insulators. Their resistivity lies between $10^{-5}$ and $10^{+4}$ ohm-m. Elemental semiconductors (Ge, Si) come from Group IV; compound semiconductors (GaAs, InP, CdS) come from Groups III and V, or II and VI. The band gap is small (0.2 to 2.5 eV), which gives the wavelengths used in LEDs and lasers. The resistance falls with temperature, and the conductivity can be controlled by doping.

7.2 Pure or intrinsic semiconductors

An intrinsic semiconductor is undoped. In silicon, each atom forms four covalent bonds, so the crystal is an insulator at 0 K (Fig. 7.1a). Thermal breaking of a bond frees an electron and leaves a hole; both carry current (Fig. 7.1b). At 0 K the valence band is full and the conduction band is empty (Fig. 7.2). Thermal excitation populates the conduction band and leaves an equal number of holes in the valence band, so $n_i=p_i$.

{{FIG:m3-f71}} {{FIG:m3-f72}}

Holes have a larger effective mass and a lower mobility than electrons. Their charge is equal and opposite to the electron charge.

Recombination removes an electron-hole pair and releases the band gap energy as radiation. In equilibrium the generation rate $g$ equals the recombination rate $R$. Since $R$ depends on the carrier densities, $R=C\,n_i p_i$, and with $n_i=p_i$:

$$g=R=C\,n_i^2\tag{7.1}$$

$C$ depends on the material; it is related to the densities of states at the band edges (see §7.5).

7.3 Impurity or extrinsic semiconductors

Doping with Group V (donor) or Group III (acceptor) atoms, at about $1$ in $10^{6}$ to $10^{8}$ atoms, changes the conductivity without changing the crystal structure. Donor atoms give excess electrons; acceptor atoms give excess holes.

7.3.1 Donor or $n$-type semiconductor

A pentavalent atom (P, As, Sb) forms four bonds, and its fifth electron is weakly bound (Fig. 7.3a). The donor level lies just below the conduction band, about 0.01 eV below in Ge and 0.03 eV below in Si, so the electron is easily promoted. The ionised donor is a fixed positive ion. Electrons are the majority carriers and holes the minority carriers. The product $np$ is fixed (Eq. 7.2):

{{FIG:m3-f73}}
$$np=n_i p_i=n_i^2\tag{7.2}$$

Equation (7.2) is the law of mass action, derived in §7.5.4.

7.3.2 Acceptor or $p$-type semiconductor

A trivalent atom (B, Al, Ga, In) has one bond incomplete. It accepts an electron from a neighbour, which leaves a hole (Fig. 7.4a). The acceptor level lies about 0.01 eV above the valence band in Ge and 0.046 to 0.16 eV above it in Si. Holes are the majority carriers, electrons the minority carriers, and $np=n_i^2$ still holds. The crystal remains neutral, because the ionised acceptor is a fixed negative ion balanced by the mobile holes.
