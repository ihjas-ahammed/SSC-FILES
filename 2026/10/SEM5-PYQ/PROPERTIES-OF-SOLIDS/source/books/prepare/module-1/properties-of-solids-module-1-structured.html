Before you start

Plus Two recall: area and volume from vector products

Recall. The cross product $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$ has magnitude $ab\sin\theta$, the area of the parallelogram formed by $\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b$.

Re-derive.

  1. Parallelogram with sides $a$, $b$ and angle $\theta$: base $a$, height $b\sin\theta$, area $ab\sin\theta=|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b|$.
  2. Parallelepiped with third edge $\mathbf c$: volume = base area × height, height $= |\mathbf c\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}|$ with $\hat{\mathbf n}=(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)/|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b|$.
  3. Multiply: $V=|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b|\,|\mathbf c\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}|=|\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)|$.

Saved. $V=|\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)|$ (book §1.3, not numbered).

1.1 Definition of a lattice

A point lattice (space lattice) is an infinite array of imaginary points in three-dimensional space in which every point has identical surroundings. The lattice has the same appearance seen from any of its points. This holds only if the points form a repeating pattern unit, moved by a translation operation.

1.2 Translation vectors

The translation operation is

$$\mathbf T = n_1\mathbf a + n_2\mathbf b + n_3\mathbf c \tag{1.1}$$

where $n_1$, $n_2$, $n_3$ are arbitrary integers and $\mathbf a$, $\mathbf b$, $\mathbf c$ are the fundamental translation vectors. The relation between two lattice points is

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf r' &= \mathbf r + \mathbf T \\ &= \mathbf r + n_1\mathbf a + n_2\mathbf b + n_3\mathbf c\end{aligned} \tag{1.2}$$

A perfect lattice satisfies Eq. (1.2) for every integer choice. An imperfect lattice does not admit such vectors.

Result 1: primitive and non-primitive vectors

Vectors whose translation operation has integral coefficients are primitive. Vectors that need non-integral coefficients, for example $\mathbf R'=\mathbf R+\tfrac12\mathbf a_2+\tfrac12\mathbf b_2$, are non-primitive. The book prefers an orthogonal, shortest set (Fig. 1.1).

1.3 Unit cell

A unit cell is a parallelogram (2D) or parallelepiped (3D) built from the translation vectors. Repetition of its translation generates the full lattice. A cell is primitive when its lattice points lie at its corners only, so that its effective count is one. A non-primitive cell has points at other positions inside or on its faces, so its effective count is greater than one (Fig. 1.2).

The Wigner–Seitz cell is constructed by drawing the bisectors of the lines from a lattice point to its neighbours; the smallest enclosed volume is primitive (Fig. 1.3).

The conventional unit cell is chosen for convenience; it is the primitive cell of least volume, unless a non-primitive cell shows higher symmetry.

1.4 Basis and crystal structure

A basis is an atom or group of atoms placed identically at every lattice point. The combination is the crystal structure:

Space lattice + Basis → Crystal structure

A lattice is mathematical; a crystal structure is physical. Fourteen lattices suffice because the bases vary.

Result 2: effective lattice points

A lattice point at a corner is shared by eight cells (weight 1/8); at a face centre by two (1/2); a point inside belongs wholly to the cell (weight 1). Eq. (1.5) gives the count, checked in §1.7.

Summary

  • Translations (Eqs. 1.1 and 1.2) define the lattice.
  • Primitive cells have one effective lattice point; non-primitive cells have more.
  • Crystal structure = lattice + basis.

1.5–1.6 Symmetry operations, point groups and space groups (structured notes)

Before you start

  • Lattice translation: $\mathbf T=n_1\mathbf a+n_2\mathbf b+n_3\mathbf c$ (Eq. 1.1).
  • Rotation by $\theta$ about an axis; reflection in a plane; inversion $\mathbf r\to-\mathbf r$.
  • Plus Two recall: full turn $2\pi$ (box below).
Plus Two recall: full turn and integral multiples

Recall. A full turn is $2\pi$. A rotation that returns a figure to itself after $n$ steps satisfies $n\theta = 2\pi$.

Re-derive.

  1. Let $\theta$ be the smallest rotation that maps the lattice to itself.
  2. Applying it $n$ times gives a total turn $n\theta$, which must equal $2\pi$ (one full turn).
  3. Therefore $\theta=2\pi/n$, and $n$ is a positive integer.

Saved. $n\theta=2\pi$ (Eq. 1.3).

1.5 Symmetry operations

The four basic operations are:

  1. Translation $\mathbf r\to\mathbf r+\mathbf T$, defined by Eq. (1.1). Applies to lattices only.
  2. Rotation by $\theta$ about a point (2D) or axis (3D).
  3. Reflection in a plane $m$ (line in 2D).
  4. Inversion $\mathbf r\to-\mathbf r$ about a centre $\bar 1$ (3D only).

Rotation: since the lattice repeats after a full turn, the rotation angle must divide $2\pi$ into an integer number of parts. The multiplicity $n$ satisfies

$$n\theta = 2\pi \quad\text{or}\quad \theta=\frac{2\pi}{n} \tag{1.3}$$

Compatibility with translations restricts $n$ to 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6 (Fig. 1.5).

1.5.1 Proof that a five-fold axis is excluded

Consider points A, B, C, D on a row, with $\mathbf T$ the lattice translation between neighbours (Fig. 1.6). Rotation by $\theta=2\pi/n$ about B (clockwise) and about C (anticlockwise) gives $B'$ and $C'$. These must be lattice points, so $B'C'$ is an integral multiple of $BC$:

$$\begin{gathered}B'C' = m\,(BC) \\ 2T\cos\theta+T=mT \\ \cos\theta=\frac{m-1}{2}\end{gathered} \tag{1.4}$$

with $m$ an integer. The inequality $|\cos\theta|\le1$ restricts $m\in\{3,2,1,0,-1\}$, giving $\theta\in\{0^\circ,60^\circ,90^\circ,120^\circ,180^\circ\}$ and therefore $n\in\{1,6,4,3,2\}$. The five-fold rotation is excluded.

1.5.2 Reflections and inversion

A reflection plane divides the lattice into mirror-image halves (Fig. 1.8).

Combining each of the five allowed rotations with or without a mirror gives the ten two-dimensional point groups $1,\,1m,\,2,\,2mm,\,3,\,3m,\,4,\,4mm,\,6,\,6mm$ (Fig. 1.9, book). The three-dimensional case adds the inversion $\bar 1$ and its combinations, giving 32 point groups.

Result 2: counts

Two dimensions: 10 point groups, 5 Bravais lattices, 17 space groups. Three dimensions: 32 point groups, 14 Bravais lattices, 230 space groups.

1.6 Point groups and space groups

A point group is the set of point operations that leave one point of the crystal fixed. The point symmetry of the crystal is that of the lattice together with the basis. A unit cell may have point symmetry at more than one location. The full set of symmetry elements, including translations, is the space group.

Summary

  • Allowed rotation multiplicities: $n=1,2,3,4,6$ (Eqs. 1.3 and 1.4).
  • Ten 2D point groups; 32 3D point groups; 17 and 230 space groups.

$$N = N_i + \frac{N_f}{2} + \frac{N_c}{8} \tag{1.5}$$

1.7.4 Why only fourteen

The orthorhombic system has four lattices, the cubic three and the tetragonal two. The extra cells are not new arrangements: two face-centred tetragonal lattices placed side by side give a body-centred tetragonal lattice (Fig. 1.12), which is already listed.

Result: fourteen Bravais lattices

Five in two dimensions, fourteen in three. Every crystal structure is one of these lattices with a basis.

1.8.1 Lattice directions

A direction is indexed by $[uvw]$: take a point on the line through the origin, read its coordinates in units of the axes, and reduce them to the smallest integers. A negative component carries a bar. Families: $\langle100\rangle$ (six cube edges), $\langle110\rangle$ (twelve face diagonals), $\langle111\rangle$ (eight body diagonals). Example (Fig. 1.14): AB with projections $-1$, $1$, $0$ is $[\bar1\,1\,0]$.

1.8.2 Angle between directions

$$\cos\theta = \frac{hh' + kk' + ll'}{(h^2+k^2+l^2)^{1/2}\,(h'^2+k'^2+l'^2)^{1/2}} \tag{1.6}$$
Plus Two recall: dot product

Recall. $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=|\mathbf u||\mathbf v|\cos\theta=\sum u_iv_i$.

Re-derive.

  1. In a cubic lattice the axes are orthogonal and of equal length, so the components of $[hkl]$ in the Cartesian frame are $(h,k,l)$ times the axis length.
  2. The sum of products of components is $hh'+kk'+ll'$; each magnitude is $(h^2+k^2+l^2)^{1/2}$ in the same units.
  3. Dividing gives Eq. (1.6).

Saved. Eq. (1.6).

1.8.3 Miller indices of planes

Steps: (i) find the intercepts in units of the axes; (ii) take reciprocals; (iii) clear fractions and write the result in round brackets, $(hkl)$. A parallel plane has the same indices. A plane through the origin is treated by shifting the origin. Families use braces: $\{100\}$ (six planes), $\{110\}$ (six), $\{111\}$ (eight).

Worked case (Fig. 1.15): intercepts $1$, $2$, $1$; reciprocals $1$, $\tfrac12$, $1$; cleared $2$, $1$, $2$; the plane is $(212)$.

1.9 Interplanar spacing

For planes $(hkl)$ with unit normal $\hat{\mathbf n}$, the spacing $d$ satisfies

$$\begin{aligned}d &= OP \\ &= \frac{a}{h}\cos\alpha \\ &= \frac{b}{k}\cos\beta \\ &= \frac{c}{l}\cos\gamma\end{aligned} \tag{1.7}$$
$$\begin{aligned}d &= \frac{\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf a}{h} \\ &= \frac{\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf b}{k} \\ &= \frac{\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf c}{l}\end{aligned} \tag{1.8}$$
Plus Two recall: normal and distance of a plane

Recall. For a plane $px+qy+rz=s$, the normal is $(p,q,r)$ and the perpendicular distance from the origin is $|s|/\sqrt{p^2+q^2+r^2}$.

Re-derive.

  1. Write the intercept plane as $hx/a+ky/b+lz/c=1$: $p=h/a$, $q=k/b$, $r=l/c$, $s=1$.
  2. Normal $\propto(h/a,k/b,l/c)$.
  3. $d=1/\sqrt{(h/a)^2+(k/b)^2+(l/c)^2}$.

Saved. Eq. (1.9). Only the plane equation is partly recalled.

$$d = \frac{1}{\left(\dfrac{h^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{k^2}{b^2}+\dfrac{l^2}{c^2}\right)^{1/2}} \tag{1.9}$$

Valid for orthogonal lattices. For a non-orthogonal lattice, find $\hat{\mathbf n}$ by another method and use Eq. (1.8).

$$d = \frac{a}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}} \tag{1.10}$$

For a cubic lattice; the direction $[hkl]$ is then normal to $(hkl)$.

Result: cubic spacing
$$d_{hkl}=\frac{a}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}}$$

Example: $(312)$ planes in an orthorhombic lattice with the intercepts of Example 1.3 (book).

Summary

  • Eq. (1.6): angle between directions. Eq. (1.7)–(1.8): spacing from the normal. Eq. (1.9): orthogonal lattices. Eq. (1.10): cubic lattices.

Plus Two recall: volume of a sphere

Recall. A sphere of radius $r$ has volume $\tfrac43\pi r^3$.

Re-derive.

  1. Packing fraction $f=\frac{N\cdot\tfrac43\pi r^3}{V_{\text{cell}}}$, with $N$ atoms per cell.
  2. For sc: $N=1$, $V_{\text{cell}}=a^3$, $a=2r$, so $f=\tfrac43\pi r^3/8r^3=\pi/6$.

1.10.1 Close-packed structures

Coordination number 12; six neighbours in the plane, three above and three below. Two stackings:

hcp ($\cdots ABABAB\cdots$). Conventional cell is hexagonal (Fig. 1.19). Effective atoms per cell: $12\times\tfrac16+2\times\tfrac12+3=6$. Ideal $c=1.633\,a$. Primitive lattice: simple hexagonal with a two-atom basis at $(0,0,0)$ and $(\tfrac23,\tfrac13,\tfrac12)$; primitive volume is one-third of the hexagonal cell. Packing fraction $f=\frac{2\cdot\frac43\pi r^3}{a^2c\sin60^\circ}=0.74$.

fcc ($\cdots ABCABC\cdots$). Conventional cell is a cube (Fig. 1.20), effective atoms $8\times\tfrac18+6\times\tfrac12=4$. Touching along the face diagonal, $4r=\sqrt2\,a$. $f=\frac{4\cdot\frac43\pi r^3}{a^3}=\frac{\pi}{3\sqrt2}\approx0.74$.

1.10.2 Loose-packed structures

bcc. Effective atoms $8\times\tfrac18+1=2$; coordination 8; $4r=\sqrt3\,a$; $f=\frac{2\cdot\frac43\pi r^3}{a^3}=\frac{\pi\sqrt3}{8}\approx0.68$. Examples: Na, K, Mo, W.

sc. Atoms at corners; $a=2r$; coordination 6; $f=\pi/6\approx0.52$. Polonium at room temperature.

1.11 Diamond cubic structure

Space lattice fcc; basis two carbon atoms, at $(0,0,0)$ and at one quarter of the body diagonal. The second atom occupies an alternate tetrahedral void. Packing efficiency 34 %; coordination 4; nearest-neighbour distance $\sqrt3\,a/4$. Equivalent description: two interpenetrating fcc sublattices with origins $(0,0,0)$ and $(\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14)$ (Fig. 1.24). Examples: Si, Ge, SiC, GaAs, grey tin.

1.12 Zinc blende (ZnS)

As diamond cubic, but the two fcc sublattices hold different elements (Zn and S).

1.13 Sodium chloride

Each Na+ has six Cl− octahedral neighbours. Unit cell fcc with Cl− on the fcc sites and Na+ in the octahedral voids. As a lattice and basis: fcc lattice, basis Na+ at $(0,0,0)$ and Cl− at $(a/2,0,0)$. Positions as listed in the book; four molecules per cell; $a=2(r_+ + r_-)$.

$$\text{Na}^+:\ (0,0,0);\ (\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0);\ (\tfrac12,0,\tfrac12);\ (0,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$$
$$\text{Cl}^-:\ (\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12);\ (0,0,\tfrac12);\ (0,\tfrac12,0);\ (\tfrac12,0,0)$$

Example 1.1 and Example 1.5 (book)

Example 1.1: $a=2r$ (sc), $a=4r/\sqrt3$ (bcc), $a=2\sqrt2\,r$ (fcc). Example 1.5: NaCl with $r_{\text{Na}}=0.98$ Å, $r_{\text{Cl}}=1.81$ Å gives $a=5.58$ Å, $f=0.663$ and density $2234$ kg m−3.

$$f=\frac{16\pi}{3}\cdot\frac{0.98^3+1.81^3}{5.58^3}=0.663$$
Result: packing fractions

hcp and fcc 0.74; bcc 0.68; sc 0.52; diamond 0.34.

2.1–2.2 X-ray diffraction: Bragg's law and the Laue equations (structured notes)

Source: book-sources/bs-2-xray-reciprocal.html, Puri and Babbar pp. 34–39. Equations 2.1 to 2.5 are cited by their book numbers. Section 2.3 is outside the module. Figures are redrawn.

Before you start

  • Constructive interference: path difference $n\lambda$ (box below).
  • Interplanar spacing $d_{hkl}$ and the normal to a plane (Section 1.9).
Plus Two recall: interference condition

Recall. Constructive interference occurs for path difference $n\lambda$.

Re-derive.

  1. Rays reflect from adjacent planes of spacing $d$ at angle $\theta$.
  2. Path difference $=$ (down) + (up) $=2d\sin\theta$.
  3. Set $2d\sin\theta=n\lambda$.

Saved. Eq. (2.1).

2.1 Introduction

X-rays are electromagnetic waves, but ordinary gratings cannot diffract them, because their wavelength is too short. Von Laue (1912) proposed a single crystal as a three-dimensional grating. Friedrich and Knipping confirmed it with zinc blende. The Laue pattern reflects the symmetry of the crystal. X-ray diffraction determines crystal structures and the wavelength of X-rays.

2.2 X-ray diffraction

Electrons in the crystal are set oscillating by the incident beam and become secondary sources at the same frequency. The secondary waves interfere, giving maxima and minima in certain directions. Figure 2.1 shows a one-dimensional row of atoms with spacing $a$, where reinforcement occurs in zeroth, first and second order directions only.

2.2.1 Bragg's treatment

Diffraction from atoms is converted into reflection from parallel planes of atoms. For a set of planes with spacing $d$ and glancing angle $\theta$, the path difference is $2d\sin\theta$, and

$$2d\sin\theta = n\lambda \tag{2.1}$$

$n$ is the order. $\sin\theta\le1$ requires $\lambda\le2d$.

2.2.2 Laue's treatment

Two identical scatterers A and B separated by $\mathbf r$ with incident and scattered unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf n}_1$, $\hat{\mathbf n}_2$ (angle $2\theta$ between them). Path difference $=\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf N$, $\mathbf N=\hat{\mathbf n}_1-\hat{\mathbf n}_2$, $|\mathbf N|=2\sin\theta$ (Figs. 2.3, 2.4).

$$\phi=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf N) \tag{2.2}$$

Requiring constructive interference along $\mathbf a$, $\mathbf b$, $\mathbf c$ together:

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf N)=2\pi nh' \\ \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf N)=2\pi nk' \\ \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf N)=2\pi nl'\end{gathered} \tag{2.3}$$

With $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf N=2a\sin\theta\cos\alpha$ and so on:

$$\begin{gathered}2a\sin\theta\cos\alpha=h'\lambda=nh\lambda \\ 2b\sin\theta\cos\beta=k'\lambda=nk\lambda \\ 2c\sin\theta\cos\gamma=l'\lambda=nl\lambda\end{gathered} \tag{2.4}$$

together with the direction-cosine identity (orthogonal axes)

$$\cos^2\alpha+\cos^2\beta+\cos^2\gamma=1 \tag{2.5}$$

The direction cosines of $\mathbf N$ are proportional to $h/a$, $k/b$, $l/c$, so $\mathbf N$ is the normal to $(hkl)$. Using Eq. (1.7) in Eq. (2.4) gives $2d\sin\theta=n\lambda$, i.e. Bragg's law (Eq. 2.1). Laue's equations contain Bragg's law.

Convention: $n$ is absorbed by writing $(nh\,nk\,nl)$ as the first-order reflection, so structure work uses $n=1$ with all planes (real or imaginary) considered.

Result: equivalence of the two treatments

The Laue conditions (2.4) with (2.5) imply $2d_{hkl}\sin\theta=n\lambda$ (Eq. 2.1).

Summary

  • Eq. (2.1): Bragg's law. Eq. (2.2): phase. Eq. (2.3): three simultaneous conditions. Eq. (2.4): Laue equations. Eq. (2.5): identity.
  • Diffraction requires $\lambda\le2d$.

2.4–2.6 Reciprocal lattice and Bragg's law in reciprocal space (structured notes)

Source: book-sources/bs-2-xray-reciprocal.html, Puri and Babbar pp. 46–54. Equations 2.10 to 2.30 are cited by their book numbers. Figures are redrawn.

Before you start

  • Normals to planes; interplanar spacing (Section 1.9).
  • Scalar and vector products; scalar triple product $V=\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)$.
Plus Two recall: dot product and perpendicularity

Recall. A vector is perpendicular to another when their dot product is zero; $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u=|\mathbf u|^2$.

Re-derive.

  1. $\mathbf b\times\mathbf c$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf b$ and to $\mathbf c$.
  2. Hence $\mathbf a^*=\mathbf b\times\mathbf c/V$ satisfies $\mathbf a^*\cdot\mathbf b=\mathbf a^*\cdot\mathbf c=0$ (Eq. 2.15).
  3. $\mathbf a^*\cdot\mathbf a=\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)/V=1$ (Eq. 2.16).

Saved. Eqs. (2.15) and (2.16).

2.4.1 Reciprocal lattice vectors

$$\mathbf a_{hkl}=\frac{1}{d_{hkl}}\,\hat{\mathbf n} \tag{2.10}$$

The reciprocal basis of the direct basis $\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c$ follows from Eqs. (2.11) and (2.12), $\mathbf a_{100}=(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)/V=\hat{\mathbf n}/d_{100}$, applied to each face:

$$\begin{gathered}\mathbf a^*=\frac{\mathbf b\times\mathbf c}{V} \\ \mathbf b^*=\frac{\mathbf c\times\mathbf a}{V} \\ \mathbf c^*=\frac{\mathbf a\times\mathbf b}{V}\end{gathered} \tag{2.13}$$

Each reciprocal vector is normal to two direct vectors (Eq. 2.14), with the orthogonality relations (2.15) and normalisation (2.16). The book notes that $\mathbf a^*$ and $\mathbf a$ need not be parallel (monoclinic case; Fig. 2.9): then $|\mathbf a^*|=1/(a\cos\theta)$.

In the $2\pi$ convention (Eq. 2.17, $\mathbf a^*\cdot\mathbf a=\mathbf b^*\cdot\mathbf b=\mathbf c^*\cdot\mathbf c=2\pi$):

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf a^* &= 2\pi\frac{\mathbf b\times\mathbf c}{\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)},\quad \mathbf b^* \\ &= 2\pi\frac{\mathbf c\times\mathbf a}{\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)},\quad \mathbf c^* \\ &= 2\pi\frac{\mathbf a\times\mathbf b}{\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)}\end{aligned} \tag{2.18}$$

Dimensions: direct $[\text{length}]$, reciprocal $[\text{length}]^{-1}$. Cell volumes are inversely related. Real space versus $k$-space.

2.4.2–2.4.4 Reciprocal of the cubic lattices

Simple cubic: $\mathbf a^*=\tfrac{2\pi}{a}\hat{\mathbf i}$ etc. (Eq. 2.21), so the reciprocal of sc is sc with edge $2\pi/a$.

$$\begin{gathered}\mathbf a^*=\frac{2\pi}{a}\hat{\mathbf i} \\ \mathbf b^*=\frac{2\pi}{a}\hat{\mathbf j} \\ \mathbf c^*=\frac{2\pi}{a}\hat{\mathbf k}\end{gathered} \tag{2.21}$$

bcc, primitive vectors (Eq. 2.22), $V=a^3/2$; reciprocal (Eq. 2.23) is fcc with primitive vectors $\tfrac{2\pi}{a}(\hat{\mathbf j}+\hat{\mathbf k})$ and cyclic permutations.

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf a &= \frac a2(\hat{\mathbf i}+\hat{\mathbf j}-\hat{\mathbf k}),\quad \mathbf b \\ &= \frac a2(-\hat{\mathbf i}+\hat{\mathbf j}+\hat{\mathbf k}),\quad \mathbf c \\ &= \frac a2(\hat{\mathbf i}-\hat{\mathbf j}+\hat{\mathbf k})\end{aligned} \tag{2.22}$$
$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf a^* &= \frac{2\pi}{a}(\hat{\mathbf j}+\hat{\mathbf k}),\quad \mathbf b^* \\ &= \frac{2\pi}{a}(\hat{\mathbf i}+\hat{\mathbf k}),\quad \mathbf c^* \\ &= \frac{2\pi}{a}(\hat{\mathbf i}+\hat{\mathbf j})\end{aligned} \tag{2.23}$$

fcc, primitive vectors (Eq. 2.24), $V=a^3/4$; reciprocal (Eq. 2.25) is bcc with edge $4\pi/a$.

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf a' &= \frac a2(\hat{\mathbf i}+\hat{\mathbf j}),\quad \mathbf b' \\ &= \frac a2(\hat{\mathbf j}+\hat{\mathbf k}),\quad \mathbf c' \\ &= \frac a2(\hat{\mathbf k}+\hat{\mathbf i})\end{aligned} \tag{2.24}$$
$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf a^* &= \frac{2\pi}{a}(\hat{\mathbf i}+\hat{\mathbf j}-\hat{\mathbf k}),\quad \mathbf b^* \\ &= \frac{2\pi}{a}(-\hat{\mathbf i}+\hat{\mathbf j}+\hat{\mathbf k}),\quad \mathbf c^* \\ &= \frac{2\pi}{a}(\hat{\mathbf i}-\hat{\mathbf j}+\hat{\mathbf k})\end{aligned} \tag{2.25}$$

2.4.3 Lattice points and the reciprocal lattice

Direct points $\mathbf T=m\mathbf a+n\mathbf b+p\mathbf c$ (Eq. 2.19). Reciprocal points $\mathbf G=h\mathbf a^*+k\mathbf b^*+l\mathbf c^*$ (Eq. 2.20). Since $\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf T=2\pi(hm+kn+lp)$, $\exp(i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf T)=1$. Each reciprocal point $(hkl)$ corresponds to the planes $(hkl)$.

$$\mathbf T = m\mathbf a + n\mathbf b + p\mathbf c \tag{2.19}$$
$$\mathbf G = h\mathbf a^* + k\mathbf b^* + l\mathbf c^* \tag{2.20}$$

2.5 Properties

  1. Each reciprocal point is one set of parallel direct planes.
  2. Its distance from the origin is inversely proportional to that spacing.
  3. Reciprocal cell volume is inversely proportional to the direct cell volume.
  4. The Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice is the Brillouin zone.
  5. The direct lattice is the reciprocal of its reciprocal; sc is self-reciprocal; bcc and fcc are reciprocal to each other.

2.6 Bragg's law in reciprocal space

Ewald construction: AO of length $1/\lambda$ to origin $O$; sphere of radius $AO$ about $A$.

$$|\mathbf{OB}|=\frac{n}{d_{hkl}} \tag{2.26}$$
$$\mathbf{OB}=\frac{2\sin\theta}{\lambda} \tag{2.27}$$

Equating: $2d_{hkl}\sin\theta=n\lambda$. Magnifying by $2\pi$ gives $\mathbf k'=\mathbf k+\mathbf G$, $k'^2=k^2$, and

$$2\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf G+G^2=0 \tag{2.28}$$
$$\begin{gathered}\mathbf k'=\mathbf k+\mathbf G \\ k'^2=k^2 \\ \mathbf k'-\mathbf k=\Delta\mathbf k=\mathbf G\end{gathered} \tag{2.29}$$

Eq. (2.30) states the last of these: the change in $\mathbf k$ is the reciprocal vector $\mathbf G$.

Result

Eq. (2.28) is the vector form of Bragg's law and is used for the Brillouin zones.

Summary

  • Eqs. (2.13), (2.18): reciprocal basis. Eqs. (2.21), (2.23), (2.25): cubic reciprocals.
  • Eq. (2.28): Bragg's law in vector form.

2.7 Definition

The Brillouin zone is the locus of wave vectors $\mathbf k$ that are Bragg reflected by the reciprocal lattice (Ewald construction).

2.7.1 Square lattice

With $\mathbf a^*=(2\pi/a)\hat{\mathbf i}$, $\mathbf b^*=(2\pi/a)\hat{\mathbf j}$, $\mathbf G=(2\pi/a)(h\hat{\mathbf i}+k\hat{\mathbf j})$ and $\mathbf k=k_x\hat{\mathbf i}+k_y\hat{\mathbf j}$, Eq. (2.28) gives

$$hk_x+kk_y=-\frac{\pi}{a}(h^2+k^2) \tag{2.31}$$

$(h,k)=(\pm1,0)$ gives $k_x=\pm\pi/a$; $(0,\pm1)$ gives $k_y=\pm\pi/a$. These bound the first zone, a square of side $2\pi/a$ (Fig. 2.14). $(\pm1,\pm1)$ gives $\pm k_x\pm k_y=2\pi/a$, bounding the second zone.

Zone boundaries are reflecting planes of order given by the zone number. A $\mathbf k$ off the boundaries is not reflected.

2.7.2 Cubic lattices in three dimensions

$$hk_x+kk_y+lk_z=-\frac{\pi}{a}(h^2+k^2+l^2) \tag{2.32}$$

First zone: cube of side $2\pi/a$. Method of normal bisectors: first zone bounded by bisectors of the shortest $\mathbf G$; second zone by the next shortest, and so on.

2.7.3 bcc zone

$\mathbf G=\frac{2\pi}{a}[(h+l)\hat{\mathbf i}+(h+k)\hat{\mathbf j}+(k+l)\hat{\mathbf k}]$ (Eq. 2.33). Shortest $\mathbf G$: twelve vectors $(2\pi/a)(\pm\hat{\mathbf i}\pm\hat{\mathbf j})$ and cyclic. Zone: rhombic dodecahedron (Fig. 2.15).

$$\mathbf G=\frac{2\pi}{a}\big[(h+l)\hat{\mathbf i}+(h+k)\hat{\mathbf j}+(k+l)\hat{\mathbf k}\big] \tag{2.33}$$

2.7.4 fcc zone

$\mathbf G=\frac{2\pi}{a}[(h-k+l)\hat{\mathbf i}+(h+k-l)\hat{\mathbf j}+(-h+k+l)\hat{\mathbf k}]$ (Eq. 2.34). Eight shortest $\mathbf G$: $(2\pi/a)(\pm\hat{\mathbf i}\pm\hat{\mathbf j}\pm\hat{\mathbf k})$. Corners of the octahedron truncated by $(2\pi/a)(\pm2\hat{\mathbf i})$ and cyclic. Zone: truncated octahedron (Fig. 2.16), also a primitive cell of bcc.

$$\mathbf G=\frac{2\pi}{a}\big[(h-k+l)\hat{\mathbf i}+(h+k-l)\hat{\mathbf j}+(-h+k+l)\hat{\mathbf k}\big] \tag{2.34}$$
Result

sc: cube, $2\pi/a$. bcc: rhombic dodecahedron. fcc: truncated octahedron.

Summary

  • Eqs. (2.31)–(2.34): zone conditions for square, cubic, bcc and fcc.
