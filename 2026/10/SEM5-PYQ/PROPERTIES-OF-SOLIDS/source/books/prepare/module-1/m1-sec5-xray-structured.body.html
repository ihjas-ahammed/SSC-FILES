2.1 Introduction

X-rays are electromagnetic waves, but ordinary gratings cannot diffract them, because their wavelength is too short. Von Laue (1912) proposed a single crystal as a three-dimensional grating. Friedrich and Knipping confirmed it with zinc blende. The Laue pattern reflects the symmetry of the crystal. X-ray diffraction determines crystal structures and the wavelength of X-rays.

2.2 X-ray diffraction

Electrons in the crystal are set oscillating by the incident beam and become secondary sources at the same frequency. The secondary waves interfere, giving maxima and minima in certain directions. Figure 2.1 shows a one-dimensional row of atoms with spacing $a$, where reinforcement occurs in zeroth, first and second order directions only.

{{FIG:m1-s5-bragg}}

2.2.1 Bragg's treatment

Diffraction from atoms is converted into reflection from parallel planes of atoms. For a set of planes with spacing $d$ and glancing angle $\theta$, the path difference is $2d\sin\theta$, and

$$2d\sin\theta = n\lambda \tag{2.1}$$

$n$ is the order. $\sin\theta\le1$ requires $\lambda\le2d$.

2.2.2 Laue's treatment

Two identical scatterers A and B separated by $\mathbf r$ with incident and scattered unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf n}_1$, $\hat{\mathbf n}_2$ (angle $2\theta$ between them). Path difference $=\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf N$, $\mathbf N=\hat{\mathbf n}_1-\hat{\mathbf n}_2$, $|\mathbf N|=2\sin\theta$ (Figs. 2.3, 2.4).

{{FIG:m1-s5-laue}}
$$\phi=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf N) \tag{2.2}$$

Requiring constructive interference along $\mathbf a$, $\mathbf b$, $\mathbf c$ together:

$$\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf N)=2\pi nh',\qquad \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf N)=2\pi nk',\qquad \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf N)=2\pi nl' \tag{2.3}$$

With $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf N=2a\sin\theta\cos\alpha$ and so on:

$$2a\sin\theta\cos\alpha=h'\lambda=nh\lambda,\qquad 2b\sin\theta\cos\beta=k'\lambda=nk\lambda,\qquad 2c\sin\theta\cos\gamma=l'\lambda=nl\lambda \tag{2.4}$$

together with the direction-cosine identity (orthogonal axes)

$$\cos^2\alpha+\cos^2\beta+\cos^2\gamma=1 \tag{2.5}$$

The direction cosines of $\mathbf N$ are proportional to $h/a$, $k/b$, $l/c$, so $\mathbf N$ is the normal to $(hkl)$. Using Eq. (1.7) in Eq. (2.4) gives $2d\sin\theta=n\lambda$, i.e. Bragg's law (Eq. 2.1). Laue's equations contain Bragg's law.

Convention: $n$ is absorbed by writing $(nh\,nk\,nl)$ as the first-order reflection, so structure work uses $n=1$ with all planes (real or imaginary) considered.

Result: equivalence of the two treatments

The Laue conditions (2.4) with (2.5) imply $2d_{hkl}\sin\theta=n\lambda$ (Eq. 2.1).

Summary

  • Eq. (2.1): Bragg's law. Eq. (2.2): phase. Eq. (2.3): three simultaneous conditions. Eq. (2.4): Laue equations. Eq. (2.5): identity.
  • Diffraction requires $\lambda\le2d$.
