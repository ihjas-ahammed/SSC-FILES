1. Even and odd permutations

Every permutation of a finite set with at least two elements is a product of transpositions (Theorem 8.15), but the product is not unique: $(1, 2)(1, 2)$ is the identity. The number of transpositions can be changed, but only by an even number. The next theorem says the parity cannot be changed.

Theorem 8.19 No permutation in $S_n$ can be expressed both as a product of an even number of transpositions and as a product of an odd number of transpositions.

Proof in outline. Fix $\sigma \in S_n$ and a transposition $\tau = (i, j)$. The claim is that the number of orbits of $\sigma$ and of $\tau\sigma$ differ by exactly $1$.

Case I: $i$ and $j$ lie in different orbits of $\sigma$. Write $\sigma$ as disjoint cycles, one containing $j$ and one containing $i$. Figure 8.20 shows these two orbits as two circles, labelled with $j$, $a$, $b$ and $i$. Symbolically the two cycles are $(b, j, \times, \times, \times)(a, i, \times, \times)$, where $\times$ stands for other elements of the orbits. Multiplying by $(i, j)$ on the left joins the two orbits into one. The product of the first three cycles is

$$(i, j)(b, j, \times, \times, \times)(a, i, \times, \times) = (a, j, \times, \times, \times, b, i, \times, \times).$$

So two orbits become one.

Exercise 42 asks you to repeat this computation for the case where one or both of $i$ and $j$ is the only element of its orbit. It is printed directly below.

Exercise 42. a. Draw a figure like Fig. 8.20 to illustrate that if $i$ and $j$ are in different orbits of $\sigma$ and $\sigma(i) = i$, then the number of orbits of $(i, j)\sigma$ is one less than the number of orbits of $\sigma$.
b. Repeat part (a) if $\sigma(j) = j$ also.

Case II: $i$ and $j$ lie in the same orbit of $\sigma$. Write $\sigma$ as disjoint cycles with the first of the form $(a, i, \times, \times, \times, b, j, \times, \times)$. Multiplying by $(i, j)$ splits this orbit into two, as Figure 8.21 shows: the product of the first two cycles is

$$(i, j)(a, i, \times, \times, \times, b, j, \times, \times) = (a, j, \times, \times, \times)(b, i, \times, \times, \times).$$

So one orbit becomes two.

In both cases the number of orbits changes by $1$. The identity $\iota$ has $n$ orbits, since each element is alone in its orbit. A permutation $\sigma$ then has a number of orbits that differs from $n$ by an even number, or by an odd number, but not both. If $\sigma = \tau_1\tau_2\cdots\tau_m\iota$ with $m$ even and also with $m$ odd, this would be impossible. ◆

Definition 8.22 A permutation of a finite set is even or odd according as it can be written as a product of an even number of transpositions, or as a product of an odd number of transpositions.

Example 8.23. The identity $\iota$ in $S_n$ is even, since $\iota = (1, 2)(1, 2)$. For $n = 1$ the identity cannot be written as such a product, and we define it to be even. The permutation $(1, 4, 5, 6)(2, 1, 5)$ in $S_6$ can be written

$$(1, 4, 5, 6)(2, 1, 5) = (1, 6)(1, 5)(1, 4)(2, 5)(2, 1),$$

which has five transpositions, so it is odd.

2. The alternating groups

For $n \ge 2$ the even and odd permutations of $S_n$ are equally many. Let $A_n$ be the set of even permutations and $B_n$ the set of odd ones. Fix a transposition $\tau = (1, 2)$. Define $\lambda_\tau : A_n \to B_n$ by $\lambda_\tau(\sigma) = \tau\sigma$. This lands in $B_n$, since $\tau\sigma$ is a product of one more transposition than $\sigma$, so it is odd. It is one-to-one, because $\tau\sigma = \tau\mu$ gives $\sigma = \mu$. It is onto $B_n$, because for $\rho \in B_n$ the element $\tau^{-1}\rho$ is even and maps to $\rho$. So $A_n$ and $B_n$ have the same number of elements, $n!/2$ each.

The product of two even permutations is even, the identity is even, and the inverse of an even permutation is even: if $\sigma$ is a product of transpositions in one order, $\sigma^{-1}$ is the same transpositions in the opposite order. So the even permutations form a subgroup.

Theorem 8.24 If $n \ge 2$, the even permutations of $\{1, 2, \dots, n\}$ form a subgroup of $S_n$ of order $n!/2$.

The sign of a permutation is the map $\operatorname{sgn} : S_n \to \{1, -1\}$ with $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) = 1$ if $\sigma$ is even and $-1$ if $\sigma$ is odd. The set $\{1, -1\}$ is a group under multiplication, and $\operatorname{sgn}$ is a homomorphism. Its kernel $\operatorname{Ker}(\operatorname{sgn})$ is the set of even permutations, which is the subgroup of Theorem 8.24. The sign map is the standard way of defining the determinant of a square matrix.

Exercise 52 asks you to prove some standard facts about determinants from this definition. It is printed directly below.

Exercise 52. The usual definition for the determinant of an $n \times n$ matrix $A = (a_{ij})$ is

$$\det(A) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\, a_{1,\sigma(1)}a_{2,\sigma(2)}a_{3,\sigma(3)}\cdots a_{n,\sigma(n)},$$

where $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)$ is the sign of $\sigma$. Using this definition, prove the following properties of determinants.
a. If a row of matrix $A$ has all zero entries, then $\det(A) = 0$.
b. If two different rows of $A$ are switched to obtain $B$, then $\det(B) = -\det(A)$.
c. If $r$ times one row of $A$ is added to another row of $A$ to obtain a matrix $B$, then $\det(A) = \det(B)$.
d. If a row of $A$ is multiplied by $r$ to obtain the matrix $B$, then $\det(B) = r\det(A)$.

Definition 8.25 The subgroup of $S_n$ consisting of the even permutations of $n$ letters is the alternating group $A_n$ on $n$ letters.

The groups $S_n$ and $A_n$ are important. Cayley’s theorem shows that every finite group of order $n$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $S_n$. There are no formulas using only radicals for solving polynomial equations of degree $n \ge 5$, and this fact is due to the structure of $A_n$.
