25PYQ N24 Q17

Width of principal maxima, N slits.

Know firstT7T6M2

The N-slit intensity is $I = I_0\frac{\sin^2\beta}{\beta^2}\frac{\sin^2N\gamma}{\sin^2\gamma}$, $\gamma = \frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}$.

m-th principal maximum: $\gamma = m\pi$, i.e. $d\sin\theta_m = m\lambda$
$\sin N\gamma = 0$ there, and the nearest zeros on either side are at $N\gamma = Nm\pi \pm \pi$, i.e. $d\sin(\theta_m \pm \Delta\theta_m) = m\lambda \pm \dfrac{\lambda}{N}$
Expand: $\sin(\theta_m \pm \Delta\theta_m) \approx \sin\theta_m \pm \Delta\theta_m\cos\theta_m$ (Δθ small)
Subtract: $d\cos\theta_m\,\Delta\theta_m = \dfrac{\lambda}{N}$
$\Delta\theta_m = \dfrac{\lambda}{Nd\cos\theta_m}$ (half-width); the maxima get sharper as N increases

Marks: intensity expression 1 · condition for zeros 2 · approximation and result 2.

29PYQ N19 Q19

Resolving power of a microscope; oil immersion.

Know firstT4T9

The resolving power of a microscope is its ability to show two close points of an object as separate. Because of diffraction at the objective, each point gives an Airy disc; by Rayleigh's criterion two points are just resolved when the centre of one disc falls on the first dark ring of the other. This gives the smallest separable distance

$$d_{min} = \frac{0.61\lambda}{n\sin\alpha}$$

where α is the semi-angle of the cone of light entering the objective and $n$ the refractive index of the medium between object and objective; $n\sin\alpha$ is the numerical aperture (NA). Resolving power ∝ $1/d_{min}$ = $\frac{n\sin\alpha}{0.61\lambda}$.

Oil immersion: filling the gap with cedar-wood oil ($n \approx 1.5$, nearly equal to glass) increases NA by about 1.5 times, so $d_{min}$ decreases and finer detail is resolved. It also reduces light loss by reflection at the cover slip. (Using shorter wavelength, e.g. UV, also improves resolution.)

31PYQ N20 Q21

Fraunhofer diffraction at a circular aperture.

Know firstT4T2

A plane wave falls normally on a circular aperture of radius $a$ and the pattern is observed in the focal plane of a lens. By symmetry the pattern consists of circular rings. Adding the contributions of all elements of the aperture gives

$$I = I_0\left[\frac{2J_1(v)}{v}\right]^2,\qquad v = \frac{2\pi a}{\lambda}\sin\theta$$

where $J_1$ is the Bessel function of order one ($2J_1(v)/v \to 1$ as $v\to0$).

{{fig:airy}}
  • Central bright disc (Airy disc), containing about 84% of the light.
  • Dark rings where $J_1(v) = 0$: $v = 3.832, 7.016, 10.17, \ldots$; the first at $\sin\theta = \frac{0.61\lambda}{a} = \frac{1.22\lambda}{D}$.
  • Weak bright rings between them, of rapidly decreasing intensity.
  • Radius of the first dark ring in the focal plane ≈ $\frac{1.22\lambda f}{D}$: smaller aperture → larger disc.

This sets the resolving limit of telescopes and microscopes. (N22 Q22 asks the same.)

32PYQ N19 Q20

Construction of half-period zones; area.

Know firstT10
{{fig:half_period_zones}}
Let a plane wavefront WW′ travel towards P, at perpendicular distance $OP = b$. With P as centre, draw spheres of radii $b+\frac\lambda2, b+\frac{2\lambda}{2}, b+\frac{3\lambda}{2}, \ldots$, cutting the wavefront in circles of radii $r_1, r_2, r_3, \ldots$
The annular regions between successive circles are the half-period zones: light from corresponding points of adjacent zones reaches P with path difference λ/2 (half a period apart)
$r_n^2 = \left(b + \frac{n\lambda}{2}\right)^2 - b^2 = n\lambda b + \frac{n^2\lambda^2}{4} \approx n\lambda b$, so $r_n = \sqrt{n\lambda b}$
Area of n-th zone $= \pi(r_n^2 - r_{n-1}^2) = \pi\lambda b$
Area of each zone ≈ πλb: the same for all zones; radii ∝ √n

Since the areas are equal, the amplitudes $u_n$ depend only on the slowly increasing distance and obliquity, so they decrease slowly, and the resultant is $u_1/2$. (N24 Q19 asks the same with figure.)

34PYQ N22 Q21

Principle and construction of a zone plate.

Know firstT12T10
{{fig:zone_plate}}

Construction: concentric circles with radii proportional to the square roots of the natural numbers ($r_n = r_1\sqrt n$) are drawn on white paper, and alternate annular zones are blackened. A reduced photograph on a glass plate gives the zone plate.

Principle: for a point P on the axis at distance $b = r_1^2/\lambda$, the rings are exactly the half-period zones. Without the plate, $u = u_1 - u_2 + u_3 - \cdots \approx u_1/2$. With the even zones blocked, $u = u_1 + u_3 + u_5 + \cdots$: all the transmitted light arrives in phase, so P is extremely bright. The plate focuses light like a convex lens of focal length $f = r_1^2/\lambda$.

At $b = f/3$ each transparent ring contains three zones (net one), so there is another, weaker focus; similarly at $f/5, f/7, \ldots$.

36

Lens formula for a zone plate.

Know firstT12
Point source S on the axis at distance $a$, image point P at distance $b$ on the other side; L a point on the edge of the first zone, at height $r_1$
First-zone condition: $SL + LP - SP = \dfrac\lambda2$
$SL = \sqrt{a^2+r_1^2} \approx a + \dfrac{r_1^2}{2a}$, $LP \approx b + \dfrac{r_1^2}{2b}$, $SP = a + b$Binomial approximation, $r_1 \ll a, b$.
$\dfrac{r_1^2}{2}\left(\dfrac1a + \dfrac1b\right) = \dfrac\lambda2$
$\dfrac1a + \dfrac1b = \dfrac{\lambda}{r_1^2} \equiv \dfrac1f$
$\dfrac1a+\dfrac1b=\dfrac1f$ with $f = \dfrac{r_1^2}{\lambda} = \dfrac{r_n^2}{n\lambda}$

Since $f \propto 1/\lambda$, the zone plate has large chromatic aberration (red focused nearer than violet).
