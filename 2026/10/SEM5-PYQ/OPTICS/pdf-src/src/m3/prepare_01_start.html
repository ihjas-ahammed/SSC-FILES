One rule for the whole module

Add arrows, then squareEvery wave is an arrow (length = amplitude, angle = phase). To find the light at a point, add all the arrows arriving there head to tail. The brightness is the length of the total arrow, squared. Every formula in this module is this rule applied carefully.

The whole module in one story

The idea

1. Light bends round edges and spreads out of openings, because it is a wave (T1).

2. A slit = many tiny sources. Adding their arrows gives the single-slit pattern; a round hole gives rings (T2–T4).

3. Two slits, then N slits: sharp bright lines. Many slits = a grating, which separates colours and can tell close wavelengths apart (T5–T9).

4. With a nearby source and screen (Fresnel), cut the wavefront into rings (zones). This explains why light seems to travel straight, a bright spot behind a disc, and the zone plate "lens" (T10–T12).

5. Behind a straight edge, the shadow is not sharp: the Cornu spiral gives the fringes (T13).

P1

Phase, path and intensity

The three wave ideas used on every page.

Remind me · Plus One Ch 14
  • Wave: $y = a\cos(kx - \omega t)$, $k = 2\pi/\lambda$. Light: λ ≈ 5000 Å $= 5\times10^{-5}$ cm.
  • Path difference Δ ⟹ phase difference $\delta = \dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta$. Δ = mλ: in step (bright). Δ = (m + ½)λ: opposite (dark).
  • Intensity ∝ amplitude²: $I \propto a^2$.

P2

Huygens' principle and wavefronts

Every point of a wave acts like a new little source.

Remind me · Plus Two Ch 10
  • A wavefront is a surface on which all points vibrate in step (same phase). Far from a point source it is nearly flat: a plane wave.
  • Huygens: every point on a wavefront sends out small secondary wavelets; the new wavefront is the surface touching them all.
  • A lens brings a parallel beam to a point in its focal plane; a beam at angle θ to the axis focuses at height $f\tan\theta \approx f\theta$.
The ideaFresnel added one thing to Huygens: the wavelets interfere with each other. So to find the light at any point P, add up the wavelets from every point of the wavefront, with their phases. That is diffraction theory in one sentence.
P3

Young's double slit (as in Plus Two)

Needed for the two-slit and N-slit patterns.

Remind me · Plus Two Ch 10
  • Two slits $d$ apart. At angle θ, path difference $d\sin\theta$.
  • Bright: $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$. Dark: $d\sin\theta = (m+\frac12)\lambda$.
  • Fringe width on a screen at distance D: $\beta = \lambda D/d$. Intensity $I = 4I_0\cos^2(\delta/2)$.

P4

Diffraction in Plus Two

What NCERT already told you. This module proves it and goes further.

Remind me · Plus Two §10.6
  • A single slit of width $a$ gives a broad central bright band with dark bands at $a\sin\theta = n\lambda$ ($n = \pm1, \pm2, \ldots$), and weaker bands beyond.
  • The narrower the slit, the wider the pattern.
  • Resolving power: two objects are just resolved when the centre of one diffraction pattern falls on the first dark ring of the other. For a circular lens of diameter D: smallest angle ≈ $1.22\lambda/D$.
Exam trapIn this module Ghatak calls the slit width $b$ (not $a$) and the slit spacing $d$. For a single slit, $b\sin\theta = m\lambda$ gives dark places; for slits, $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ gives bright places. Same look, opposite meaning.
