One rule for the whole module

Count the extra path, then compare with λEvery question in this module is the same three steps. (1) Two copies of the same light reach a point by two routes. (2) Find how much longer one route is (the path difference Δ), adding half a wave for any "bad" reflection. (3) If Δ is a whole number of wavelengths the copies add (bright); if it is a whole number plus a half they cancel (dark).

The whole module in one story

The idea

1. Two waves at one place simply add (superposition). In step: bright. Opposite: dark (T1).

2. Two ordinary lamps never show this, because their phases jump at random. You must split one source into two copies (coherent sources) (T2).

3. Splitting the wavefront (side by side): Young's slits, Fresnel's two mirrors, the biprism, Lloyd's mirror. Bright and dark bands of width λD/d (T3–T10).

4. A reflection from a denser medium adds half a wave (T11).

5. Splitting the amplitude (part reflected, part passed): thin films, soap bubbles, coatings on lenses, wedges, Newton's rings, Michelson's interferometer. The extra path is 2nd cos r (T12–T21).

P1

Waves: phase and path

Plus One Ch 14. The language of the whole module.

{{fig:wave_terms}}
Remind me · Plus One Ch 14
  • A wave: $y = a\cos(kx - \omega t)$ with $k = \dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}$, $\omega = 2\pi\nu$. $a$ is the amplitude, the bracket is the phase.
  • Light has λ ≈ 4000 Å (violet) to 7000 Å (red). 1 Å $= 10^{-10}$ m $= 10^{-8}$ cm.
{{fig:phase_circle}}
Result · path → phase $$\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,\Delta$$
Δ = 0, λ, 2λ, …δ = 0, 2π, 4π: in step Δ = λ/2, 3λ/2, …δ = π, 3π: opposite
Exam trapDo not mix up path difference (a length, in cm or Å) with phase difference (an angle, in radians). Always convert with $2\pi/\lambda$.
Check yourself
Path difference λ/4. Phase difference?
$\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{\lambda}{4} = \frac{\pi}{2}$, i.e. 90°.
P2

Intensity = amplitude squared, averaged

Plus Two Ch 10. What the eye actually sees.

Remind me · Plus Two §10.4
  • The brightness (intensity) of light is proportional to the square of the amplitude: $I \propto a^2$. Double the amplitude: four times as bright.
  • Light vibrates about $10^{15}$ times a second. The eye (and any detector) responds in about 0.1 s, so it sees only the average over millions of vibrations.
Maths tool · averages over time

Averaged over a full cycle (or many cycles):

$\langle\cos\omega t\rangle$$= 0$ (as much positive as negative) $\langle\cos^2\omega t\rangle$$= \frac12$ (because $\cos^2x = \frac{1+\cos2x}{2}$ and $\langle\cos 2x\rangle = 0$)

A quantity that changes at random (not a cycle) also averages to zero: $\langle\cos\phi_{\text{random}}\rangle = 0$.

Check yourself
Two waves give amplitude 3 units and 1 unit at two places. Ratio of intensities?
$3^2 : 1^2 = 9 : 1$.
P3

Young's experiment (as in Plus Two)

Plus Two §10.5. This module starts here and goes deeper.

{{fig:ydse}}
Remind me · Plus Two §10.5
  • One narrow slit S lights two slits S₁, S₂ a distance $d$ apart. A screen is at distance $D$ ($D \gg d$).
  • Bright fringe where $\Delta = n\lambda$, dark where $\Delta = (n+\frac12)\lambda$.
  • Bright fringes at $y_n = \dfrac{n\lambda D}{d}$; fringe width $\beta = \dfrac{\lambda D}{d}$.
  • $I = 4I_0\cos^2(\phi/2)$; two independent sources give just $2I_0$ everywhere.
What is new in this moduleWhy it works (coherence), the proof of $I = 4I_0\cos^2(\delta/2)$ for unequal waves too, and the other ways of making two coherent sources.
P4

Refraction and optical path

Class 10, Plus Two Ch 9. Needed for every thin-film question.

Remind me · Class 10 and Plus Two
  • Refractive index $n = \dfrac{c}{v}$: light is $n$ times slower in the medium.
  • Snell's law (Class 10 form): $n = \dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r}$ for light from air into the medium. General form: $n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2$.
  • The frequency stays the same, so the wavelength inside is shorter: $\lambda_{\text{medium}} = \dfrac{\lambda}{n}$.
The idea · optical pathA length $t$ inside glass contains $n$ times as many waves as the same length of air. So for counting waves, glass of thickness $t$ "counts as" air of length $nt$. This $nt$ is the optical path. Path differences in this module are always optical paths.
Result
Optical path$n \times$ (geometrical length) Plate in one beamadds $nt - t = (n-1)t$ Down and back in a film$2nt$ (normal incidence)
Check yourself
Glass plate, $n = 1.5$, $t = 0.01$ mm, put in one beam. Extra path?
$(1.5 - 1)\times0.01 = 0.005$ mm $= 5000$ nm, about 10 wavelengths of green light.
P5

Mirrors, prisms and lenses

Plus Two Ch 9. Each method of making two sources uses one of these.

Remind me · Plus Two Ch 9
  • Plane mirror: the image is as far behind the mirror as the object is in front. Turning a mirror by an angle θ turns the reflected ray by 2θ.
  • Thin prism (small angle A): every ray is bent by the same small angle $\delta = (n-1)A$.
  • Lens (Cartesian sign convention, as in Module 1): $\dfrac1v - \dfrac1u = \dfrac1f$; magnification $= v/u$.
  • Reflection from a denser medium (for example air → glass) turns the wave upside down: a phase change of π. (Proved in T11.)
Check yourself
A thin prism of angle 1° and $n = 1.5$. Deviation?
$(1.5-1)\times1° = 0.5°$.
P6

Geometry of a circle

Class 10 Pythagoras, used for Newton's rings.

{{fig:newton_geom}}
A circle of radius R touches a flat line. At distance $r$ sideways, the gap $t$ between circle and line satisfies $R^2 = r^2 + (R-t)^2$Pythagoras in the right triangle: hypotenuse R, sides r and R − t.
Expand: $R^2 = r^2 + R^2 - 2Rt + t^2 \;\Rightarrow\; r^2 = 2Rt - t^2$
$t$ is tiny compared with $2R$ (micrometres against metres), so drop $t^2$: $\;r^2 \approx 2Rt$
Check yourself
R = 100 cm, r = 0.1 cm. Gap t?
$t = r^2/2R = 0.01/200 = 5\times10^{-5}$ cm, about one wavelength.
