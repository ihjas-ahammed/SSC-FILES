UNIT 5 · GHATAK §13.5

Adding two waves

T1

Superposition of two sinusoidal waves

The principle, and the conditions for bright and dark (PYQ N19 Q15, N22 Q19, N24 Q4, N25 Q2).

{{fig:superpose}}
Result · principle of superpositionWhen two (or more) waves pass through the same point at the same time, the resultant displacement is the sum of the separate displacements: $y = y_1 + y_2$. Each wave carries on afterwards as if the other had never been there.
The idea · interferenceBecause displacements add with their signs, two waves can make more than either (crest on crest) or nothing (crest on trough). This rearrangement of light into bright and dark regions is interference.

Two waves of the same frequency

$x_1 = a_1\cos(\omega t + \theta_1)$ and $x_2 = a_2\cos(\omega t + \theta_2)$. Find the resultant $x = x_1 + x_2$.

Open each bracket: $x = (a_1\cos\theta_1 + a_2\cos\theta_2)\cos\omega t - (a_1\sin\theta_1 + a_2\sin\theta_2)\sin\omega t$Identity (a) of M1 on each wave.
Try $x = a\cos(\omega t + \theta) = a\cos\theta\cos\omega t - a\sin\theta\sin\omega t$We guess the answer is again one wave, with amplitude $a$ and phase θ.
Match the $\cos\omega t$ and $\sin\omega t$ parts: $a\cos\theta = a_1\cos\theta_1 + a_2\cos\theta_2$, $\;a\sin\theta = a_1\sin\theta_1 + a_2\sin\theta_2$True at every time t only if both coefficients agree (Ghatak 13.23–13.24).
Square and add: $a^2 = a_1^2 + a_2^2 + 2a_1a_2\cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2)$$\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$, and the cross terms give $\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2 + \sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2 = \cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2)$.
Divide: $\tan\theta = \dfrac{a_1\sin\theta_1 + a_2\sin\theta_2}{a_1\cos\theta_1 + a_2\cos\theta_2}$

Same answer as the arrow picture (M2), with δ = θ₁ − θ₂.

Result · constructive and destructive $$a^2 = a_1^2 + a_2^2 + 2a_1a_2\cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2)$$
Constructive$\theta_1 - \theta_2 = 0, 2\pi, 4\pi, \ldots$ ⟹ $a = a_1 + a_2$ (largest) Destructive$\theta_1 - \theta_2 = \pi, 3\pi, \ldots$ ⟹ $a = |a_1 - a_2|$ (smallest; zero if $a_1 = a_2$)
In terms of path: constructive if $\Delta = m\lambda$, destructive if $\Delta = (m + \frac12)\lambda$.
Energy is not lostWhere the waves cancel, the energy is not destroyed: it appears at the bright places. Interference only redistributes energy. (Proof in T4: the average intensity stays $I_1 + I_2$.)
More than two wavesFor many waves, add all the arrows: $a\cos\theta = \sum a_i\cos\theta_i$, $a\sin\theta = \sum a_i\sin\theta_i$ (Ghatak 13.31–13.34).
Exam trap · "Do intensities obey superposition?"No. Displacements (amplitudes) add. Intensities are squares, and $(a_1 + a_2)^2 \ne a_1^2 + a_2^2$. Equal waves in step give 4 times one intensity, not 2. Intensities add only for incoherent waves (T2, T4).
Check yourself
Amplitudes 3 and 4, phase difference 90°. Resultant amplitude?
$a^2 = 9 + 16 + 0 = 25$, $a = 5$.

UNIT 6 · GHATAK §14.1, 14.3–14.4

Getting light to interfere

T2

Coherence

Why two lamps never give fringes (PYQ N24 Q5, N22 Q13, N20 Q13, N25 Q3, N19 Q17).

{{fig:wave_trains}}
Result · coherent sourcesTwo sources are coherent if the phase difference between their waves stays constant in time. If the phase difference changes at random, they are incoherent.

Why two independent lamps give no fringes

An atom radiates for about $10^{-10}$ s (a wave train of about $10^6$ waves), then another atom takes over with a new, random phaseGhatak §14.4.
So the phase difference φ between two lamps jumps about $10^{10}$ times a second. The fringe pattern moves just as oftenBright and dark swap places each time φ jumps.
The eye averages over 0.1 s. In $I = I_1 + I_2 + 2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos\phi$, the average of $\cos\phi$ is zeroP2: a random angle averages $\cos$ to 0.
Result: $I = I_1 + I_2$, uniform. Two equal lamps give $2I$ everywhere, with no fringes
The cureTake one source and split its light into two parts. Every random jump happens in both parts at once, so their phase difference stays fixed. There are two ways to split:
Division of wavefrontUse two different parts of the same wavefront, side by side (Young's slits, Fresnel's mirrors, biprism, Lloyd's mirror). Division of amplitudeSplit the same part of the wave into two weaker beams by partial reflection (thin films, Newton's rings, Michelson).

Coherence length

The ideaThe two copies of one wave train must overlap when they meet. If one copy travels more than the train's length further, it meets a different train, and the fringes vanish. So the path difference must be smaller than the length of a wave train.
Result $$L = c\,\tau_c \approx \frac{\lambda^2}{\Delta\lambda}$$
Ordinary lamp$\tau_c\sim10^{-10}$ s ⟹ $L\sim3$ cm Sodium lightabout 1 cm White lighta few wavelengths only Lasermetres or more
$\Delta\lambda$ = spread of wavelengths in the light. Purer colour ⟹ longer trains.

This is temporal coherence. Spatial coherence: the source must also be narrow, or the fringes from its different points wash each other out (T5).

Result · conditions for sustained (steady) interference
  1. The two sources must be coherent: derived from one source.
  2. They must give the same frequency (same wavelength).
  3. Their amplitudes should be equal or nearly equal, so the dark fringes are really dark (good contrast).
  4. They should be narrow (fine slits), so each fringe is not blurred.
  5. They must be close together ($d$ small) and the screen far enough away, so the fringes are wide enough to see.
  6. The waves should have the same state of polarisation.
  7. The path difference must be smaller than the coherence length.
Check yourself
Two monochromatic waves, each of intensity I, completely incoherent. Resultant intensity?
$2I$ everywhere (no fringes), because $\langle\cos\phi\rangle = 0$.
Wave trains last $10^{-10}$ s. Coherence length?
$3\times10^8\times10^{-10} = 0.03$ m $= 3$ cm.

UNIT 7 · GHATAK §14.5–14.6, 14.10

Young's fringes, properly

T3

Path difference and fringe width

Why the fringes are equally spaced (PYQ N25 Q14, N22 Q7).

{{fig:ydse}}

Slits S₁, S₂ are $d$ apart; the screen is at distance $D$. P is a point on the screen at height $x$ above the centre O.

$(S_2P)^2 = D^2 + \left(x + \frac d2\right)^2$, $\;(S_1P)^2 = D^2 + \left(x - \frac d2\right)^2$Pythagoras: horizontal side D, vertical side the height difference.
Subtract: $(S_2P)^2 - (S_1P)^2 = 2xd$$D^2$ cancels; $(x + \frac d2)^2 - (x - \frac d2)^2 = 2xd$.
$S_2P - S_1P = \dfrac{2xd}{S_2P + S_1P}$$p^2 - q^2 = (p - q)(p + q)$.
$D \gg d, x$, so $S_2P + S_1P \approx 2D$: $\;\Delta = S_2P - S_1P \approx \dfrac{xd}{D}$M3. For d = 0.02 cm, D = 50 cm, x = 0.5 cm the error is 0.005%.
Bright: $\dfrac{x_nd}{D} = n\lambda$ ⟹ $x_n = \dfrac{n\lambda D}{d}$, $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$
Fringe width: $\beta = x_{n+1} - x_n = \dfrac{\lambda D}{d}$. Dark fringes at $x = (n + \frac12)\dfrac{\lambda D}{d}$, exactly halfwayβ does not depend on n: the fringes are equally spaced.
Result $$\beta = \frac{\lambda D}{d}$$ Bigger λ or bigger D ⟹ wider fringes. Bigger d ⟹ narrower fringes.
Exam trap · "The screen must be close to the sources" (T/F)False. β ∝ D. Bring the screen closer and the fringes crowd together until they cannot be seen. The formula also needs $D \gg d$.
Exam trapβ is the distance from one bright fringe to the next bright one (or dark to dark), not bright to dark.

The true shape of the fringes

The ideaA fringe is a set of points with the same $S_2P - S_1P$. In a plane, the points whose distances from two fixed points differ by a constant form a hyperbola (Plus One conic sections). In space they are hyperboloids. Near the centre of a distant screen ($y \ll D$) these hyperbolas are almost straight lines parallel to the slits: that is what we see.
Result · Ghatak 14.22$\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ with $b^2 = D^2 + \frac14(d^2 - \Delta^2)$. The central fringe (Δ = 0) is the straight line $x = 0$.
Check yourself
λ = 500 nm, d = 0.5 cm, D = 1 m. Fringe width?
$\beta = \frac{5\times10^{-7}\times1}{5\times10^{-3}} = 10^{-4}$ m $= 0.1$ mm.

T4

Intensity distribution

The 10-mark derivation (PYQ N19 Q32, N24 Q20).

Two coherent waves reach P from S₁ and S₂. Their electric fields (nearly parallel, since $D \gg d$):

$$E_1 = E_{01}\cos\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}S_1P - \omega t\right),\quad E_2 = E_{02}\cos\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}S_2P - \omega t\right)$$
Total field $E = E_1 + E_2$; intensity $I = K\langle E^2\rangle$Superposition (T1); intensity ∝ field² averaged (P2). K is a constant.
$E^2 = E_1^2 + E_2^2 + 2E_1E_2$
$2E_1E_2 = E_{01}E_{02}\left\{\cos\!\left[\tfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}(S_2P - S_1P)\right] + \cos\!\left[2\omega t - \tfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}(S_1P + S_2P)\right]\right\}$Identity (c) of M1: product → sum.
Average over time: $\langle E_1^2\rangle = \frac12E_{01}^2$, $\langle E_2^2\rangle = \frac12E_{02}^2$; the $2\omega t$ term averages to 0P2: $\langle\cos^2\rangle = \frac12$, $\langle\cos\rangle = 0$. The first cos does not depend on t, so it stays.
Write $I_1 = \frac12KE_{01}^2$, $I_2 = \frac12KE_{02}^2$, $\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(S_2P - S_1P)$:
Result · two-beam interference $$I = I_1 + I_2 + 2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\,\cos\delta$$
Maximum$\delta = 2m\pi$: $\;I_{\max} = (\sqrt{I_1} + \sqrt{I_2})^2$ Minimum$\delta = (2m+1)\pi$: $\;I_{\min} = (\sqrt{I_1} - \sqrt{I_2})^2$

Equal beams

$I_1 = I_2 = I_0$: $I = 2I_0(1 + \cos\delta) = 4I_0\cos^2\dfrac\delta2$Identity (d) of M1.
Maximum $4I_0$, minimum 0, average $2I_0$Average of $\cos^2$ is ½. The average equals $I_1 + I_2$: energy is only moved around, not created.
{{fig:fringes_cos2}}

Incoherent sources

If the phase difference δ changes at random, $\langle\cos\delta\rangle = 0$ and $I = I_1 + I_2$ (equal sources: $2I_0$) everywhere: uniform light, no fringes (T2).

Visibility (contrast) of fringes

Result $$V = \frac{I_{\max} - I_{\min}}{I_{\max} + I_{\min}} = \frac{2\sqrt{I_1I_2}}{I_1 + I_2}$$ $V = 1$ for equal beams (perfectly dark minima); $V \to 0$ when one beam is much weaker. This is why condition 3 of sustained interference asks for equal amplitudes.
Short way (equal amplitudes)$a\cos\omega t + a\cos(\omega t + \delta) = 2a\cos\frac\delta2\cos(\omega t + \frac\delta2)$ by identity (b). Amplitude $2a\cos\frac\delta2$; intensity $\propto 4a^2\cos^2\frac\delta2$.
Check yourself
$I_1 = 4I_2$. Find $I_{\max}/I_{\min}$ and V.
$\sqrt{I_1} = 2\sqrt{I_2}$: $I_{\max} = 9I_2$, $I_{\min} = I_2$, ratio 9. $V = 8/10 = 0.8$.

T5

Other fringe shapes; wide sources

Ghatak §14.6 examples. Not yet asked, but in the syllabus section.

Two plane waves crossing

Two plane waves of equal amplitude travel at angles +θ and −θ to the z-axis.

$E = E_0\cos(kz\cos\theta + kx\sin\theta - \omega t) + E_0\cos(kz\cos\theta - kx\sin\theta - \omega t)$A wave going at angle θ has phase $k(z\cos\theta \pm x\sin\theta) - \omega t$.
$E = 2E_0\cos(kx\sin\theta)\cos(kz\cos\theta - \omega t)$Identity (b) of M1.
$I = 4I_0\cos^2(kx\sin\theta)$: straight fringes along z, spacing $\beta = \dfrac{\lambda}{2\sin\theta}$$\cos^2$ repeats when $kx\sin\theta$ grows by π, i.e. x grows by $\pi/(k\sin\theta) = \lambda/(2\sin\theta)$.
Check yourself
θ = 30°, λ = 5000 Å. β?
$\beta = \lambda/(2\times0.5) = \lambda = 5000$ Å $= 0.5$ μm.

Screen perpendicular to the line S₁S₂

If the screen faces along the line joining the two sources, Δ is the same on circles round the axis: the fringes are circles (this is the shape seen in Michelson, T20).

A wide source

The ideaEach point of a wide source S makes its own fringe pattern, slightly shifted. The patterns of different points are incoherent, so their intensities add. If the source is too wide, bright fringes of one point fall on dark fringes of another, and the contrast is lost. This is why the first slit must be narrow (spatial coherence).
ResultFringes stay visible only if the source width $w$ satisfies roughly $w \lesssim \dfrac{\lambda D_s}{2d}$ ($D_s$ = source-to-slit distance).

In the model paper: Q32; also used in Q4.

T6

Shift of fringes by a thin plate

Ghatak §14.10. A favourite numerical.

{{fig:fringe_shift}}
Time from S₁ to P through a plate of thickness t: $\dfrac{S_1P - t}{c} + \dfrac{t}{v} = \dfrac{1}{c}[S_1P + (n-1)t]$$v = c/n$, so $t/v = nt/c$.
So the plate adds the optical path $(n-1)t$ to beam 1 (P4)
The new central fringe O′ is where both total paths are equal: $S_1O' + (n-1)t = S_2O'$
From T3: $S_2O' - S_1O' = \dfrac{d}{D}\,OO'$
So $(n-1)t = \dfrac{d}{D}\,OO'$, and the shift is $\;\Delta x = \dfrac{D(n-1)t}{d}$Ghatak 14.37.
Result $$\Delta x = \frac{D(n-1)t}{d} = \frac{(n-1)t}{\lambda}\,\beta$$ The whole pattern shifts towards the covered slit by $(n-1)t/\lambda$ fringes. The fringe width does not change.
Exam trapUse $(n-1)t$, not $nt$: the plate replaces a thickness t of air. The shift is towards the slit with the plate (that beam is "slowed", so it needs a shorter geometric path).
Example (Ghatak 14.10): mica $n = 1.58$, $d = 0.1$ cm, $D = 50$ cm, shift 0.2 cm. $t = \dfrac{d\,\Delta x}{D(n-1)} = \dfrac{0.1\times0.2}{50\times0.58}$
$t = 6.9\times10^{-4}$ cmThe book prints 6.7; the arithmetic gives 6.9.
Check yourself
After inserting the plate, the centre O is occupied by the 11th bright fringe (λ = 5893 Å, n = 1.58). t?
$(n-1)t = 11\lambda$: $t = 11\times5893\times10^{-8}/0.58 = 1.12\times10^{-3}$ cm.
