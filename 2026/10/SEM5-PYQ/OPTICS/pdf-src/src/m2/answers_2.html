25PYQ N22 Q19

Superposition of two sinusoidal waves; conditions.

Know firstT1M1M2

Let $x_1 = a_1\cos(\omega t + \theta_1)$, $x_2 = a_2\cos(\omega t + \theta_2)$. By superposition, $x = x_1 + x_2$.

$x = (a_1\cos\theta_1 + a_2\cos\theta_2)\cos\omega t - (a_1\sin\theta_1 + a_2\sin\theta_2)\sin\omega t$
Put $a\cos\theta = a_1\cos\theta_1 + a_2\cos\theta_2$ and $a\sin\theta = a_1\sin\theta_1 + a_2\sin\theta_2$. Then $x = a\cos(\omega t + \theta)$: an SHM of the same frequency
Squaring and adding: $a^2 = a_1^2 + a_2^2 + 2a_1a_2\cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2)$; $\;\tan\theta = \dfrac{a_1\sin\theta_1 + a_2\sin\theta_2}{a_1\cos\theta_1 + a_2\cos\theta_2}$
Constructive$\theta_1 - \theta_2 = 2m\pi$ ⟹ $a = a_1 + a_2$, $I \propto (a_1 + a_2)^2$ (path diff. mλ) Destructive$\theta_1 - \theta_2 = (2m+1)\pi$ ⟹ $a = |a_1 - a_2|$ (path diff. $(m+\frac12)\lambda$)

For $a_1 = a_2$ the destructive resultant is zero. The energy missing at minima appears at maxima: interference redistributes energy.

27PYQ N20 Q19

Fresnel's biprism and wavelength.

Know firstT8P5T3
{{fig:biprism}}

Principle. A biprism is two thin prisms of very small refracting angle α (about 30′) joined base to base. Light from a narrow slit S, parallel to the edge, is refracted by the two halves; the emerging beams appear to come from two virtual sources S₁, S₂ (division of wavefront). Being images of the same slit, they are coherent, and interference fringes are formed where the beams overlap.

Theory. Each half deviates light by $(n-1)\alpha$; with the slit at distance a from the biprism, $d = S_1S_2 = 2a(n-1)\alpha$. With the eyepiece at distance b beyond, $D = a + b$ and $\beta = \lambda D/d$.

Determination of λ. Slit, biprism and micrometer eyepiece are mounted on an optical bench and lit by sodium light; the slit is made parallel to the edge to get sharp fringes.

  1. β: the cross-wire is moved across N fringes and β = distance/N.
  2. D: slit-to-eyepiece distance from the bench scale.
  3. d: a convex lens (f < D/4) is placed between biprism and eyepiece; at two positions it forms sharp images of S₁, S₂, with separations d₁ (magnified) and d₂ (diminished). Since the magnifications are v/u and u/v, $d = \sqrt{d_1d_2}$.
$\lambda = \dfrac{\beta\,d}{D} = \dfrac{\beta\sqrt{d_1d_2}}{D}$

Marks: principle and diagram 2 · d and D 1 · measurement procedure and formula 2.

30

Stokes relations; phase change on reflection.

Know firstT11
{{fig:stokes}}

Let r, t be the amplitude reflection and transmission coefficients for light going from medium 1 to 2, and r′, t′ from 2 to 1. A wave of amplitude a gives reflected $ar$ and transmitted $at$.

Reversibility: if the reflected and transmitted waves are reversed, they must recombine to give only the original wave reversed, of amplitude a
Reversed $ar$ gives $ar^2$ (reflected, in medium 1) and $art$ (transmitted, into medium 2). Reversed $at$ gives $att'$ (into medium 1) and $atr'$ (reflected, in medium 2)
In medium 1: $ar^2 + att' = a$ ⟹ $tt' = 1 - r^2$
In medium 2 there must be no wave: $art + atr' = 0$ ⟹ $r' = -r$
$r' = -r$, $\;tt' = 1 - r^2$

Since $r' = -r = r\,e^{i\pi}$, the reflections at the two sides of an interface differ in phase by π. Lloyd's mirror (dark central fringe) shows that the phase change π occurs on reflection at the denser medium; reflection at the rarer medium gives no phase change. Consistent with Fresnel's normal-incidence result $r = (n_1 - n_2)/(n_1 + n_2)$, negative when $n_2 > n_1$.

Marks: reversibility set-up 1 · two equations 2 · relations 1 · phase change 1.

31PYQ N24 Q13

Plane parallel film, plane wave.

Know firstT13T12T2
{{fig:thin_film}}
  • A plane wave falls at angle i on a film of index n and thickness d. Each ray is divided at the upper surface: one part reflected (ray 1), one refracted, reflected at the lower surface and emerging parallel to ray 1 (ray 2). This is division of amplitude.
  • The path difference is $2nd\cos r$ (cosine law), and ray 1 has an extra phase π (reflection at the denser medium). Reflected light: maxima $2nd\cos r = (m+\frac12)\lambda$, minima $2nd\cos r = m\lambda$; transmitted: the reverse.
  • For a plane wave, i (hence r) is the same at every point; if d is uniform, the path difference is the same everywhere, and the whole film appears uniformly bright or dark. No fringes are formed; fringes appear only if the thickness varies (fringes of equal thickness).
  • The eye sees only the light from a small region of the film which enters the pupil.
  • If the film is thick, the path difference exceeds the coherence length and no interference is seen.

Marks: division of amplitude and diagram 2 · conditions 1 · uniform illumination and limits 2.

33PYQ N24 Q16

Plane parallel film, point (or extended) source.

Know firstT14T13
{{fig:haidinger}}
  • Light from a point source reaches the film at different angles. The reflected rays from the two surfaces have path difference $2nd\cos\theta'$, which now varies with the angle of refraction θ′.
  • All rays making the same angle with the normal form a cone about the normal from the source, so the points of equal path difference lie on circles: the fringes are concentric circular rings. Dark rings: $2nd\cos\theta' = m\lambda$ (film in air).
  • With an extended source, each point gives such rings, and they coincide only at infinity; the fringes are localised at infinity and are seen with the eye relaxed or a telescope focused at infinity. These are Haidinger's fringes, or fringes of equal inclination.
  • The order is highest at the centre (θ′ = 0) and decreases outward.

N20 Q20 (conditions; difference from Newton's rings). Conditions: film of uniform thickness, broad monochromatic source, observation at infinity near normal incidence. Haidinger: constant thickness, fringes of equal inclination, localised at infinity, highest order at centre. Newton's rings: varying thickness, fringes of equal thickness, localised at the film, zero order (dark) at centre.

Marks: diagram 1 · path difference 1 · ring formation 2 · localisation 1.

34PYQ N24 Q15

Non-reflecting films.

Know firstT15T11T12
{{fig:ar_coating}}

About 4% of light is reflected at each air–glass surface; in multi-lens instruments this loses light and produces flare. A thin transparent film of index $n_f$ (between $n_a$ and $n_g$, e.g. MgF₂, 1.38) on the glass reduces the reflection.

Both reflections (air → film, film → glass) are at denser media, so both suffer π: no net phase change
Destructive interference: $2n_fd = \dfrac\lambda2$ ⟹ $d = \dfrac{\lambda}{4n_f}$ (quarter-wave film)
Complete cancellation requires equal amplitudes: $\dfrac{n_f - n_a}{n_f + n_a} = \dfrac{n_g - n_f}{n_g + n_f}$ ⟹ $n_f = \sqrt{n_an_g}$

For glass of 1.5, the ideal $n_f = 1.22$; MgF₂ (1.38) is used, with d ≈ 900 Å for λ = 5000 Å, reducing reflection from 4% to about 1.4%. The reflected energy is not destroyed but transmitted. Cancellation is exact for one wavelength (chosen in the yellow-green), so coated lenses look purple.

N19 Q22 (application in reducing reflectivity of lens surfaces): same answer.

38PYQ N22 Q20

Michelson interferometer; difference of wavelengths.

Know firstT20T21
{{fig:michelson}}

Light from a broad source S is divided by the half-silvered plate G₁ (45°) into a reflected beam to M₁ (movable) and a transmitted beam to M₂ (fixed); G₂ is a compensating plate. The beams return to G₁ and recombine in the telescope. M₂′ (image of M₂ in G₁) and M₁ form an air film of thickness d, giving fringes with $2d\cos\theta = m\lambda$ (dark).

Light with two close wavelengths λ₁ > λ₂ (sodium D lines). At d ≈ 0 (found with white light) both sets of rings coincide: distinct fringes
As M₁ moves, the fringes become indistinct when the bright rings of one fall on the dark rings of the other: $\dfrac{2d}{\lambda_2} - \dfrac{2d}{\lambda_1} = \dfrac12$
Between two successive disappearances the mirror moves Δd, and the orders differ by one more: $\dfrac{2\Delta d}{\lambda_2} - \dfrac{2\Delta d}{\lambda_1} = 1$
$\Delta\lambda = \lambda_1 - \lambda_2 = \dfrac{\lambda_1\lambda_2}{2\Delta d} \approx \dfrac{\lambda^2}{2\Delta d}$
$\Delta\lambda = \dfrac{\lambda^2}{2\Delta d}$; e.g. Δd = 0.289 mm, λ = 5893 Å gives Δλ = 6.0 Å

Marks: diagram and working 2 · disappearance condition 1 · derivation 1 · result 1.
