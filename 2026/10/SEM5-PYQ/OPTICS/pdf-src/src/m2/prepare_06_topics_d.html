T18

Newton's rings

The most-asked topic of the module (PYQ N22 Q32, N22 Q28, N19 Q27, N20 Q26, N19 Q3, N20 Q3).

{{fig:newton_rings}}
The set-upA plano-convex lens of large radius R (curved side down) rests on a flat glass plate. Between them is a thin air film: zero thickness at the contact point, growing outward. Sodium light is turned down onto it by a glass plate at 45°, and the rings are viewed from above with a travelling microscope.
At distance r from the contact point the air film has thickness t, with $r^2 \approx 2Rt$Circle geometry (P6).
Light falls normally; air film (n = 1) between glass: one π change (glass → air at the lens: none; air → glass at the plate: π)T12, "count the π's".
Dark: $2t = m\lambda$. Put $2t = r^2/R$: $\;r_m^2 = m\lambda R$With a liquid of index n in the gap: $2nt = m\lambda$, so $r_m^2 = m\lambda R/n$.
Bright: $2t = (m + \frac12)\lambda$ ⟹ $r^2 = (m + \frac12)\lambda R$
Points of equal thickness lie on circles round the contact point ⟹ the fringes are rings (fringes of equal thickness)
Result · Newton's rings (reflected) $$r_m^2 = m\lambda R\ \text{(dark)},\qquad D_m^2 = 4m\lambda R$$
  • Centre (t = 0, m = 0): dark in reflected light; bright in transmitted light (complementary, but low contrast).
  • $r \propto \sqrt m$: rings get closer further out (1, 1.41, 1.73, 2, …).

Measuring λ

Measure the diameters of many dark rings with the travelling microscope (cross-wire on the left edge, then the right edge)
$D_{m+p}^2 = 4(m+p)\lambda R$, $\;D_m^2 = 4m\lambda R$
Subtract: $\lambda = \dfrac{D_{m+p}^2 - D_m^2}{4pR}$Using a difference cancels any error from dust or imperfect contact at the centre (which adds a constant to every $D^2$), and we need not know which ring is m = 1.
Find R with a spherometer. Or plot $D^2$ against m: a straight line of slope $4\lambda R$
Result $$\lambda = \frac{D_{m+p}^2 - D_m^2}{4pR},\qquad n_{\text{liquid}} = \frac{D_{\text{air}}^2}{D_{\text{liquid}}^2}$$
Exam trapThe formula uses diameters squared. If given radii, $D^2 = 4r^2$. With a liquid the rings shrink by $\sqrt n$.
Example (PYQ N22 Q28): λ = 670 nm, $r_{20} = 1.1\times10^{-2}$ m. $R = \dfrac{r_{20}^2}{20\lambda} = \dfrac{1.21\times10^{-4}}{20\times6.7\times10^{-7}} = 9.03$ m
$r_{30} = r_{20}\sqrt{30/20} = 1.1\times10^{-2}\times1.2247 = 1.35\times10^{-2}$ m
Example (PYQ N19 Q27): $D_{10} = 0.3$ cm, λ = $6\times10^{-5}$ cm. $r_{10} = 0.15$ cm: $R = \dfrac{0.0225}{10\times6\times10^{-5}} = 37.5$ cm
Air film there: $t = \dfrac{m\lambda}{2} = \dfrac{10\times6\times10^{-5}}{2} = 3\times10^{-4}$ cm
Exam trap · "9th bright ring"Bright rings: $r^2 = (m+\frac12)\lambda R$ with m = 0 for the first bright ring. So the 9th bright ring has m = 8. State the counting you use. (PYQ N20 Q26: R = 100 cm, λ = $6\times10^{-5}$ cm gives 0.226 cm and 0.239 cm for the 9th and 10th.)
Check yourself
Air replaced by water (n = 1.33). The 10th ring's diameter changes by what factor?
$1/\sqrt{1.33} = 0.867$: it shrinks by about 13%.

T19

Two close wavelengths: rings fade and return

Sodium light is really two lines, 5890 Å and 5896 Å (Ghatak §15.10).

The ideaEach wavelength makes its own set of rings. Near the centre they coincide. Further out, the slightly shorter wavelength gets ahead; where its dark ring lands on a bright ring of the other, the rings blur out. Further still they come back into step.
Rings vanish where the orders differ by a half: $\dfrac{2t}{\lambda_2} - \dfrac{2t}{\lambda_1} = \dfrac12$ (λ₁ > λ₂)
$2t\,\dfrac{\lambda_1 - \lambda_2}{\lambda_1\lambda_2} = \dfrac12$ ⟹ $t_0 = \dfrac{\lambda_1\lambda_2}{4(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)}$
They reappear when the difference is 1: $t = 2t_0$. Then vanish again at $3t_0$, and so on
Sodium: $t_0 = \dfrac{5890\times5896\times10^{-16}}{4\times6\times10^{-8}}$ cm $= 1.45\times10^{-2}$ cm
That is near ring number $m \approx 2t_0/\lambda \approx 490$. With R = 100 cm, $r = \sqrt{2Rt_0} = 1.7$ cm
Check yourself
Why do Newton's rings in white light show only a few coloured rings?
Same idea with very many wavelengths: they fall out of step after a few rings, and the mixture looks white.

T20

Michelson interferometer

Construction, fringes, λ, the metre (PYQ N19 Q21, N20 Q32, N25 Q4).

{{fig:michelson}}

Construction and working

  • S: broad source (sodium lamp with a ground glass).
  • G₁: glass plate, half-silvered on the back, at 45°. It splits each wave into two beams of equal amplitude (division of amplitude): one reflected towards M₁, one transmitted towards M₂.
  • M₁, M₂: plane mirrors at right angles. M₁ moves along its arm on a screw with a fine scale; M₂ is fixed, with tilt screws.
  • G₂: compensating plate, same thickness and glass as G₁, parallel to it.
  • The beams return, recombine at G₁ and go to the telescope T, where they interfere.
The key pictureSeen from the telescope, M₂ appears (by reflection in G₁) as a virtual mirror M₂′ beside M₁. The interferometer behaves like an air film between M₁ and M₂′, of thickness $d = x_1 - x_2$ (the difference of the two arm lengths). Moving M₁ changes d at will.

Circular fringes

With M₁ parallel to M₂′, it is a parallel air film: path difference $2d\cos\theta$, θ = angle of the ray to the mirror normalCosine law (T13) with n = 1.
One beam reflects off the silvered surface of G₁ from the air side, the other from the glass side: they differ by an extra π (Ghatak's convention)Stokes, T11.
Dark: $2d\cos\theta = m\lambda$; bright: $2d\cos\theta = (m + \frac12)\lambda$. Equal θ ⟹ rings, Haidinger type (T14), seen focused at infinity
Result · Michelson $$2d\cos\theta = m\lambda\ (\text{dark}),\quad 2d\cos\theta = (m+\tfrac12)\lambda\ (\text{bright})$$ d = separation of M₁ and M₂′ (= $x_1 - x_2$), θ = angle of the ray, m = order. Many books ignore the π and write $2d\cos\theta = m\lambda$ for bright; say which you use.
  • As d decreases, each ring shrinks and disappears into the centre; the rings get wider. As d increases, rings come out of the centre.
  • Rings near the centre: $\theta_p^2 \approx p\lambda/d$, so radius ∝ √p.

Measuring λ

Centre (θ = 0): $2d = m\lambda$ for a dark centre
Move M₁ by $d_0$ and count N fringes passing the centre: $2(d - d_0) = (m - N)\lambda$
Subtract: $2d_0 = N\lambda$ ⟹ $\lambda = \dfrac{2d_0}{N}$Each fringe = a mirror movement of λ/2 (the light goes there and back).
Example: 1000 fringes for $d_0 = 2.90\times10^{-2}$ cm: λ $= 5.8\times10^{-5}$ cm = 5800 Å

Compensating plate

The ideaThe beam to M₁ crosses the glass of G₁ three times; without G₂, the beam to M₂ crosses it once. So one beam has an extra glass path $2(n-1)t$. For one wavelength, moving M₁ could make up for it. But n depends on λ, so for white light no single mirror position equalises all colours. G₂ gives the other beam the same glass, so the paths are equal for every λ. It is essential for white-light fringes.

Types of fringes

Straight and white-light types are general-knowledge material (Ghatak gives circular only); they are standard in Indian textbooks.

White light finds d = 0White-light fringes appear only when the two paths are equal within a few wavelengths. So they mark exactly the position d = 0 (used in the metre experiment and in measuring Δλ).

Standardising the metre

The ideaMichelson and Benoit (1892–93) measured the old standard metre bar in wavelengths of the red cadmium line, so that the metre could be rebuilt anywhere from light.
  1. Nine etalons (two mirrors fixed a set distance apart) were made, each half the length of the previous: from 10 cm down to about 0.39 mm.
  2. The number of Cd red wavelengths in the smallest etalon was found by counting fringes as M₁ moved from one of its mirrors to the other (white-light fringes mark each mirror position).
  3. Each etalon was then compared with one twice its length, by stepping it along; the 10 cm etalon was stepped 10 times along the metre bar.
Result1 m = 1 553 164.13 wavelengths of the red cadmium line (λ = 6438.4696 Å), accurate to about 1 part in 10⁹. (Today the metre is defined through the speed of light.)

Etalon details are from Book 2 / general knowledge (Ghatak gives the result only).

T21

Michelson: the difference of two wavelengths

Separating the sodium doublet (PYQ N22 Q20).

With white light, set d = 0. Then use sodium light (λ₁ > λ₂, very close). Each makes its own rings; at d = 0 they coincide: sharp fringes
Move M₁. The fringes fade when the dark rings of one fall on the bright rings of the other: $\dfrac{2d}{\lambda_2} - \dfrac{2d}{\lambda_1} = \dfrac12$Same idea as T19.
They become sharp again, then fade again when the difference of orders grows by 1. Between two successive fadings M₁ moves Δd with $\dfrac{2\Delta d}{\lambda_2} - \dfrac{2\Delta d}{\lambda_1} = 1$
$2\Delta d\,\dfrac{\lambda_1 - \lambda_2}{\lambda_1\lambda_2} = 1$ ⟹ $\Delta\lambda = \dfrac{\lambda_1\lambda_2}{2\Delta d} \approx \dfrac{\lambda^2}{2\Delta d}$
Result $$\Delta\lambda \approx \frac{\lambda^2}{2\,\Delta d}$$ Δd = mirror movement between two successive disappearances.
Example (Ghatak 15.4): Δd = 0.289 mm, λ = 5893 Å
$\Delta\lambda = \dfrac{(5.893\times10^{-7})^2}{2\times2.89\times10^{-4}} = 6.0\times10^{-10}$ m = 6 Å ✓ (5896 − 5890)
Exam trapUse the distance between two successive disappearances (difference of orders = 1). The first disappearance from d = 0 is only half that (difference ½).
Link to coherence lengthIf the light has a continuous spread Δλ, the fringes vanish for good once $2d \gtrsim \lambda^2/\Delta\lambda$: the coherence length (T2).
