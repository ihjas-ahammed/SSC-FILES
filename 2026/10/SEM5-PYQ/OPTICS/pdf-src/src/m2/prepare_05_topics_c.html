UNIT 10 · GHATAK §15.1–15.4, 15.8

Splitting the amplitude: thin films

T12

A thin film at normal incidence

The simplest film, and the half-wave rule (PYQ N19 Q13, N19 Q5, N22 Q27).

The ideaLight falls straight down on a thin transparent film (a soap film, an oil layer) of index n and thickness t, with air on both sides. Part reflects at the top surface (ray 1). Part enters, reflects at the bottom, and comes back out (ray 2). These two are copies of the same wave: division of amplitude.
Ray 2 goes down and up through the film: extra optical path $2nt$Optical path = n × length (P4).
Ray 1 reflects air → film (rare → dense): phase change π. Ray 2 reflects film → air (dense → rare): noneT11.
So the two rays differ by $2nt$ plus an extra half wave
Result · film in air, reflected light, normal incidence
Dark$2nt = m\lambda$ Bright$2nt = (m + \frac12)\lambda$
$m = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ In transmitted light the conditions are swapped (no reflection π difference), so a film that looks bright in reflection looks dark in transmission, and vice versa.

When the half-wave rule changes

Count the π'sWhat matters is whether the two rays get a different number of π changes.
  • Film in air, or air film between glass plates: one π (one reflection rare → dense) ⟹ the conditions above.
  • $n_1 < n_2 < n_3$ (for example oil 1.60 between crown glass 1.52 above and flint 1.66 below, or a coating on glass): both reflections are rare → dense, both get π, which cancel ⟹ conditions swap: bright $2nt = m\lambda$, dark $2nt = (m+\frac12)\lambda$.
Exam trap · fill in the blank"The optical path of light reflected from the lower surface of a thin film increases with increase of refractive index of the film." Because the extra path is $2nt$.
Example (PYQ N22 Q27): soap bubble n = 1.42, λ = 5839 Å. Least thickness that looks bright in reflection?
Bright: $2nt = (m+\frac12)\lambda$; least thickness for $m = 0$: $t = \dfrac{\lambda}{4n}$
$t = \dfrac{5839}{4\times1.42} = 1028$ Å $\approx 1.03\times10^{-7}$ m
Remind meInside the film the wavelength is $\lambda/n$. So $2nt = m\lambda$ just says "the round trip $2t$ holds m wavelengths of the film".
Check yourself
A very thin soap film (t → 0) in reflected light looks…?
Dark: $2nt = 0 = m\lambda$ with m = 0. Only the π difference is left, and the rays cancel. (Soap films look black just before they burst.)

T13

Oblique incidence: the cosine law

The 5- and 10-mark derivation (PYQ N24 Q14, N25 Q20, N24 Q13).

{{fig:thin_film}}

A plane wave falls at angle θ on a film of index $n_2$ and thickness d, in a medium of index $n_1$. Inside, the angle is θ′ with $n_1\sin\theta = n_2\sin\theta'$.

{{fig:cosine_law}}
One incident ray splits at B: part reflects along BC, part enters, reflects at D and leaves at F, parallel to the firstDivision of amplitude: both come from the same ray.
Draw FC ⟂ the two outgoing rays (an outgoing wavefront). Beyond C and F the two rays travel equal paths, so $\Delta = n_2(BD + DF) - n_1\,BC$Both start together at B; one goes B → C outside, the other B → D → F inside.
Let B′ be the mirror image of B in the lower surface (BJ = JB′ = d). Then $BD = B'D$, and B′, D, F are on one straight lineReflection at D is like coming straight from the image point (P5). So $BD + DF = B'F$.
$\angle CFB = \theta$, so $BC = BF\sin\theta$FC ⟂ the reflected ray, which makes angle θ with the normal; so FC makes angle θ with the surface. In the right triangle BCF, BC is opposite that angle.
Drop BK ⟂ B′F. B′F makes angle θ′ with the normal, so $90° - \theta'$ with the surface; in the right triangle BKF, $KF = BF\cos(90° - \theta') = BF\sin\theta'$
Snell: $n_1\sin\theta = n_2\sin\theta'$ ⟹ $n_1\,BC = n_1BF\sin\theta = n_2BF\sin\theta' = n_2\,KF$
$\Delta = n_2\,B'F - n_2\,KF = n_2\,B'K$
In triangle BKB′: $BB' = 2d$ and $\angle KB'B = \theta'$, so $B'K = 2d\cos\theta'$B′F makes angle θ′ with the normal BB′.
Result · cosine law $$\Delta = 2n_2\,d\cos\theta'$$ θ′ is the angle of refraction (inside the film). Normal incidence: θ′ = 0, Δ = 2nd.
Result · maxima and minima (film in air, $n_2 = n$)
Reflected, bright$2nd\cos\theta' = (m+\frac12)\lambda$ Reflected, dark$2nd\cos\theta' = m\lambda$ Transmitted, bright$2nd\cos\theta' = m\lambda$ Transmitted, dark$2nd\cos\theta' = (m+\frac12)\lambda$
Reflected and transmitted patterns are complementary (energy is conserved).
Exam trapUse cos θ′ (inside angle), not cos θ. If asked for i: $\cos\theta' = \sqrt{1 - \sin^2 i/n^2}$, so $\Delta = 2d\sqrt{n^2 - \sin^2 i}$.

Why the reflected fringes are sharp, the transmitted faint

Ghatak's example (n = 1.36): the two main reflected rays have amplitudes 0.153a and 0.149a (almost equal ⟹ near-perfect dark fringes). The two main transmitted rays have 0.977a and 0.023a (very unequal ⟹ poor contrast; see visibility in T4).

A film lit by a plane wave

The idea · PYQ N24 Q13

In a plane wave every ray has the same θ, so θ′ is the same everywhere. If the film has the same thickness everywhere, Δ is the same at every point: the whole film looks uniformly bright, or uniformly dark, or in between. There are no fringes.

If the thickness varies a little, bright and dark bands follow lines of equal thickness (wedge, T17; Newton's rings, T18).

Looking at a large film, the eye's pupil collects reflected light only from a small region, so it sees only a small part at a time.

If the film is too thick, Δ exceeds the coherence length (T2): no fringes.

Check yourself
n = 1.5, d = 2000 Å, θ′ = 60°. Δ?
$2\times1.5\times2000\times0.5 = 3000$ Å.

T14

Point or extended source: Haidinger's fringes

Fringes of equal inclination (PYQ N24 Q16, N20 Q20).

The ideaFrom a point source, rays reach a parallel film at different angles. Δ = 2nd cos θ′ now changes with the angle. All rays with the same angle lie on a cone around the normal from the source, so the fringes are circles.
{{fig:haidinger}}
Result · Haidinger's fringes
  • Conditions: a film (plate) of constant thickness; a broad (extended) monochromatic source; viewing with the eye or a telescope focused at infinity, near normal incidence.
  • Each fringe = one value of θ′: fringes of equal inclination, concentric rings, localised at infinity.
  • At the centre θ′ = 0, cos θ′ largest, so the order is highest at the centre and decreases outward.

LocalisationWith an extended source, fringes are clear only in one place. For a parallel film: at infinity. For a wedge (T17) or Newton's rings: at (very near) the film, so you must focus on the film. With a point source the fringes are seen everywhere (not localised).

T15

Non-reflecting (anti-reflection) films

Why camera lenses look purple (PYQ N19 Q22, N24 Q15, N22 Q4).

{{fig:ar_coating}}
The problemEach air–glass surface reflects about 4% of the light (T11 check). A camera with many lens surfaces loses a lot of light and gets stray reflections (flare). Fix: coat the lens with a thin transparent film that makes the two reflections cancel.

Air $n_a$, film $n_f$, glass $n_g$, with $n_a < n_f < n_g$ (for example MgF₂, $n_f = 1.38$, on glass 1.5).

Both reflections are rare → dense: both get π, which cancelT12, "count the π's".
For cancellation, the path difference must be half a wave: $2n_fd = \dfrac\lambda2$ ⟹ $d = \dfrac{\lambda}{4n_f}$A "quarter-wave" film: a quarter of the wavelength inside the film.
For complete cancellation the two reflected amplitudes must be equal: $\dfrac{n_f - n_a}{n_f + n_a} = \dfrac{n_g - n_f}{n_g + n_f}$Fresnel's r (T11); the transmission factors through the top surface are close to 1 (about 0.97).
Cross-multiply and simplify: $n_f^2 = n_an_g$ ⟹ $n_f = \sqrt{n_an_g}$
Result · non-reflecting film $$d = \frac{\lambda}{4n_f},\qquad n_f = \sqrt{n_a\,n_g}$$
Ideal for glass 1.5$n_f = 1.22$ (no hard material that low; MgF₂ 1.38 is used) MgF₂, λ = 5000 Å$d = 5000/(4\times1.38) \approx 900$ Å Reflection4% uncoated → about 1.4% with MgF₂
Where does the light go?Not destroyed: the energy not reflected goes into the transmitted beam. The lens passes more light.
Why purpleThe film is a quarter-wave only for one wavelength, chosen in the middle of the spectrum (yellow-green, where the eye is most sensitive). Red and violet are not fully cancelled and are partly reflected: red + violet = purple tint.
Exact result (Ghatak 15.30–15.36)$R = \dfrac{r_1^2 + r_2^2 + 2r_1r_2\cos2\delta}{1 + r_1^2r_2^2 + 2r_1r_2\cos2\delta}$, $\delta = \frac{2\pi n_fd}{\lambda}$. Minimum at $d = \frac{\lambda}{4n_f}, \frac{3\lambda}{4n_f}, \ldots$ with $R_{\min} = \left(\frac{n_an_g - n_f^2}{n_an_g + n_f^2}\right)^2$. The thinnest (λ/4) is preferred: its dip is broadest over wavelengths.

Colours, wedges, rings and Michelson

T16

Colours of thin films

Asked in four different years (PYQ N19 Q14, N22 Q14, N24 Q7, N22 Q8, N19 Q17).

The idea

White light falls on a soap bubble or an oil film on water. For a given thickness and viewing angle, $2nd\cos\theta'$ is fixed. Some wavelengths satisfy the bright condition $2nd\cos\theta' = (m+\frac12)\lambda$ and are reflected strongly; others satisfy the dark condition and are missing. The film shows the mixture of the reinforced colours.

As the thickness changes from place to place (and with the viewing angle), the colour changes: coloured bands following lines of equal thickness.

Exam trap · "Colours of soap bubbles are due to dispersion" (T/F)False. They are due to interference between light reflected from the two surfaces of the film. (Dispersion = splitting by a prism, because n depends on λ.)

No colours in thick plates

Result · two reasons
  1. In a thick plate, $2nd$ is many wavelengths. At any point, very many wavelengths across the spectrum are bright and very many are dark; their mixture looks white (as far out in white-light fringes, T9).
  2. The path difference is longer than the coherence length of white light (T2), so the two reflected beams are not coherent.
So colours appear only for films a few wavelengths thick.
Check yourself
Why does a soap film's colour change as it drains?
Its thickness changes, so a different set of wavelengths satisfies the bright condition.

T17

The wedge-shaped film

Straight, equally spaced fringes (PYQ N20 Q31, N20 Q10).

{{fig:wedge}}

Two flat glass plates touch at one edge; a thin paper or wire at the other edge makes a thin wedge of air (or liquid of index n) with a very small angle φ. Light falls normally.

At distance x from the edge, the thickness is $t = x\tan\phi \approx x\phi$Small angle (M3).
Bright (reflected): $2nt = (m+\frac12)\lambda$, so $2n\phi\,x_m = (m+\frac12)\lambda$One π change, as in T12.
Next bright fringe: $2n\phi\,x_{m+1} = (m+\frac32)\lambda$
Subtract: $2n\phi(x_{m+1} - x_m) = \lambda$ ⟹ $\beta = \dfrac{\lambda}{2n\phi}$
Result · wedge $$\beta = \frac{\lambda}{2n\phi}$$
  • Fringes are straight and parallel to the edge (each = a line of equal thickness), and equally spaced (β does not depend on m).
  • From one fringe to the next, the thickness changes by $\lambda/2n$.
  • At the edge (t = 0): dark in reflected light.
Example (PYQ N20 Q31): air wedge, λ = 6000 Å, 100 fringes per cm ⟹ β = 0.01 cm
$\phi = \dfrac{\lambda}{2\beta} = \dfrac{6\times10^{-5}}{2\times0.01} = 3\times10^{-3}$ rad $\approx 0.17°$
Exam trap · "Fringes from two perfectly plane surfaces are not equal" (T/F)False. Between two optically flat plates the thickness grows evenly, so the fringes are equally spaced straight lines. Curved or uneven fringes show the surface is not flat (a test of flatness).
UsesMeasuring the thickness of a thin wire, hair or foil (lab experiment 7): if it is at distance L from the edge, its thickness is $L\phi = L\lambda/2\beta$. Testing optical flatness.
Check yourself
Air replaced by a liquid of n = 1.33. Fringe width?
Divided by 1.33: the fringes come closer.
