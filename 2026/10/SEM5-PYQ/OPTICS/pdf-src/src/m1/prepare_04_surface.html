One curved surface

Snell's law at a curved piece of glass. Lenses and mirrors are built from this.

T6

Paraxial rays, axis and signs

The ground rules before any curved-surface formula.

Result · paraxial approximation (PYQ N22 Q1)

We use only rays that stay close to the axis and make small angles with it. These are paraxial rays. For them:

  • $\sin\phi \approx \tan\phi \approx \phi$ (M1);
  • the bulge of the surface is negligible (M2), so distances to the point where the ray hits can be measured from the pole P;
  • all paraxial rays from one object point meet at one image point.
Axis and meridional rays (Ghatak §4.2.2)

For a single spherical surface, the axis is the line joining the object point O and the centre C. Every ray from O lies in a plane that contains this line (a meridional plane), so after refraction it must cross the axis again. A ray that does not lie in such a plane is a skew ray.

For a lens, the axis is the line joining the centres of its two surfaces.

Remind me · the sign rule (P4) Origin at the pole P. Light goes left → right. Right is +, left is −, up is +. $R>0$ if C is to the right of P.
How Ghatak works In the derivations, Ghatak first uses plain sizes $x$, $y$, $r$ (all positive) from the picture. At the end he replaces them with signed values: for a real object on the left, $u = -x$. This two-stage method is safe: geometry first, signs last. Use it in your answers too.
Exam trapThe approximation fails for rays far from the axis. Their images are blurred: spherical aberration (T5). Say "paraxial" in every derivation of T7–T15. It earns a mark.
Check yourself
1. Fill in: in the paraxial approximation we consider rays which pass ______ the axis.
close to (and at small angles with) the axis.
2. What is a meridional ray?
A ray lying in a plane that contains the axis. All rays from an axial object point are meridional for a single spherical surface.

T7

Refraction at one spherical surface (geometry route)

The most important derivation of the module (PYQ N19 Q18). Everything after this reuses it.

{{fig:refraction_surface}}

O is a point object in medium $n_1$, at distance $x$ from the pole P. The surface has centre C at distance $r$. A ray from O hits the surface at A, height $h$, and after refraction meets the axis at I, distance $y$ from P. The normal at A is the line CA.

Angles: $\alpha_1$ at O, $\beta$ at C, $\alpha_2$ at I (all with the axis). At A: angle of incidence $\phi_1$, angle of refraction $\phi_2$, both measured from the normal CA.

Step A: angles from geometry

$\phi_1 = \alpha_1 + \beta$Triangle OAC. $\phi_1$ is the exterior angle at A, which equals the sum of the far angles at O and C (M2).
$\beta = \phi_2 + \alpha_2 \;\Rightarrow\; \phi_2 = \beta - \alpha_2$Triangle CAI. $\beta$ is the exterior angle at C.

Step B: small angles

$\alpha_1 \approx \dfrac{h}{x}$,   $\beta \approx \dfrac{h}{r}$,   $\alpha_2 \approx \dfrac{h}{y}$Paraxial (T6): angle ≈ height ÷ distance along the axis (M1).
$\phi_1 \approx \dfrac hx + \dfrac hr$,   $\phi_2 \approx \dfrac hr - \dfrac hy$Put these into step A. These are Ghatak eqs. 4.2 and 4.3.

Step C: Snell's law, then cancel $h$

$n_1\phi_1 = n_2\phi_2$Snell $n_1\sin\phi_1 = n_2\sin\phi_2$, with $\sin\phi\approx\phi$.
$n_1\left(\dfrac hx + \dfrac hr\right) = n_2\left(\dfrac hr - \dfrac hy\right)$Substitute step B.
$\dfrac{n_1}{x} + \dfrac{n_2}{y} = \dfrac{n_2-n_1}{r}$Divide by $h$ and collect the $r$ terms on the right (Ghatak 4.4). $h$ has gone: every paraxial ray from O, at any height, reaches the same I. So I is an image.

Step D: put in the signs

$u = -x$ (object on the left),   $v = +y$,   $R = +r$ (C on the right)Sign rule (P4).
$-\dfrac{n_1}{u} + \dfrac{n_2}{v} = \dfrac{n_2-n_1}{R}$Replace $x$ by $-u$, $y$ by $v$, $r$ by $R$.
Result · single spherical surface (Ghatak 4.5) $$\frac{n_2}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R}$$

$n_1$ = medium the light comes from, $n_2$ = medium it goes into. This formula holds for every case (convex or concave surface, real or virtual object or image) if you put in the signs.

Sanity checks

• Flat surface ($R\to\infty$): $\dfrac{n_2}{v} = \dfrac{n_1}{u}$, so $v = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}u$. A coin 12 cm deep in water ($n_1 = 4/3$, $n_2=1$) appears at $v = \frac{1}{4/3}(-12) = -9$ cm, i.e. 9 cm deep. This is the apparent depth from Plus Two.

• $n_1 = n_2$: the right side is 0 and $v = u$. With no change of medium, nothing happens.

Worked example: three sign cases

Glass ($n_2=1.5$), light coming from air ($n_1=1$).

(a) Convex towards the light, $R=+10$, real object $u=-30$: $\dfrac{1.5}{v} = \dfrac{0.5}{10} + \dfrac{1}{-30} = 0.05 - 0.0333 = 0.0167$, so $v = +90$ cm$v>0$: real image inside the glass, 90 cm from P.
(b) Same surface, but a converging beam heading for a point 20 cm to the right of P, so a virtual object with $u=+20$: $\dfrac{1.5}{v} = 0.05 - \dfrac1{20} = 0$, so $v = \infty$The refracted rays become parallel.
(c) Concave towards the light, $R=-10$, $u=-30$: $\dfrac{1.5}{v} = \dfrac{0.5}{-10} - \dfrac1{30} = -0.0833$, so $v = -18$ cm$v<0$: a virtual image on the left, 18 cm from P.
Exam traps

• Swapping $n_1$ and $n_2$. $n_1$ is always the side the light comes from.

• Writing $\frac{n_1}{u} + \frac{n_2}{v}$ with Cartesian signs. That is a different convention (P4).

• Forgetting the minus in $-\frac{n_1}{u}$ when $u$ itself is negative. Write it out: $-\frac{1}{(-30)} = +\frac1{30}$.

T8

The same formula from Fermat (optical path route)

Ghatak Ex. 3.3. A 10-mark essay favourite: it links Unit 1 to Unit 2.

{{fig:surface_fermat}}

Same surface: centre C, radius $r$, object O at distance $x$ from P. Pick any point S on the surface, where CS makes angle $\theta$ with the axis. Let Q be any point on the axis, distance $y$ from P, in medium $n_2$. Fermat says: the ray O → S → Q is real if $L = n_1\,OS + n_2\,SQ$ is stationary as S moves, i.e. $dL/d\theta = 0$.

Step A: length OS

$OS^2 = (x+r)^2 + r^2 - 2(x+r)\,r\cos\theta$Cosine rule in triangle OCS: $OC = x+r$, $CS = r$, angle at C is $\theta$.
$\cos\theta \approx 1 - \dfrac{\theta^2}{2}$ gives $OS^2 \approx x^2 + r(x+r)\theta^2$Small angle (M1). Expand: $(x+r)^2 + r^2 - 2(x+r)r = x^2$, and the $\theta^2$ part is $(x+r)r\theta^2$.
$OS \approx x + \dfrac{r(x+r)}{2x}\theta^2 = x + \tfrac12 r^2\left(\dfrac1r + \dfrac1x\right)\theta^2$Binomial shortcut (M4): $\sqrt{x^2+k\theta^2}\approx x + \frac{k\theta^2}{2x}$.

Step B: length SQ

$SQ \approx y - \tfrac12 r^2\left(\dfrac1r - \dfrac1y\right)\theta^2$Same steps in triangle SCQ, where $CQ = y - r$ and the angle at C is $180^\circ - \theta$, so the cosine changes sign.

Step C: total optical path

$L = n_1 OS + n_2 SQ \approx (n_1x + n_2y) + \tfrac12 r^2\left[\dfrac{n_1}{x} + \dfrac{n_2}{y} - \dfrac{n_2-n_1}{r}\right]\theta^2$Add the two, collect the $\theta^2$ terms (Ghatak 3.7).

Step D: apply Fermat

$\dfrac{dL}{d\theta} = r^2\left[\dfrac{n_1}{x} + \dfrac{n_2}{y} - \dfrac{n_2-n_1}{r}\right]\theta = 0$Stationary path (Ghatak 3.8).
Either $\theta = 0$ (the ray along the axis, which always works), or the bracket is zeroFor all rays from O to reach Q, it must hold for every $\theta$, so the bracket must be 0.
$\dfrac{n_1}{x} + \dfrac{n_2}{y_0} = \dfrac{n_2-n_1}{r}$The special distance $y = y_0$ where this happens is the image I (Ghatak 3.9). Signs $u=-x$, $v=y_0$, $R=r$ give $\frac{n_2}{v}-\frac{n_1}{u}=\frac{n_2-n_1}{R}$ again.
The ideaAt the image, $L$ does not depend on $\theta$ (to order $\theta^2$). Every paraxial ray takes the same time to get from O to I. That is the "perfect imaging" condition of T5.

Minimum or maximum? (Ghatak, after eq. 3.10)

$\dfrac{d^2L}{d\theta^2} = r^2\left[\dfrac{n_1}{x} + \dfrac{n_2}{y} - \dfrac{n_2-n_1}{r}\right] = r^2 n_2\left(\dfrac1y - \dfrac1{y_0}\right)$Replace $\frac{n_2-n_1}{r}-\frac{n_1}{x}$ by $\frac{n_2}{y_0}$ using step D.
Q beyond the image ($y > y_0$): negative, so a maximum. Q before it ($y < y_0$): positive, so a minimum. At $y = y_0$: constant (stationary)Second-derivative test (M3). A real example where the actual ray takes a maximum time.

Virtual images by Fermat (Ghatak Ex. 3.4)

If the refracted rays spread out, they only appear to come from a point I on the object's side. The condition is that $n_1\,OS - n_2\,SI$ is the same for all S.

Take any far point B on a refracted ray. $L = n_1 OS + n_2 SB$The real optical path from O to B.
$L = (n_1 OS - n_2 SI) + n_2(IS + SB)$Add and subtract $n_2\,SI$.
If the first bracket is the same for all S, Fermat needs only $IS + SB$ stationaryA sum of two lengths is least when I, S, B are on one straight line.
So each refracted ray lies on a straight line through I: it appears to come from IThis is what a virtual image means. A minus sign in the optical path means "counted backwards".
Check yourself
In the Fermat route, why must the bracket be zero, and not just $\theta = 0$?
$\theta=0$ is only the ray along the axis. For an image, rays at all small angles must reach the same point, so $dL/d\theta=0$ must hold for every $\theta$. That forces the bracket to vanish.

T9

Foci of one surface, and surfaces in a row

Two small ideas we need before lenses.

Focal lengths of a single surface

First focus $F_1$: the object point whose image is at infinity. Put $v = \infty$, $u = f_1$: $\;-\dfrac{n_1}{f_1} = \dfrac{n_2-n_1}{R}$, so $f_1 = -\dfrac{n_1R}{n_2-n_1}$Rays from $F_1$ come out parallel.
Second focus $F_2$: the image of an object at infinity. Put $u=-\infty$, $v=f_2$: $\;\dfrac{n_2}{f_2} = \dfrac{n_2-n_1}{R}$, so $f_2 = \dfrac{n_2R}{n_2-n_1}$Parallel rays meet at $F_2$.
$\dfrac{f_2}{f_1} = -\dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$Divide. For one surface $|f_1| \ne |f_2|$: the two focal lengths are not equal, unlike a lens in air.

Example: $n_1=1$, $n_2=1.5$, $R=+10$ cm: $f_1 = -20$ cm, $f_2 = +30$ cm.

Surfaces in a row: the image becomes the next object

The idea (Ghatak Ex. 4.1) When light meets several surfaces, solve them one at a time. The image made by surface 1 is the object for surface 2. Measure the new $u$ from surface 2's own pole.
Result · virtual object If rays heading towards an image are caught by the next surface before they meet, that image lies to the right of the next surface. Then it acts as a virtual object with $u > 0$.
Glass rod, $n=1.5$, first surface $R_1 = +15$ cm, object at $u=-40$ cm: $\dfrac{1.5}{v} = \dfrac{0.5}{15} - \dfrac{1}{40} = 0.00833$, so $v = +180$ cmSurface 1 alone would form an image 180 cm inside the glass.
Second surface 30 cm further on, $R_2=-25$ cm. The image is $180-30 = 150$ cm to its right, so $u = +150$ (virtual object)The rays meet surface 2 before they can converge.
Now glass → air: $\dfrac{1}{v} - \dfrac{1.5}{150} = \dfrac{1-1.5}{-25} = 0.02$, so $v = +33.3$ cmFinal real image 33.3 cm beyond the second surface.

In the model paper: Q13, Q12. This "image becomes object" idea is exactly how the lens formula is built in T11.

T10

Reflection at a spherical mirror

Same method, with the law of reflection instead of Snell. Two routes: geometry, and Fermat.

{{fig:mirror_geometry}}

Geometry route (Ghatak §4.3)

$\phi_1 = \beta - \alpha_1$Triangle OAC. $\beta$ is exterior at C, so $\beta = \alpha_1 + \phi_1$.
$\phi_2 = \alpha_2 - \beta$Triangle CAI. $\alpha_2$ is exterior at I, so $\alpha_2 = \beta + \phi_2$.
$\phi_1 = \phi_2$ gives $2\beta = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2$Law of reflection, $i = r$.
$\dfrac2r = \dfrac1x + \dfrac1y$Small angles: $\beta\approx\frac hr$, $\alpha_1\approx\frac hx$, $\alpha_2\approx\frac hy$, then cancel $h$ (Ghatak 4.6).
Signs: O, C and I are all on the left, so $u=-x$, $v=-y$, $R=-r$:   $\dfrac1u + \dfrac1v = \dfrac2R$Ghatak 4.7. With $f = R/2$ this is the Plus Two formula $\frac1v + \frac1u = \frac1f$.
Result · mirror equation $$\frac1u + \frac1v = \frac2R = \frac1f$$

Concave mirror: $R<0$, $f<0$. Convex mirror: $R>0$, $f>0$.

Shortcut: a mirror is "refraction with $n_2 = -n_1$" (Ghatak §4.3)

Put $n_2 = -n_1$ in the surface formula:

$$\frac{-n_1}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{-2n_1}{R}$$

Divide by $-n_1$: $\;\dfrac1v + \dfrac1u = \dfrac2R$. The mirror equation.

Why it works: Snell's law $n_1\sin\phi_1 = -n_1\sin\phi_2$ gives $\phi_2 = -\phi_1$, i.e. the light turns back with equal angle: reflection.

{{fig:concave_mirror_virtual}}

Fermat route: object closer than $R/2$ (PYQ N20 Q25)

{{fig:mirror_fermat}}

Concave mirror, centre C at distance $r$, object O at distance $x < r/2$ from P. The image Q is virtual, behind the mirror, at distance $y$. S is a point on the mirror where CS makes angle $\theta$ with the axis.

$OS \approx x + \tfrac12 r^2\left(\dfrac1x - \dfrac1r\right)\theta^2$Cosine rule in triangle OCS ($CO = r - x$), then the small-angle and binomial steps of T8.
$SQ \approx y + \tfrac12 r^2\left(\dfrac1y + \dfrac1r\right)\theta^2$Triangle QCS with $CQ = r + y$.
Virtual image, so use $L = OS - SQ \approx (x - y) + \tfrac12 r^2\left[\dfrac1x - \dfrac1y - \dfrac2r\right]\theta^2$As in T8 (virtual images): the reflected ray only appears to come from Q, so SQ is counted backwards.
$\dfrac{dL}{d\theta} = 0$ for all $\theta$:   $\dfrac1x - \dfrac1y = \dfrac2r$The bracket must vanish.
Signs $u=-x$, $v=+y$ (behind), $R=-r$:   $\dfrac1u + \dfrac1v = \dfrac2R$Check: $-\frac1x + \frac1y = -\frac2r$. ✓ The same mirror equation.

Note: $\frac1y = \frac1x - \frac2r > 0$ only when $x < \frac r2$. That is exactly why the image is behind the mirror (virtual) when the object is inside the focus.

Exam traps

• Mirror: $\frac1v \mathbf{+} \frac1u$. Lens: $\frac1v \mathbf{-} \frac1u$. Do not mix them.

• A concave mirror has $R<0$ and $f<0$ in this convention.

• Mirror magnification is $m = -v/u$ (T15), lens magnification is $m = +v/u$.

Check yourself
1. Concave mirror, $R = -60$ cm, object at $u=-80$ cm. Image?
$\frac1v = \frac2R - \frac1u = -\frac1{30} + \frac1{80} = \frac{-8+3}{240}$, so $v = -48$ cm: real, in front of the mirror.
2. Same mirror, object at $u=-20$ cm?
$\frac1v = -\frac1{30}+\frac1{20} = \frac1{60}$, so $v=+60$ cm: virtual, behind the mirror.
