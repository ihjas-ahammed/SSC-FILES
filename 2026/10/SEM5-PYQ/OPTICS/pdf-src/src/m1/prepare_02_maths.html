M1

Radians and the small-angle trick

Used in every curved-surface derivation. It turns hard trigonometry into simple fractions.

Remind me · Plus One

An angle in radians = arc length ÷ radius. A full turn is $2\pi$ rad = 360°, so $1^\circ = \pi/180 \approx 0.0175$ rad.

In a right triangle:

$$\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}},\qquad \tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}$$
{{fig:small_angle}}
Result · small-angle approximation If $\theta$ is small (below about 10°, i.e. 0.17 rad) and in radians: $$\sin\theta \approx \theta,\qquad \tan\theta\approx\theta,\qquad \cos\theta \approx 1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2}$$
How we use it A ray hits a surface at height $h$, at distance $x$ along the axis. Its angle with the axis is $\alpha$, with $\tan\alpha = h/x$. For small angles we just write $$\alpha \approx \frac{h}{x}.$$ Nearly every angle in T7, T10 and T15 is written this way.
Exam trapNever use degrees: $\sin 5^\circ \ne 5$. And the trick works only for rays close to the axis. That condition is called the paraxial approximation (T6).
Check yourself
1. A ray hits a surface 2 cm above the axis, at a point 40 cm from the object. Its angle with the axis?
$\alpha \approx 2/40 = 0.05$ rad (about 2.9°).
M2

Geometry tools

Four facts from Class 9–10 that every derivation uses.

1. Pythagoras

A right triangle with sides $a$, $b$ has hypotenuse $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$. Light going from height $h$ down to a point at horizontal distance $x$ travels $\sqrt{x^2+h^2}$.

2. Exterior angle rule

{{fig:exterior_angle}}

Why: $a+b+(\text{third angle}) = 180^\circ$, and $c + (\text{third angle}) = 180^\circ$ because they form a straight line. So $c = a+b$.

3. Similar triangles

Two triangles with the same angles have the same shape, so their sides are in the same ratio. If a small triangle has height $h$ and base $u$, and a similar big one has height $h'$ and base $v$: $$\frac{h'}{h} = \frac{v}{u}$$ This gives every magnification formula (T14, T15).

4. Sagitta: how much a curve bulges

{{fig:sagitta}}
$R^2 = h^2 + (R-s)^2$Pythagoras in the right triangle with sides $h$, $R-s$ and hypotenuse $R$.
$R^2 = h^2 + R^2 - 2Rs + s^2$Expand the bracket.
$2Rs \approx h^2$, so $s \approx \dfrac{h^2}{2R}$Cancel $R^2$. $s$ is tiny, so $s^2$ is even tinier: drop it.
Check yourself
1. Triangle angles 50° and 60°. The exterior angle next to the third angle?
$50^\circ+60^\circ = 110^\circ$.
2. A lens surface with $R = 50$ cm, measured 1 cm from the axis. How much does it bulge?
$s \approx 1^2/(2\times50) = 0.01$ cm. Very small, which is why we can treat the surface as flat when we measure distances.
M3

Slope zero: finding a stationary point

The calculus inside Fermat's principle. You need only one idea: at the bottom of a valley the ground is flat.

Remind me · Plus One / Plus Two maths

The derivative $\dfrac{dL}{dx}$ is the slope of the graph of $L$ against $x$: how fast $L$ changes when $x$ changes a little.

Chain rule: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sqrt{g(x)} = \dfrac{g'(x)}{2\sqrt{g(x)}}$.

{{fig:stationary_graphs}}
Derivatives from zero (if you never learnt them)

Slope = (change in $L$) ÷ (change in $x$), for a very tiny change. Try $L = x^2$ at $x = 3$: $L(3.01) - L(3) = 9.0601 - 9 = 0.0601$. Divide by the change $0.01$: slope $\approx 6.01 \approx 6$.

The rule $\dfrac{d}{dx}x^n = n\,x^{n-1}$ gives $2x = 6$ at once. A constant has slope 0. A sum: add the slopes.

Chain rule in words: slope of the outside × slope of the inside. For $\sqrt{g}$: outside slope $\frac{1}{2\sqrt g}$, inside slope $g'$.

The idea Where the graph is flat, a tiny change in $x$ makes almost no change in $L$. This is a stationary point. To find it, set $\dfrac{dL}{dx} = 0$. It can be a minimum, a maximum or a flat shelf. The second derivative tells which: $L''>0$ means a valley (minimum), $L''<0$ a hilltop (maximum).

The one derivative you must know

$\dfrac{d}{dx}\sqrt{x^2+a^2} = \dfrac{2x}{2\sqrt{x^2+a^2}} = \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+a^2}}$Chain rule with $g = x^2+a^2$, $g' = 2x$. The 2s cancel.
$\dfrac{d}{dx}\sqrt{(d-x)^2+b^2} = \dfrac{-(d-x)}{\sqrt{(d-x)^2+b^2}}$Now $g' = 2(d-x)\times(-1)$. Note the minus: as $x$ grows, $d-x$ shrinks.
Read it as a sine In a right triangle with horizontal side $x$ and vertical side $a$, the hypotenuse is $\sqrt{x^2+a^2}$. So $\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+a^2}}$ is opposite/hypotenuse for the angle measured from the vertical, which is the normal. That is $\sin\theta$. This is how Snell's law appears in T3.
Check yourself
1. Where is $L(x) = (x-3)^2 + 5$ stationary? Minimum or maximum?
$L' = 2(x-3) = 0$ gives $x = 3$. $L'' = 2 > 0$, so it is a minimum (valley), with $L = 5$.
2. Derivative of $\sqrt{x^2+16}$ at $x=3$?
$3/\sqrt{25} = 0.6$.
M4

The binomial shortcut

Square roots of "1 + something tiny". Used in the Fermat route for curved surfaces (T8, T10).

Result For small $\varepsilon$: $$(1+\varepsilon)^n \approx 1 + n\varepsilon,\qquad\text{so}\qquad \sqrt{1+\varepsilon}\approx 1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}$$

Why it works: $(1+\varepsilon)^2 = 1 + 2\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2 \approx 1 + 2\varepsilon$, because $\varepsilon^2$ is tiny. Going backwards, $\sqrt{1+2\varepsilon}\approx 1+\varepsilon$, which is the same as $\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}\approx1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}$.

Check with numbers: $\sqrt{1.02} = 1.00995\ldots$ and $1 + 0.02/2 = 1.01$Almost the same. The error is about $\varepsilon^2/8$.
Typical use: $\sqrt{x^2 + k\theta^2} = x\sqrt{1 + \dfrac{k\theta^2}{x^2}} \approx x + \dfrac{k\theta^2}{2x}$Take the big part $x^2$ outside the root. What is left is $1 + $ (tiny), then use the shortcut.
Remind meKeep terms up to $\theta^2$ and drop $\theta^4$ and higher. That is the whole "paraxial" game.
M5

Ellipse and parabola: the focus property

Two curves whose shape makes all light paths equal. Used in T5.

Remind me · Plus One conic sections

Ellipse: the set of points P whose distances to two fixed points $F_1$, $F_2$ (the foci) add up to a constant: $PF_1 + PF_2 = 2a$. Draw it with two pins and a loop of string.

Parabola: the set of points equally far from one point (the focus S) and one line (the directrix).

Numbers make it real Take an ellipse with $2a = 10$ and foci 8 apart. At the end of the long axis: distances 1 and 9, sum 10. At the top: distances 5 and 5, sum 10. The split changes but the total stays the same.
Exam trapIt is the sum of the two distances that is constant. Each distance on its own changes from point to point.
M6

Working with reciprocals

How to solve $\frac1v - \frac1u = \frac1f$ without mistakes.

Make the unknown alone: $\dfrac1v = \dfrac1f + \dfrac1u$Move $-\frac1u$ to the other side. It becomes $+\frac1u$.
Put numbers with signs: $f = +20$, $u = -30$ gives $\dfrac1v = \dfrac1{20} + \dfrac1{-30} = \dfrac1{20} - \dfrac1{30}$The sign of $u$ goes inside the fraction.
Common denominator: $\dfrac{3}{60} - \dfrac{2}{60} = \dfrac{1}{60}$LCM of 20 and 30 is 60.
Flip only at the end: $v = +60$ cmCombine everything into one fraction first, then flip.
Exam trap$\frac1v = \frac1{20} - \frac1{30}$ does not give $v = 20 - 30$. Never flip the fractions one by one.
Remind meOn a calculator use decimals: $1/20 - 1/30 = 0.05 - 0.0333 = 0.01667$, and $1/0.01667 = 60$.
Check yourself
Solve $\frac1v = \frac{1}{-90} + \frac{1}{50}$.
$\frac{-5+9}{450} = \frac{4}{450}$, so $v = 112.5$.
