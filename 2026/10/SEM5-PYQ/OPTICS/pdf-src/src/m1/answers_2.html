Know firstT7T6P4M1M2
{{fig:refraction_surface}}

A spherical surface of radius $r$ (centre C) separates media of indices $n_1$ and $n_2$. A point object O on the axis is at distance $x$ from the pole P. A ray OA meets the surface at A (height $h$) and, after refraction, cuts the axis at I, at distance $y$ from P. CA is the normal at A. Laws of reflection from Fermat's principle.

Know firstT3T2M2
{{fig:fermat_reflection}}
Light goes from A to B after reflection at a point P of the plane mirror. Let A′ be the image of A in the mirror (AA′ ⟂ mirror, bisected by it)Construction.

By Fermat's principle the actual path is stationary (here, least). $A'P+PB$ is least when A′, P, B are collinearAny other point Q gives $A'Q+QB > A'B$.
The line A′B lies in the plane containing A, A′, B. Since AA′ is parallel to the normal at P, the normal also lies in this planeSo the incident ray, reflected ray and normal are coplanar (first law).

Snell's law from Fermat's principle.

{{fig:fermat_refraction}}

A (in medium $n_1$) is at height $h_1$ and B (in $n_2$) at depth $h_2$ from the plane surface. Their feet are a distance $d$ apart. The ray crosses the surface at P, a distance $x$ from the foot of A.

Equal optical paths ⇒ perfect image; spherical surface perfect only for paraxial rays.

Know firstT5T8T2
{{fig:surface_optical_path}}

A point I is a perfect image of O if every ray leaving O passes through I. By Fermat's principle, each such ray must have a stationary optical path. If many neighbouring paths all reach I, the optical path must be the same for all of them:

$$n_1\,OA + n_2\,AI = n_1\,OP + n_2\,PI$$

for every point A of the surface.

Then all the light from O arrives at I in step (same time), giving a sharp image. Surfaces that satisfy this exactly are Cartesian ovals: a paraboloid for parallel light, an ellipsoid for its two foci.

For a spherical surface (T8) the optical path from O through a point at angle $\theta$ is

$$L \approx (n_1x+n_2y) + \tfrac12r^2\Big[\cdots\Big]\theta^2 + (\theta^4\text{ terms})$$

where the bracket is $\left[\frac{n_1}{x}+\frac{n_2}{y}-\frac{n_2-n_1}{r}\right]$.

At the image the $\theta^2$ term vanishes, but the $\theta^4$ terms do not. So the optical paths are equal only when $\theta$ is small, i.e. for paraxial rays. Rays far from the axis do not meet at I: spherical aberration.

Derive Newton's formula for a thin lens.

Know firstT14T13M2P4
{{fig:newton}}

A thin convex lens of focal length $f$ is in air. An object of height $h$ is at distance $x_1$ from $F_1$; its image, of height $h'$, is at distance $x_2$ from $F_2$.

Ray through $F_1$ emerges parallel at the height of the image top. Similar triangles at $F_1$: $\dfrac{|h|}{|x_1|} = \dfrac{|h'|}{f}$
Ray parallel to the axis passes through $F_2$. Similar triangles at $F_2$: $\dfrac{|h|}{f} = \dfrac{|h'|}{|x_2|}$
Equating the two values of $|h'|/|h|$:   $\dfrac f{|x_1|} = \dfrac{|x_2|}f$, so $|x_1||x_2| = f^2$
With signs, the object is left of $F_1$ ($x_1<0$) and the image right of $F_2$ ($x_2>0$): $x_1x_2 = -f^2$. In general, $x_1x_2 = f_1f_2$
$x_1x_2 = f_1f_2 = -f^2$   (magnitudes: $|x_1||x_2| = f^2$)

Also, from steps 1–2: $m = \dfrac{h'}{h} = \dfrac{f}{x_1} = -\dfrac{x_2}{f}$.
