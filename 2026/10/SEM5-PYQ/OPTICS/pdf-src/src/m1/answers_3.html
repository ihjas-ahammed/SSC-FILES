Fermat's principle → refraction at a spherical surface (optical path method); maximum or minimum?

Know firstT2T8T7M1M3M4P4

1. Fermat's principle

The optical path of a ray from A to B is $L = \int_A^B n\,ds = c\tau$, where $\tau$ is the time taken. Fermat's principle states that the actual ray is the path for which the optical path is stationary with respect to small variations of the path, $\delta\int n\,ds = 0$. It may be a minimum, a maximum or constant.

2. Set-up

{{fig:surface_fermat}}

A spherical surface, centre C, radius $r$, separates media $n_1$ and $n_2$. O is a point object at distance $x$ from the pole P. S is a point on the surface with $\angle SCP = \theta$. Q is a point on the axis in medium 2, at distance $y$ from P.

3. Optical path O → S → Q

$OS^2 = (x+r)^2 + r^2 - 2r(x+r)\cos\theta$Cosine rule in triangle OCS.
With $\cos\theta\approx1-\frac{\theta^2}2$ (paraxial): $OS^2\approx x^2 + r(x+r)\theta^2$
$OS\approx x + \tfrac12r^2\left(\dfrac1r+\dfrac1x\right)\theta^2$Binomial approximation.
Similarly, in triangle SCQ ($CQ = y-r$, angle at C $=\pi-\theta$): $SQ \approx y - \tfrac12r^2\left(\dfrac1r - \dfrac1y\right)\theta^2$
$L = n_1OS + n_2SQ \approx (n_1x+n_2y) + \tfrac12r^2\left[\dfrac{n_1}x+\dfrac{n_2}y-\dfrac{n_2-n_1}r\right]\theta^2$

4. Applying Fermat's principle

$\dfrac{dL}{d\theta} = r^2\left[\dfrac{n_1}x+\dfrac{n_2}y-\dfrac{n_2-n_1}r\right]\theta = 0$
$\theta = 0$ gives only the axial ray OPQ, which passes undeviated (normal incidence). For all paraxial rays from O to reach the same point, the bracket must vanish
$\dfrac{n_1}{x}+\dfrac{n_2}{y_0} = \dfrac{n_2-n_1}{r}$This fixes the image distance $y_0$. All paraxial rays take equal time from O to I.
Signs $u = -x$, $v=y_0$, $R = r$:   $\dfrac{n_2}{v}-\dfrac{n_1}{u} = \dfrac{n_2-n_1}{R}$

5. Maximum or minimum?

$\dfrac{d^2L}{d\theta^2} = r^2\left[\dfrac{n_1}x+\dfrac{n_2}y-\dfrac{n_2-n_1}r\right]$
Using the image condition, $\frac{n_2-n_1}{r}-\frac{n_1}{x} = \frac{n_2}{y_0}$:   $\dfrac{d^2L}{d\theta^2} = r^2n_2\left(\dfrac1y-\dfrac1{y_0}\right)$
Q beyond the image ($y>y_0$): $\frac{d^2L}{d\theta^2}<0$, so the actual ray OPQ is a path of maximum optical path
Q between P and I ($y0$, a minimum
Q at I ($y=y_0$): $L$ is independent of $\theta$ (to this order), so it is stationary, neither a maximum nor a minimum

Physical check: $n_1OP + n_2PI = n_1OS + n_2SI$. For Q beyond I, $n_1OP + n_2PQ = n_1OS + n_2(SI + IQ) > n_1OS + n_2SQ$, because $SI + IQ > SQ$ (triangle inequality). The axial path is longer than its neighbours: a maximum.

6. Conclusion

Fermat's principle gives the refraction formula without using Snell's law. It also shows that the real ray can be a path of maximum time, so the principle must be stated as "stationary", not "least" time.

Thin lens formula and lens-maker's formula; assumptions; converging vs diverging.

Know firstT11T7T9T12P4

1. Refraction at one surface

For a spherical surface separating $n_1$ (incident side) and $n_2$: $\dfrac{n_2}{v}-\dfrac{n_1}{u}=\dfrac{n_2-n_1}{R}$ (Cartesian signs, paraxial rays).

2. Assumptions

  • The lens is thin: its thickness is negligible, so all distances are measured from the centre P.
  • Rays are paraxial.
  • The lens (index $n_2$) has the same medium (index $n_1$) on both sides.
{{fig:thin_lens_two_surfaces}}

3. Derivation

First surface (radius $R_1$), from $n_1$ into $n_2$:   $\dfrac{n_2}{v'}-\dfrac{n_1}{u}=\dfrac{n_2-n_1}{R_1}$If the second surface were absent, the image would be at $v'$.
This image acts as the object for the second surface (radius $R_2$), from $n_2$ into $n_1$:   $\dfrac{n_1}{v}-\dfrac{n_2}{v'}=\dfrac{n_1-n_2}{R_2}$Thin lens, so $v'$ is measured from the same point.
Adding: $\dfrac{n_1}{v}-\dfrac{n_1}{u} = (n_2-n_1)\left(\dfrac1{R_1}-\dfrac1{R_2}\right)$
Dividing by $n_1$, with $n = n_2/n_1$:   $\dfrac1v-\dfrac1u = (n-1)\left(\dfrac1{R_1}-\dfrac1{R_2}\right)$
For $u\to-\infty$ the image is at $F_2$, $v=f$:   $\dfrac1f = (n-1)\left(\dfrac1{R_1}-\dfrac1{R_2}\right)$Lens-maker's formula.
$\dfrac1v-\dfrac1u=\dfrac1f$,   $\dfrac1f=(n-1)\left(\dfrac1{R_1}-\dfrac1{R_2}\right)$

4. Converging or diverging

  • $f>0$: converging; $f<0$: diverging.
  • Lens denser than its surroundings ($n>1$): biconvex ($R_1>0$, $R_2<0$) gives $f>0$, converging; biconcave gives $f<0$, diverging.
  • Lens rarer than its surroundings ($n<1$): the roles reverse. A biconvex lens diverges and a biconcave lens converges (e.g. an air bubble in water).

Example: $n = 1.5$, $R_1 = 20$ cm, $R_2 = -20$ cm in air: $\frac1f = 0.5\times\frac{2}{20}$, so $f = 20$ cm. In water ($n = 1.128$) $f \approx 78$ cm, still converging.

Principal foci; $f_1$, $f_2$ for different media; $f_2=-f_1$; lens in a denser medium.

Know firstT12T11T9T7

1. Definitions

First principal focus $F_1$: the axial point such that rays from it (or directed towards it) emerge parallel to the axis; its image is at infinity. Second principal focus $F_2$: the axial point at which rays incident parallel to the axis meet (or from which they appear to diverge) after refraction. $f_1$ and $f_2$ are their distances from the lens, with sign.

{{fig:principal_foci}}

2. Lens between different media

Medium $n_1$ on the left, lens of index $n_2$, medium $n_3$ on the right.

Surface 1: $\dfrac{n_2}{v'}-\dfrac{n_1}{u}=\dfrac{n_2-n_1}{R_1}$
Surface 2 (from $n_2$ to $n_3$): $\dfrac{n_3}{v}-\dfrac{n_2}{v'}=\dfrac{n_3-n_2}{R_2}$
Add: $\dfrac{n_3}{v}-\dfrac{n_1}{u} = \dfrac{n_2-n_1}{R_1}+\dfrac{n_3-n_2}{R_2}\equiv D$
$F_1$: $v=\infty$, $u=f_1$:   $\dfrac1{f_1} = -\dfrac{1}{n_1}\left[\dfrac{n_2-n_1}{R_1}+\dfrac{n_3-n_2}{R_2}\right]$
$F_2$: $u=-\infty$, $v=f_2$:   $\dfrac1{f_2} = \dfrac{1}{n_3}\left[\dfrac{n_2-n_1}{R_1}+\dfrac{n_3-n_2}{R_2}\right]$
Hence $\dfrac{f_2}{f_1} = -\dfrac{n_3}{n_1}$

3. Same medium on both sides

With $n_3 = n_1$: $\dfrac{f_2}{f_1} = -1$, so $f_2 = -f_1 = f$, where $\dfrac1f = \left(\dfrac{n_2}{n_1}-1\right)\left(\dfrac1{R_1}-\dfrac1{R_2}\right)$. The two foci are equidistant from the lens, on opposite sides. For a converging lens $f_1<0$, $f_2>0$; for a diverging lens $f_1>0$, $f_2<0$.

4. Biconvex lens in a denser medium

If $n_1 > n_2$ (e.g. glass of 1.5 in a liquid of 1.6), then $n = n_2/n_1 < 1$, so $n-1<0$. With $\frac1{R_1}-\frac1{R_2}>0$, $f$ becomes negative: the biconvex lens diverges. Example: $n = 1.5/1.6 = 0.9375$, so the lens is a weak diverging lens.

Lateral magnification: single surface, thin lens, Newtonian form, example.

Know firstT15T14T7T11M1M2

1. Definition

Lateral magnification $m = h'/h$: the ratio of image height to object height, both measured perpendicular to the axis with sign. $m>0$ erect, $m<0$ inverted.

2. Single refracting surface

{{fig:magnification_surface}}
Take the ray from the top of the object to the pole P. At P the normal to the surface is the axis itself. Angles of incidence and refraction: $\theta_1\approx\dfrac{h}{-u}$, $\theta_2\approx\dfrac{-h'}{v}$Paraxial; $-u$ and $-h'$ are positive.
Snell (small angles): $n_1\theta_1 = n_2\theta_2$, so $n_1\dfrac{h}{-u} = n_2\dfrac{-h'}{v}$
$m = \dfrac{h'}{h} = \dfrac{n_1v}{n_2u}$

3. Thin lens as two surfaces

First surface: $m_1 = \dfrac{n_1v'}{n_2u}$; second surface: $m_2 = \dfrac{n_2v}{n_1v'}$
Total: $m = m_1m_2 = \dfrac vu$

4. In terms of distances from the foci

From the similar triangles of Newton's formula (with $x_1$ from $F_1$, $x_2$ from $F_2$):

$$m = -\frac{f_1}{x_1} = -\frac{x_2}{f_2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad m = \frac{f}{x_1} = -\frac{x_2}{f}\ \text{(lens in air)}$$

5. Numerical example

Surface: air to glass ($n=1.5$), $R = +10$ cm, $u = -30$ cm gives $v = +90$ cm, so $m = \dfrac{1\times90}{1.5\times(-30)} = -2$: inverted, twice as big.

Lens: $f = 75$ cm, $u = -100$ cm gives $v = +300$ cm, so $m = v/u = -3$. With $x_1 = -25$ cm: $m = f/x_1 = -3$ ✓; with $x_2 = 225$ cm: $m = -x_2/f = -3$ ✓.
