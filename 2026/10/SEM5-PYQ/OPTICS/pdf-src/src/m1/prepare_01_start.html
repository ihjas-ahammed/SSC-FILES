The whole module in one story

The idea

1. Light goes from A to B along a path where the travel time is "stationary": a tiny change of path does not change the time (T1–T2).

2. From that one rule we get three facts: light goes straight, angle in = angle out at a mirror, and Snell's law at a boundary (T3–T5).

3. Apply Snell's law at one curved glass surface. For rays close to the axis, all rays from a point O meet again at one point I. This gives one formula (T6–T9).

4. A mirror is the same calculation. Only the formula's details change (T10).

5. A lens is two curved surfaces. Use the formula twice to get the lens formula and the focal length (T11–T13).

6. Measure distances from the foci instead of the lens, and you get Newton's formula. Compare image size with object size to get magnification (T14–T15).

So the module is one principle (Fermat), one approximation (small angles) and one sign rule, used again and again.

P1

Light, rays and refractive index

What a "ray" is, and why light slows down in glass.

Remind me · Class 10 / Plus Two Ch 9
  • A ray is a line that shows which way light energy travels. A beam is a bundle of rays.
  • Light travels at $c = 3\times10^8$ m/s in vacuum. Inside a material it is slower, with speed $v$.
  • Refractive index $n = \dfrac{c}{v}$. Bigger $n$ means slower light. Air: $n\approx1$. Water: 1.33. Glass: about 1.5.
  • A material with larger $n$ is called optically denser.
The idea Think of $n$ as a "slowness number". In glass ($n=1.5$) light is 1.5 times slower than in vacuum, so 1 cm of glass takes as long to cross as 1.5 cm of vacuum.
Speed in glass: $v = \dfrac{c}{n} = \dfrac{3\times10^8}{1.5} = 2\times10^8$ m/sRearranged $n = c/v$.
Time to cross 2 cm of glass: $t = \dfrac{0.02}{2\times10^8} = 10^{-10}$ stime = distance ÷ speed.
Vacuum distance in the same time: $c\,t = 3\times10^8 \times 10^{-10} = 0.03$ m $= 3$ cm $= n\times 2$ cmSo "n × length" measures time, written as a distance. This is the optical path of T1.
Check yourself
1. Speed of light in water ($n=1.33$)?
$v = 3\times10^8/1.33 \approx 2.26\times10^8$ m/s.
2. Which is slower for light: a medium with $n=1.6$ or $n=1.4$?
$n=1.6$. Bigger $n$ means slower light.
P2

Laws of reflection and refraction

The two laws that Fermat's principle will later explain.

Remind me · Class 10

Reflection: (1) the incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal lie in one plane; (2) angle of incidence = angle of reflection, $i = r$.

Refraction (Snell's law): (1) the incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal lie in one plane; (2) $n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2$.

The normal is the line at 90° to the surface at the point where the ray hits it.

In Class 10 you wrote Snell's law as $\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = n_{21} = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$. Cross-multiply and you get $n_1\sin i = n_2 \sin r$: the same law.

{{fig:snell_basic}}
The idea Going into a slower material (bigger $n$), light bends towards the normal. Coming out into a faster one, it bends away. A ray along the normal ($\theta_1=0$) does not bend at all.
Exam trapAll angles are measured from the normal, never from the surface. If a question gives the angle with the surface, subtract it from 90° first.
Check yourself
1. Light goes from air into glass ($n=1.5$) at 30° to the normal. Find the refracted angle.
$\sin\theta_2 = \dfrac{1\times\sin30^\circ}{1.5} = \dfrac{0.5}{1.5} = 0.333$, so $\theta_2 \approx 19.5^\circ$. It bends towards the normal, as expected.
2. A ray makes 60° with a mirror's surface. What is the angle of incidence?
$90^\circ - 60^\circ = 30^\circ$.

Remind me · real vs virtual image

Real image: the rays really meet there. You can catch it on a screen. Usually upside down.

Virtual image: the rays only seem to come from there when you trace them backwards (dashed lines). No screen can catch it, but your eye sees it. Example: your face in a plane mirror.

P4

The sign rule (Cartesian convention)

The source of most lost marks. Learn it once, properly.

The idea Treat the axis like a number line. Put zero at the pole (or at the lens). Draw light going left to right. Then each distance is a coordinate: its sign tells you which side it is on.
{{fig:sign_line}}
Result · the four rules (Ghatak §4.2.1 = NCERT)
  1. Measure all distances from the pole P (or the centre of the lens).
  2. Distances in the direction the light travels (to the right) are +. Against it (to the left) are −.
  3. Heights above the axis are +. Below are −.
  4. $R$ is + if the centre C is to the right of P, and − if C is to the left.

So a real object (on the left, where light starts) always has $u$ negative.

Exam trap

Write each value with its sign before you put it in a formula. Write $u = -30$, not "u = 30". The formula already knows the directions. Your job is only to give it the coordinates.

Some books use a "real is positive" rule. Its formulas look different, for example $$\frac{n_1}{u}+\frac{n_2}{v}=\frac{n_2-n_1}{R}.$$ Do not mix the two. Ghatak and this file use only the Cartesian rule.

Check yourself
1. Lens formula with $u=-30$, $f=+20$. Find $v$.
$\frac1v = \frac1f + \frac1u = \frac1{20} - \frac1{30} = \frac{3-2}{60} = \frac1{60}$, so $v = +60$ cm: real, on the right.
2. A biconvex lens. Signs of $R_1$ (first surface) and $R_2$ (second surface)?
First surface bulges left, so its centre is on the right: $R_1>0$. Second surface bulges right, so its centre is on the left: $R_2<0$.
