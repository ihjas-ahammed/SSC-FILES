39PYQ N19 Q34

Produce circularly and elliptically polarised light; analyse the type of polarisation.

Know firstT10T11T9T8T7

1. Theory: two perpendicular vibrations

If plane polarised light of amplitude $E_0$ falls normally on a crystal plate (optic axis parallel to the faces) with its vibration at angle φ to the optic axis, it splits into E and O vibrations $a = E_0\cos\phi$ and $b = E_0\sin\phi$ that travel at different speeds. They emerge with phase difference $\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mu_o-\mu_e)d$ and combine to $\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}-\frac{2xy}{ab}\cos\delta = \sin^2\delta$.

{{fig:wave_plate}}

2. Production of circularly polarised light

Monochromatic light is made plane polarised by a Nicol N₁ and falls on a quarter-wave plate ($d = \lambda/4|\mu_o-\mu_e|$, δ = π/2) with its optic axis at 45° to the vibration. Then $a = b = E_0/\sqrt2$ and δ = π/2 give $x^2 + y^2 = a^2$: circularly polarised light. (Check: rotating a Nicol behind it shows no change in intensity.)

3. Production of elliptically polarised light

Same arrangement with the optic axis at an angle φ other than 0°, 45°, 90°: $a \ne b$, δ = π/2, giving $\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1$, an ellipse with axes along the optic axis and perpendicular to it.

4. Analysis of the given light

Examine the beam through a rotating Nicol (analyser):

Two positions of zero intensity per rotation → plane polarised
No variation → unpolarised, circular, or a mixture. Insert a QWP before the analyser and rotate the analyser: (a) still no variation → unpolarised; (b) two zeros → circularly polarised (QWP adds π/2 to the existing π/2, giving plane light); (c) variation without zero → mixture of unpolarised and circular.
Variation, but no zero → elliptical, or a mixture of plane and unpolarised. Place the QWP with its optic axis parallel to the analyser's position of maximum intensity, then rotate the analyser: (a) two zeros → elliptically polarised (axes of ellipse along plate axes, δ becomes 0 or π); (b) no zero, maximum at the same position → plane + unpolarised; (c) no zero, maximum shifted → elliptical + unpolarised.

40PYQ N20 Q34 (first two parts)

Distinguish plane, circular, elliptical polarisation; production of linearly polarised light by reflection and refraction.

Know firstT1T4T5T7T8

1. The three kinds

{{fig:states}}
PlaneE vibrates along one fixed line perpendicular to the direction of propagation. Two perpendicular components in phase (δ = 0 or π). CircularE has constant magnitude and its tip rotates uniformly on a circle. Two equal perpendicular components with δ = π/2. EllipticalThe tip of E traces an ellipse; magnitude and direction both vary. Unequal components with δ = π/2, or any components with δ ≠ 0, π/2, π.

Tests: through a rotating analyser, plane light gives two extinctions; circular gives constant intensity; elliptical gives a variation without extinction.

2. By reflection (Brewster)

Unpolarised light reflected from glass at the polarising angle $i_B$, where $\tan i_B = n$, is completely plane polarised with vibrations perpendicular to the plane of incidence. At this angle the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular. For glass ($n = 1.5$), $i_B = 56.3^\circ$.

{{fig:brewster}}

3. By refraction

(a) Pile of plates: the light refracted through one plate at $i_B$ is only partly polarised (it has lost about 15% of its perpendicular component). Through 10–15 plates at $i_B$, the perpendicular component is reflected away at every surface; the transmitted light becomes almost completely plane polarised in the plane of incidence.

(b) Double refraction: in calcite, unpolarised light splits into O and E rays, plane polarised at right angles. Removing one gives plane polarised light. In the Nicol prism, the O-ray is totally reflected at the Canada balsam layer (C ≈ 69°) and absorbed, and the E-ray emerges plane polarised.

{{fig:nicol}}

(c) Dichroism: crystals like tourmaline, or Polaroid sheets, absorb one of the two vibrations strongly and transmit the other.

41

Huygens' theory of double refraction; wave surfaces; three cases of normal incidence.

Know firstT6T5P3

1. Postulates

  1. A wavefront entering a uniaxial crystal produces two wavefronts, ordinary and extraordinary.
  2. For the O-wave the speed is the same in all directions: the secondary wavelet is a sphere.
  3. For the E-wave the speed varies with direction: the wavelet is an ellipsoid of revolution about the optic axis.
  4. Along the optic axis both speeds are equal ($c/\mu_o$): sphere and ellipsoid touch there.
  5. Negative crystal (calcite, $\mu_e < \mu_o$): ellipsoid outside the sphere. Positive crystal (quartz, $\mu_e > \mu_o$): inside.
{{fig:huygens_wavelets}}

2. Construction

From points on the surface reached by the incident wavefront, draw the sphere and ellipsoid reached in time $t$. The plane touching all the spheres is the O-wavefront; the plane touching all the ellipsoids is the E-wavefront. Each ray joins the wavelet's centre to its point of contact.

3. Normal incidence, three orientations

{{fig:huygens_cases}}
(a) Optic axis parallel to the surface (in the plane of incidence): sphere radius $(c/\mu_o)t$, ellipsoid semi-axis $(c/\mu_e)t$ normal to the surface. Both tangent planes are parallel to the surface, and both rays go straight down along the same line, but the wavefronts are separated: the waves travel with different speeds. No separation of rays, but a phase difference: the principle of wave plates
(b) Optic axis perpendicular to the surface: the sphere and ellipsoid touch along the normal, so the two wavefronts coincide: no double refraction; one wave with speed $c/\mu_o$
(c) Optic axis inclined to the surface: the O-ray goes straight along the normal. The E-wavefront is still parallel to the surface, but it touches each ellipsoid at a point that is not straight below its centre, so the E-ray is bent sideways: two separate rays and two images, even at normal incidence

4. Conclusion

Huygens' theory explains the existence of two rays, the absence of double refraction along the optic axis, the deviation of the E-ray at normal incidence, and (with the axis perpendicular to the plane of incidence) the fact that the E-ray then obeys Snell's law with index $\mu_e$. The perpendicular polarisation of the two rays came later from the electromagnetic theory.

42

Resultant of two perpendicular SHMs with phase difference δ.

Know firstT8M2M3

1. Setting up

Two vibrations of the same frequency along perpendicular directions (the E and O components of light):

$$x = a\cos\omega t,\qquad y = b\cos(\omega t - \delta)$$

2. Eliminating time

$\cos\omega t = x/a$, $\sin\omega t = \sqrt{1 - x^2/a^2}$
$\dfrac yb = \cos\omega t\cos\delta + \sin\omega t\sin\delta = \dfrac xa\cos\delta + \sqrt{1-\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}}\sin\delta$
$\left(\dfrac yb - \dfrac xa\cos\delta\right)^2 = \left(1 - \dfrac{x^2}{a^2}\right)\sin^2\delta$
$\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}\cos^2\delta + \dfrac{x^2}{a^2}\sin^2\delta + \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} - \dfrac{2xy}{ab}\cos\delta = \sin^2\delta$
$\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}-\dfrac{2xy}{ab}\cos\delta=\sin^2\delta$: in general an ellipse

3. Special cases

{{fig:lissajous}}
δ = 0: $\left(\frac xa - \frac yb\right)^2 = 0$, i.e. $y = \frac ba x$: a straight line through the origin → plane polarised
δ = π: $y = -\frac ba x$: a straight line the other way → plane polarised
δ = π/2: $\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$: an ellipse with axes along x and y → elliptically polarised; if $a = b$: $x^2 + y^2 = a^2$ → circularly polarised
General δ: an oblique ellipse inscribed in the rectangle $2a \times 2b$ → elliptically polarised. Its axes are tilted by ψ with $\tan2\psi = \dfrac{2ab\cos\delta}{a^2-b^2}$

4. Sense of rotation

At $t = 0$: $(x, y) = (a, b\cos\delta)$; a moment later $y$ increases if $\sin\delta > 0$. So for $0 < \delta < \pi$ the tip turns one way (right-handed in Ghatak's convention, looking at the oncoming wave) and for $\pi < \delta < 2\pi$ the other way (left-handed).

5. Application

A crystal plate provides exactly such components with a controllable δ: a QWP gives δ = π/2 (circular or elliptical light), a HWP gives δ = π (plane light rotated by 2φ). So all three kinds of polarisation are produced from plane polarised light by wave plates.
