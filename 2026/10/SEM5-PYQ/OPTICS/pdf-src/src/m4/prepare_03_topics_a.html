UNITS 17–19

What polarisation is

T1

Polarisation and its kinds

The definition (PYQ N24 Q10) and the three kinds you must tell apart (PYQ N20 Q34).

Result · definition Polarisation is the property of a transverse wave by which its vibrations (for light, the electric field $\vec E$) are confined to a particular direction, or follow a definite pattern, in the plane perpendicular to the direction of travel. Light whose $\vec E$ keeps a fixed pattern is polarised. Light whose $\vec E$ changes direction randomly and rapidly is unpolarised.
{{fig:states}}
Plane (linear)$\vec E$ vibrates along one fixed line. CircularThe tip of $\vec E$ goes round a circle (constant length, steadily turning). EllipticalThe tip of $\vec E$ traces an ellipse. Unpolarised$\vec E$ jumps between all sideways directions, randomly, about $10^8$ times a second. Drawn as a star of arrows, or as lines + dots. Partially polarisedA mixture of unpolarised and polarised light.
Plane of vibration vs plane of polarisation The plane of vibration contains $\vec E$ and the direction of travel. The plane of polarisation is the plane perpendicular to it (it contains $\vec B$). This old naming confuses everyone: when in doubt, just say "$\vec E$ vibrates along …".
Exam trapCircular light is not "rays going round in a spiral". The light goes straight; at any one point the arrow $\vec E$ turns round, keeping the same length.
Check yourself
Can sound waves in air be polarised? Why?
No. Sound in air is longitudinal: the air shakes along the direction of travel, so there is no sideways direction to choose.

T2

Polariser, analyser and Malus' law

What happens to the brightness as you turn one polariser behind another.

{{fig:malus}}
The idea

A polariser has a pass axis. It lets through only the part (component, M1) of $\vec E$ along that axis and blocks the part across it.

Unpolarised light points every way equally, so on average exactly half the intensity gets through, whatever the angle.

A second polariser used to test the light is called the analyser.

Malus' law, step by step

After the polariser: plane polarised light, amplitude $E_0$, intensity $I_0 \propto E_0^2$
Analyser axis at angle θ to that vibration. Part passed: $E_0\cos\theta$Component along the pass axis (M1). The part $E_0\sin\theta$ is blocked.
$I \propto (E_0\cos\theta)^2$, so $I = I_0\cos^2\theta$Intensity ∝ amplitude² (P1).
Result · Malus' law $$I = I_0\cos^2\theta$$

θ = 0°: all passes. θ = 90° ("crossed"): darkness. Turning the analyser through 360° gives two bright and two dark positions.

Worked example: three polaroids

Unpolarised $I_0$ through polaroid 1: $\dfrac{I_0}{2}$Unpolarised always halves.
Polaroid 2 at 45° to polaroid 1: $\dfrac{I_0}{2}\cos^2 45^\circ = \dfrac{I_0}{4}$
Polaroid 3 at 90° to polaroid 1, so 45° to polaroid 2: $\dfrac{I_0}{4}\cos^2 45^\circ = \dfrac{I_0}{8}$Always use the angle between neighbouring polaroids.

Surprise: without the middle polaroid, crossed polaroids give darkness. Adding one in between lets light through! The middle one gives the light a new direction that has a component along the last axis.

Partially polarised lightTurn an analyser in it: the brightness changes between $I_{max}$ and $I_{min}$ but never reaches zero. Degree of polarisation $P = \dfrac{I_{max}-I_{min}}{I_{max}+I_{min}}$ (1 for plane polarised, 0 for unpolarised).
Exam trapMalus' law applies to light that is already polarised. For unpolarised light, halve it first, then apply $\cos^2$.

UNIT 18 · MAKING PLANE POLARISED LIGHT
T3

Polaroids, wire grids and scattering

Three ways to remove one direction of vibration.

Dichroism and Polaroid Some materials absorb one direction of vibration much more strongly than the other (tourmaline is a natural example). A Polaroid sheet has long chain molecules (with iodine) all lined up. Electrons can move easily along the chains, so $\vec E$ along the chains is absorbed. $\vec E$ across the chains passes. The pass axis is perpendicular to the chains.
Wire grid Fine parallel wires, closer together than the wavelength, do the same for long waves (microwaves, infrared): $\vec E$ along the wires drives currents and is absorbed or reflected; $\vec E$ perpendicular to the wires passes.
Where the "picket fence" picture liesA rope through a fence of vertical slits passes vertical shakes. But a wire grid passes the vibration perpendicular to its wires: the opposite. And a fence cannot explain $\cos^2\theta$. Think "keeps the component along its pass axis", not "slits".
Scattering Sunlight makes the electrons of air molecules shake like tiny antennas. A shaking charge sends out no light along its own line of shaking. So light scattered at 90° to the sunbeam can only contain the vibration perpendicular to both the beam and the viewing line: it is plane polarised. This is why the blue sky, seen at 90° from the sun, is polarised.

T4

Polarisation by reflection: Brewster's law

PYQ N20 Q17: "State Brewster's law and what is Brewster window?"

{{fig:brewster}}
Result · Brewster's law When unpolarised light falls on a transparent surface at the angle $i_B$ given by $$\tan i_B = n\quad\left(=\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right),$$ the reflected light is completely plane polarised, vibrating perpendicular to the plane of incidence. At this angle the reflected and refracted rays are at 90° to each other.

Why the two rays are at 90°, and why $\tan i_B = n$

Experiment (and wave theory) shows that at the polarising angle the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular: $i_B + r = 90^\circ$Physical reason: in the glass, electrons shake along the refracted ray's $\vec E$ in the plane of incidence. That direction is along the would-be reflected ray, and a shaking charge sends nothing along its own line, so that vibration is missing from the reflected light.
So $r = 90^\circ - i_B$ and $\sin r = \cos i_B$
Snell: $n_1\sin i_B = n_2\sin r = n_2\cos i_B$
$\tan i_B = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$Divide by $\cos i_B$. For air → glass of $n=1.5$: $i_B = \tan^{-1}1.5 = 56.3^\circ$. Air → water: 53.1°.
Pile of plates and the Brewster window

At $i_B$ only about 15% of the perpendicular vibration is reflected per surface; the transmitted beam is only partly polarised. A pile of plates (10–15 glass plates at $i_B$) reflects away a little more perpendicular light at every surface, so the transmitted beam becomes nearly plane polarised (vibrating in the plane of incidence).

A Brewster window is a glass window tilted at Brewster's angle, used at the ends of gas laser tubes. Light vibrating in the plane of incidence passes it with no reflection loss at all, so the laser gives polarised light.

Exam trapThe reflected beam is fully polarised; the transmitted beam is only partly polarised (unless you use a pile of plates).

UNIT 20 · DOUBLE REFRACTION
T5

Double refraction: O-ray and E-ray

Look at a dot through calcite and you see two dots. Why?

{{fig:double_refraction}}
Result · double refraction (PYQ N24 Q18) When light enters certain crystals (calcite, quartz), it splits into two refracted rays:
  • the ordinary (O) ray, which obeys Snell's law and has the same speed $c/\mu_o$ in every direction;
  • the extraordinary (E) ray, which in general does not obey Snell's law and whose speed depends on its direction.
Both rays are plane polarised, with vibrations perpendicular to each other. This is double refraction (birefringence).
Optic axis and principal section

Optic axis: a direction in the crystal along which the O and E rays travel with the same speed, so there is no double refraction. (Any line parallel to it is also an optic axis.)

Uniaxial crystals (calcite, quartz) have one optic axis; biaxial crystals (mica) have two.

Principal section: a plane containing the optic axis and perpendicular to a pair of opposite faces. The E-ray vibrates in the principal section; the O-ray vibrates perpendicular to it.

Negative and positive crystals
Negative (calcite)$\mu_e < \mu_o$, so the E-ray is faster (off the axis). Calcite: $\mu_o = 1.658$, $\mu_e = 1.486$. Positive (quartz)$\mu_e > \mu_o$, so the E-ray is slower. Quartz: $\mu_o = 1.544$, $\mu_e = 1.553$.
Result · E-ray speed in a direction θ from the optic axis $$\frac{1}{v_e^2(\theta)} = \frac{\cos^2\theta}{v_{\parallel}^2} + \frac{\sin^2\theta}{v_{\perp}^2}$$

$v_\parallel = c/\mu_o$ along the axis (same as O), $v_\perp = c/\mu_e$ at 90° to it. (This is the polar equation of the E-wavelet ellipse, T6.)

PYQ N25 Q16: $v_\parallel = 1.81\times10^8$, $v_\perp = 2.02\times10^8$ m/s, θ = 45°: $\dfrac1{v_e^2} = \dfrac12\left(\dfrac1{1.81^2} + \dfrac1{2.02^2}\right)\times10^{-16}$
$= \tfrac12(0.3052 + 0.2451)\times10^{-16} = 0.2752\times10^{-16}$, so $v_e = 1.906\times10^8$ m/sBetween the two extremes, as it must be.
Exam trap"In a negative crystal the O-ray is slower than the E-ray": true (except along the optic axis, where they are equal). PYQ N22 Q9.

T6

Huygens' explanation of double refraction

PYQ N24 Q9. Two kinds of wavelets instead of one.

{{fig:huygens_wavelets}}
Result · Huygens' theory
  1. Every point of a wavefront entering a uniaxial crystal sends out two secondary wavelets.
  2. The O-wavelet is a sphere (same speed in all directions).
  3. The E-wavelet is an ellipsoid of revolution about the optic axis (speed depends on direction).
  4. The sphere and the ellipsoid touch along the optic axis, where the speeds are equal.
  5. Negative crystal: ellipsoid outside the sphere. Positive crystal: inside.
  6. The new O and E wavefronts are the surfaces touching all the spheres and all the ellipsoids respectively. A ray goes from the wavelet's centre to its point of contact with the wavefront.

The important cases (light falling normally on the crystal)

{{fig:huygens_cases}}
Optic axis ⟂ surfaceSphere and ellipsoid touch along the direction of travel: both wavefronts coincide. No double refraction; one ray at speed $c/\mu_o$. Optic axis ∥ surfaceBoth wavefronts are parallel to the surface and both rays go straight on, along the same line, but at different speeds. No separation, but one falls behind the other: the basis of wave plates (T9). Optic axis at an angleThe O-ray goes straight; the E-ray is bent sideways (it goes to the point where the ellipsoid touches the wavefront), so you see two images.
Oblique incidence, axis ⟂ plane of incidenceThen the E-wavelet's cross-section in that plane is a circle too, of radius set by $c/\mu_e$, so the E-ray obeys Snell's law with index $\mu_e$: $\sin i/\sin r_e = \mu_e$.
Exam trapFor the E-ray, the ray direction (centre → touching point) is in general not perpendicular to its wavefront.
Check yourself
Light falls normally on calcite cut with its optic axis perpendicular to the faces. How many images?
One. Along the optic axis both waves travel at the same speed.
