T7

The Nicol prism

Using double refraction to make plane polarised light (Book 2 topic).

{{fig:nicol}}
Construction: a calcite crystal about three times as long as it is wide. Its end faces are ground from 71° to 68°. It is cut in two along a diagonal plane and the halves are cemented back with Canada balsam ($\mu = 1.55$)
Splitting: unpolarised light entering the first face splits into the O-ray ($\mu_o = 1.658$) and the E-ray ($\mu_e = 1.486$)
O-ray removed: it goes from calcite (1.658) towards balsam (1.55), denser → rarer. Critical angle $\sin C = 1.55/1.658$, so $C \approx 69^\circ$. It arrives at a larger angle, so it is totally internally reflected and absorbed by the blackened sideTotal internal reflection (P3).
E-ray passes: balsam (1.55) is denser than calcite for the E-ray (1.486), so it cannot be totally reflected. It comes out plane polarised, vibrating in the principal section
UsesAs a polariser (makes plane polarised light) and as an analyser (rotate it in polarised light: two positions of darkness per turn). A Polaroid does the same job more cheaply but less perfectly.

In the model paper: Q17, Q21, Q31.

UNITS 21–22

Circles and ellipses of light

T8

Two perpendicular vibrations

Line, circle or ellipse: it all depends on the phase difference δ.

Let one vibration be along $x$ and one along $y$, same frequency, phase difference δ:

$$E_x = a\cos\omega t,\qquad E_y = b\cos(\omega t - \delta)$$
$\cos\omega t = \dfrac{E_x}{a}$,   $\sin\omega t = \sqrt{1 - \dfrac{E_x^2}{a^2}}$From the first equation, and $\sin^2+\cos^2=1$.
$\dfrac{E_y}{b} = \cos\omega t\cos\delta + \sin\omega t\sin\delta$Expand $\cos(\omega t - \delta)$ (M2).
$\dfrac{E_y}{b} - \dfrac{E_x}{a}\cos\delta = \sqrt{1 - \dfrac{E_x^2}{a^2}}\;\sin\delta$Substitute step 1, move the cos term across.
Square both sides: $\dfrac{E_y^2}{b^2} - \dfrac{2E_xE_y}{ab}\cos\delta + \dfrac{E_x^2}{a^2}\cos^2\delta = \sin^2\delta - \dfrac{E_x^2}{a^2}\sin^2\delta$$(p-q)^2 = p^2 - 2pq + q^2$ on the left; multiply out on the right.
Move the last term left and use $\cos^2\delta+\sin^2\delta = 1$:   $\dfrac{E_x^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{E_y^2}{b^2} - \dfrac{2E_xE_y}{ab}\cos\delta = \sin^2\delta$The general ellipse. Time has disappeared, so this is the path of the tip of $\vec E$.
{{fig:lissajous}}
Result · special cases
δ = 0$E_y = \frac ba E_x$: a straight line → plane polarised δ = π$E_y = -\frac ba E_x$: a line the other way → plane polarised δ = π/2, a = b$E_x^2 + E_y^2 = a^2$ → circularly polarised δ = π/2, a ≠ b$\frac{E_x^2}{a^2}+\frac{E_y^2}{b^2}=1$ → ellipse with axes along x and y any other δa tilted ellipse → elliptically polarised
Right and leftThe tip goes round either clockwise or anticlockwise. In Ghatak's convention (looking at the oncoming light, wave along +z), $0 < \delta < \pi$ is right-handed and $\pi < \delta < 2\pi$ is left-handed. E.g. $E_x = a\cos\omega t$, $E_y = a\sin\omega t$ ($\delta = \pi/2$) is right circular. Always state your convention: books differ.
Exam trapδ = 0 gives a line, not a circle. A circle needs a quarter-period lag (π/2) and equal amplitudes.

In the model paper: Q22, Q42.

T9

Wave plates: quarter-wave and half-wave

A slice of crystal that makes exactly the phase difference we want. Asked in five past papers.

{{fig:wave_plate}}
Cut a crystal plate with the optic axis parallel to its faces. Send plane polarised light in normallyHuygens case "axis ∥ surface" (T6): O and E travel along the same line at different speeds.
Inside, the light is two perpendicular vibrations: E (along the axis) and O (across it), starting in step
Optical paths through thickness $d$: $\mu_o d$ and $\mu_e d$. Path difference $\Delta = (\mu_o - \mu_e)\,d$ (take the size)
Phase difference $\delta = \dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mu_o-\mu_e)\,d$P1: phase = $(2\pi/\lambda)\times$ path.
Result · plate thicknesses

Quarter-wave plate ($\Delta = \lambda/4$, $\delta = \pi/2$):

$$d = \frac{\lambda}{4(\mu_o-\mu_e)}$$

Half-wave plate ($\Delta = \lambda/2$, $\delta = \pi$):

$$d = \frac{\lambda}{2(\mu_o-\mu_e)}$$

Use the size of $(\mu_o - \mu_e)$; calcite gives a positive difference, quartz a negative one. Odd multiples of these thicknesses work too.

Worked example (PYQ N20 Q28; same idea as N19 Q30, N22 Q30, N25 Q17)

Quartz, λ = 5000 Å, $\mu_e = 1.553$, $\mu_o = 1.544$.

$|\mu_e - \mu_o| = 0.009$,   λ = $5\times10^{-7}$ mConvert Å to m (M4).
QWP: $d = \dfrac{5\times10^{-7}}{4\times0.009} = 1.39\times10^{-5}$ m $= 13.9$ μm
HWP: $d = \dfrac{5\times10^{-7}}{2\times0.009} = 2.78\times10^{-5}$ m $= 27.8$ μmTwice the QWP.
Result · what the plates do
QWP, linear at 45° to axisequal O and E parts, 90° apart → circular QWP, linear at other angleunequal parts, 90° apart → elliptical (axes along the plate's axes) QWP, circular in→ linear out (it undoes the quarter lag) HWP, linear at angle φ→ linear at −φ: the plane is turned by 2φ HWP, circular in→ circular of the opposite hand
Exam trapA wave plate is a QWP only for the one wavelength it was cut for. At another λ, δ is different and the light comes out elliptical.

In the model paper: Q18 (PYQ N20 Q16), Q19, Q20, Q28 (PYQ N20 Q28 + 3 more), Q33, Q37.

T10

Producing plane, circular and elliptical light

PYQ N20 Q23 and N19 Q34 (first half). A recipe for each.

Result · recipes

Plane polarised: pass ordinary light through a polariser (Nicol prism or Polaroid). Or reflect it at Brewster's angle.

Circularly polarised: plane polarised light from a Nicol falls on a quarter-wave plate with its optic axis at 45° to the vibration. The O and E parts are equal ($E_0\cos45^\circ = E_0\sin45^\circ$) and leave a quarter-wave apart (δ = π/2): a circle.

Elliptically polarised: same, but with the optic axis at any angle φ other than 0°, 45° or 90°. Parts $E_0\cos\phi$ and $E_0\sin\phi$ are unequal, δ = π/2: an ellipse with axes along the plate's axes.

For the ellipse: $E_x = E_0\sin\phi\,\cos\omega t$, $E_y = E_0\cos\phi\,\cos(\omega t - \tfrac\pi2)$Components (M1), quarter-wave lag.
$\dfrac{E_x^2}{(E_0\sin\phi)^2} + \dfrac{E_y^2}{(E_0\cos\phi)^2} = 1$T8 with δ = π/2. At φ = 45° the two denominators are equal: a circle.
Plate between crossed polarisersPlate axis at φ to the first polariser, phase δ: $I = I_1\sin^2 2\phi\,\sin^2(\delta/2)$, where $I_1$ is the intensity after the first polariser. QWP at 45°: $I_1/2$. HWP at 45°: all of $I_1$ passes.

In the model paper: Q35 (PYQ N20 Q23), Q32, Q39.

T11

Analysing polarised light

You are handed a beam. What kind is it? Use an analyser, then a QWP if needed.

Rotate an analyser in the beam.Three possible results: A, B or C below.
A. Brightness drops to zero twice per turn → plane polarised
B. Brightness does not change → unpolarised, circular, or a mix. Put a QWP before the analyser and rotate the analyser again: still no change → unpolarised; zero twice → circular (the QWP turned it into linear); varies but no zero → unpolarised + circular mix
C. Brightness varies but never zero → elliptical, or partly plane polarised. Set the QWP with its optic axis along the analyser's position of maximum, then rotate the analyser: zero twice → elliptical; no zero, maximum in the same place → plane + unpolarised; no zero, maximum moved → elliptical + unpolarised
Why the QWP test worksA QWP adds a quarter-wave lag between its two axes. Circular light (or elliptical light lined up with the plate's axes) has a quarter-wave lag already. Adding another gives δ = 0 or π: a line, which an analyser can extinguish. Unpolarised light has no fixed lag, so it stays unpolarised.
Exam trapConstant brightness through an analyser does not prove the light is unpolarised. Circular light does that too. You need the QWP test.

In the model paper: Q39 (PYQ N19 Q34), Q40.

T12

Optical activity and Laurent's half-shade polarimeter

Liquids and crystals that turn the plane of vibration (PYQ N25 Q7, N20 Q5, N24 Q18).

Result · optical activity When plane polarised light passes through certain substances (quartz along its optic axis, sugar solution, turpentine), its plane of vibration is rotated. This is optical activity. Turning clockwise (looking towards the source) = dextro-rotatory (right-handed); anticlockwise = laevo-rotatory. The angle of rotation is proportional to the length of the path and (for solutions) to the concentration, and depends on wavelength and temperature.
Fresnel's explanation Plane polarised light = right circular + left circular light of equal amplitude (their sideways parts cancel, the rest adds along a line). In an active substance the two circular waves travel at slightly different speeds ($n_L \ne n_R$). After a length $z$ one is ahead of the other, and when they are added again the line has turned by $$\phi = \frac{\pi}{\lambda}(n_L - n_R)\,z$$
Result · specific rotation (PYQ N20 Q5) The rotation produced by a 1 decimetre (10 cm) length of solution of concentration 1 g/cm³, at a given wavelength and temperature: $$[\alpha]_\lambda^T = \frac{\theta}{l\,c}\qquad (\theta\text{ in degrees},\ l\text{ in dm},\ c\text{ in g/cm}^3)$$

Laurent's half-shade polarimeter (Book 2; PYQ N24 Q18)

{{fig:laurent}}
Why a half-shade? Finding the exact position of complete darkness by eye is not accurate: the brightness changes very slowly near zero. Comparing two halves of a field for equal dimness is far more sensitive
The half-shade: a disc, one half a half-wave plate (quartz, axis parallel to the dividing line), the other half plain glass that absorbs the same amount of light
The polariser's vibration makes a small angle ε with the plate's axis. In the glass half it stays at +ε; the HWP turns it to −ε (T9). So the two halves vibrate along OA and OB, symmetric about the axis
With the analyser exactly at 90° to the axis's bisector, both halves are equally dim. Turn it a little either way and one half brightens while the other darkens
Measurement: set equal dimness with water in the tube; note the analyser reading. Fill with the sugar solution: the balance is lost. Turn the analyser until equal dimness returns. The angle turned is the rotation θ
Specific rotation $[\alpha] = \dfrac{\theta}{l\,c}$, or with known $[\alpha]$, find the concentration $c = \dfrac{\theta}{[\alpha]\,l}$
Check yourself
A 20 cm tube of sugar solution rotates the plane by 13.3°. $[\alpha] = 66.5^\circ$ per dm per g/cm³. Concentration?
$l = 2$ dm, $c = \dfrac{13.3}{66.5\times2} = 0.1$ g/cm³.

In the model paper: Q23 (PYQ N25 Q7), Q24 (PYQ N20 Q5), Q34, Q36 (PYQ N24 Q18).
