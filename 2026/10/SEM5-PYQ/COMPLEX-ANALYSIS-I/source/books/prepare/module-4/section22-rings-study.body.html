1. Rings

A set with two operations is more general than a group. Such a structure is a ring. Hilbert introduced the term; Emmy Noether gave the modern axioms in 1921. The historical note on printed page 186 is kept in the excerpt.

Definition 22.1 A ring $\langle R, +, \cdot \rangle$ is a set $R$ with two binary operations, addition and multiplication, such that $\langle R, + \rangle$ is an abelian group, multiplication is associative, and for all $a, b, c \in R$ both $a(b + c) = ab + ac$ and $(a + b)c = ac + bc$ hold.

Example 22.2. $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$ are rings with the usual operations. We write $\mathbb{Z}$ for $\langle \mathbb{Z}, +, \cdot \rangle$ and so on.

Example 22.3. If $R$ is a ring, the $n \times n$ matrices with entries in $R$ form a ring $M_n(R)$. Multiplication is not commutative for $n \ge 2$.

Example 22.4. The set $F$ of all functions $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a ring under $(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x)$ and $(fg)(x) = f(x)g(x)$. Exercise 36 asks you to check the ring axioms $\mathcal{R}_2$ and $\mathcal{R}_3$ for this ring.

Exercise 36 (named in Example 22.4). Show that the multiplication defined on the set $F$ of functions in Example 22.4 satisfies axioms $\mathcal{R}_2$ and $\mathcal{R}_3$ for a ring.

Example 22.5. $n\mathbb{Z}$ with the usual addition and multiplication is a ring.

Example 22.6. $\mathbb{Z}_n$ with multiplication modulo $n$ is a ring. For example, $(3)(7) = 1$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{10}$.

Example 22.7. The direct product $R_1 \times \cdots \times R_n$ of rings, with operations by components, is a ring.

2. Elementary properties

For a ring $R$, $0$ is the additive identity, $-a$ the additive inverse, and $n \cdot a$ means $a + \cdots + a$ with $n$ summands, with $(-n) \cdot a = (-a) + \cdots + (-a)$. The symbol $n \cdot a$ is not a product of $n$ and $a$ in the ring, since $n$ may not belong to $R$.

Theorem 22.8 If $R$ is a ring with additive identity $0$, then for all $a, b \in R$:
  1. $0a = a0 = 0$;
  2. $a(-b) = (-a)b = -(ab)$;
  3. $(-a)(-b) = ab$.

Proof. From $a0 + a0 = a(0 + 0) = a0 = 0 + a0$ the cancellation law gives $a0 = 0$; likewise $0a = 0$. For (2), $a(-b) + ab = a(-b + b) = a0 = 0$, so $a(-b) = -(ab)$; similarly $(-a)b + ab = 0$. For (3), $(-a)(-b) = -(a(-b)) = -(-(ab)) = ab$ by (2) and the uniqueness of inverses. ◆

The exercise named in the text after this proof (Exercise 30) asks you to find the error in the proof of (2) that starts from $(-a)b = ((-1)a)b$.

Exercise 30 (named after Theorem 22.8). What is wrong with the following attempt at a proof of Property 2 in Theorem 22.8? $(-a)b = ((-1)a)b = (-1)(ab) = -(ab)$.

3. Homomorphisms and isomorphisms of rings

Definition 22.9 For rings $R$ and $R'$, a map $\phi : R \to R'$ is a homomorphism if for all $a, b \in R$: (1) $\phi(a + b) = \phi(a) + \phi(b)$, and (2) $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$. The kernel is $\operatorname{Ker}(\phi) = \{a \in R \mid \phi(a) = 0'\}$.

Condition (1) says $\phi$ is a group homomorphism of the additive groups, so all results on group homomorphisms apply to the additive part. In particular $\phi$ is one-to-one exactly when $\operatorname{Ker}(\phi) = \{0\}$. Factor rings are discussed in Section 30 of the book; Module V and later.

Example 22.10. For $a \in \mathbb{R}$ the evaluation map $\phi_a : F \to \mathbb{R}$, $\phi_a(f) = f(a)$, is a ring homomorphism. Finding a real root of $p(x) = 0$ amounts to finding $a$ with $\phi_a(p) = 0$.

Exercise 37 (named in Example 22.10). Show that the evaluation map $\phi_a$ of Example 22.10 is a ring homomorphism.

Example 22.11. The map $\phi : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}_n$ that sends $a$ to its remainder mod $n$ is a ring homomorphism. The additive part is Module 2 material. For the multiplicative part, write $a = q_1n + r_1$ and $b = q_2n + r_2$; then $ab = n(\cdots) + r_1r_2$, so $\phi(ab)$ is the remainder of $r_1r_2$, which is $\phi(a)\phi(b)$.

Definition 22.12 An isomorphism $\phi : R \to R'$ is a ring homomorphism that is one-to-one and onto. The rings are then isomorphic.

Isomorphism of rings is an equivalence relation: the inverse of an isomorphism and the composite of two isomorphisms are again isomorphisms. Exercise 38 asks you to check this.

Exercise 38 (named after Definition 22.12). Complete the argument outlined after Definitions 22.12 to show that isomorphism gives an equivalence relation on a collection of rings.

Example 22.13. $\langle \mathbb{Z}, + \rangle$ and $\langle 2\mathbb{Z}, + \rangle$ are isomorphic as groups under $x \mapsto 2x$. This map is not a ring isomorphism, since $\phi(xy) = 2xy$ but $\phi(x)\phi(y) = 4xy$.

4. Units, fields and division rings

$2\mathbb{Z}$ has no identity for multiplication, and matrix rings are not commutative. The ring $\{0\}$ is the zero ring. Theorem 22.8 shows that $0$ can be a multiplicative identity only in the zero ring.

Definition 22.14 A ring is a commutative ring if multiplication is commutative. A ring with a multiplicative identity is a ring with unity, and the identity is unity, written $1$.

In a ring with unity, the distributive laws give $(n \cdot 1)(m \cdot 1) = (nm) \cdot 1$.

Example 22.15. If $\gcd(r, s) = 1$, then $\mathbb{Z}_{rs} \simeq \mathbb{Z}_r \times \mathbb{Z}_s$ as rings. The map $n \cdot 1 \mapsto n \cdot (1,1)$ is an additive isomorphism, and it respects multiplication because $\phi(nm) = (nm)\cdot(1,1) = [n \cdot (1,1)][m \cdot (1,1)]$.

Exercise 45 (named in Example 22.15). Show that the multiplicative inverse of a unit in a ring with unity is unique.

A multiplicative inverse of $a$ in a ring with unity $1 \ne 0$ is an element $a^{-1}$ with $aa^{-1} = a^{-1}a = 1$. It is unique when it exists.

Definition 22.16 Let $R$ be a ring with unity $1 \ne 0$. $u \in R$ is a unit if it has a multiplicative inverse in $R$. If every nonzero element is a unit, $R$ is a division ring (or skew field). A field is a commutative division ring. A noncommutative division ring is a strictly skew field.

Example 22.17. The units of $\mathbb{Z}_{14}$ are $1, 3, 5, 9, 11, 13$. Section 24 shows that the units of $\mathbb{Z}_n$ are the $m$ with $\gcd(m, n) = 1$.

Example 22.18. $\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field: $2$ has no inverse. $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}$ are fields. Section 32 gives a strictly skew field.

A subring is a subset that is a ring under the induced operations; a subfield is defined the same way for a field. The notation $K \le L$ means $K$ is a substructure of $L$.

Exercise 50 (named after Example 22.18). Show that a subset $S$ of a ring $R$ gives a subring of $R$ if and only if $0 \in S$, $a - b \in S$ for all $a, b \in S$, and $ab \in S$ for all $a, b \in S$.
