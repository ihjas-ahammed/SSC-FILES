1

Definition of the derivative with ε and δ; verify $f'(2) = 4$ for $f(x) = x^2$.

Know firstT01P04M01

Definition 6.1.1. Let $I \subseteq \mathbb R$ be an interval, $f : I \to \mathbb R$, $c \in I$. A real number L is the derivative of f at c if, given any $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $\delta(\varepsilon) > 0$ such that if $x \in I$ and $0 < |x - c| < \delta(\varepsilon)$, then $\left|\dfrac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} - L\right| < \varepsilon$. We write $f'(c) = L$.

Scratch. $\dfrac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} = x + 2$ for $x \ne 2$, and $|(x + 2) - 4| = |x - 2|$. Want this $< \varepsilon$, so $\delta = \varepsilon$ works.

Proof. Let $\varepsilon > 0$, $\delta = \varepsilon$, $L = 4$. If $0 < |x - 2| < \delta$ then $\left|\frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} - 4\right| = |x - 2| < \varepsilon$. Hence $f'(2) = 4$.

$f'(2) = 4$ (δ = ε; e.g. ε = 0.01 needs δ = 0.01).

Marks: definition 1 · scratch 1 · δ and conclusion 1.

2CALC-24 Q5 · related FYUGP

$f'(c)$ for $f(x) = 3x$ from the definition.

Know firstT01M01

For $x \ne c$: $\dfrac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} = \dfrac{3x - 3c}{x - c} = 3$. This is the constant 3, so for every ε > 0 any δ (say δ = 1) gives $|3 - 3| = 0 < \varepsilon$.

$f'(c) = 3$ for every $c \in \mathbb R$.

Marks: definition 1 · quotient 1 · conclusion 1.

3

Derivative of $g(x) = 1/x$ at $c \ne 0$ from the definition.

Know firstT01M02M04

For $x \ne 0$, $x \ne c$: $\dfrac{1/x - 1/c}{x - c} = \dfrac{c - x}{xc\,(x - c)} = \dfrac{-1}{xc}$. As $x \to c$ the limit laws (denominator $\to c^2 \ne 0$) give $-\dfrac{1}{c^2}$.

$g'(c) = -\dfrac{1}{c^2}$, $c \ne 0$. Check c = 2: quotient at x = 2.001 is $-1/(2.001 \cdot 2) = -0.24988 \approx -0.25$.

Marks: quotient 1 · cancel $x - c$ 1 · limit 1.

4

$f'(c) = \lim n\{f(c + 1/n) - f(c)\}$; the converse fails for $|x|$.

Know firstT01P05M03

Let $x_n = c + \frac1n$. Then $x_n \to c$ and $x_n \ne c$, and $\dfrac{f(x_n) - f(c)}{x_n - c} = n\{f(c + \frac1n) - f(c)\}$. By the sequential criterion for limits, since the quotient tends to $f'(c)$ as $x \to c$, this sequence converges to $f'(c)$.

Converse false. $f(x) = |x|$, c = 0: $n\{|\tfrac1n| - 0\} = 1$ for all n, so the sequence converges to 1. But $f'(0)$ does not exist: the quotient is $+1$ for $x > 0$ and $-1$ for $x < 0$ (e.g. $x_n = -1/n$ gives $-1$).

Marks: sequence and criterion 2 · counterexample 1.

5

$f(x) = x^{1/3}$ is continuous but not differentiable at 0.

Know firstT02P05

The cube root is continuous at 0 (composition of continuous functions; or given ε, δ = ε³ works). For $x \ne 0$: $\dfrac{f(x) - f(0)}{x - 0} = x^{-2/3}$. Take $x_n = n^{-3} \to 0$: the quotient is $n^2 \to \infty$ (n = 10: 100). The quotient is unbounded, so it has no finite limit.

$f'(0)$ does not exist (vertical tangent); f is continuous at 0.

Marks: continuity 1 · quotient 1 · sequence and conclusion 1.

6

State the quotient rule; differentiate $x/(1 + x^2)$.

Know firstT03P02

Theorem 6.1.3(d). If f and g are differentiable at c and $g(c) \ne 0$, then $f/g$ is differentiable at c and $\left(\dfrac fg\right)'(c) = \dfrac{f'(c)g(c) - f(c)g'(c)}{(g(c))^2}$.

Here $f(x) = x$, $g(x) = 1 + x^2 \ge 1 \ne 0$: $\dfrac{1\cdot(1 + x^2) - x\cdot 2x}{(1 + x^2)^2}$.

$\left(\dfrac{x}{1+x^2}\right)' = \dfrac{1 - x^2}{(1 + x^2)^2}$. Check x = 0: 1; x = 1: 0.

Marks: statement 1 · f, g, g ≠ 0 1 · result 1.

7

Induction: $D\,x^n = nx^{n-1}$; deduce $D\,x^{-n}$.

Know firstT04T03

Base. n = 1: $\frac{x - c}{x - c} = 1 = 1\cdot x^0$.

Step. Assume $D x^k = kx^{k-1}$. Product rule on $x^{k+1} = x^k \cdot x$: $kx^{k-1}\cdot x + x^k \cdot 1 = (k + 1)x^k$.

Negative powers. For $x \ne 0$, $x^{-n} = 1/x^n$, $x^n \ne 0$. Quotient rule: $\dfrac{0\cdot x^n - 1\cdot nx^{n-1}}{x^{2n}} = -nx^{-n-1}$.

$D x^n = nx^{n-1}$ ($n \in \mathbb N$); $D x^{-n} = -nx^{-n-1}$ ($x \ne 0$).

Marks: base 1 · inductive step 1 · negative powers 1.

8

State the chain rule; differentiate $\sqrt{5 - 2x + x^2}$; why differentiable on ℝ.

Know firstT06M08

Theorem 6.1.6 (Chain Rule). Let I, J be intervals in ℝ, let $g : I \to \mathbb R$ and $f : J \to \mathbb R$ be functions such that $f(J) \subseteq I$, and let $c \in J$. If f is differentiable at c and g is differentiable at $f(c)$, then $g \circ f$ is differentiable at c and $(g \circ f)'(c) = g'(f(c)) \cdot f'(c)$.

Inner $f(x) = x^2 - 2x + 5 = (x - 1)^2 + 4 \ge 4 > 0$; outer $g(y) = \sqrt y$, differentiable for every $y > 0$, $g'(y) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt y}$. Since f takes only values in $(0, \infty)$, the rule applies at every x.

$g'(x) = \dfrac{2x - 2}{2\sqrt{x^2 - 2x + 5}} = \dfrac{x - 1}{\sqrt{x^2 - 2x + 5}}$. Check x = 3: $2/\sqrt8 = 0.7071$.

Marks: statement 1 · f, g and positivity 1 · derivative 1.

9

Even f has odd $f'$; odd f has even $f'$.

Know firstT06M08

Let $h(x) = f(-x)$. Chain rule with inner $x \mapsto -x$ (derivative −1): $h'(x) = -f'(-x)$.

f even: $h = f$, so $f'(x) = -f'(-x)$, i.e. $f'(-x) = -f'(x)$: $f'$ is odd.

f odd: $h = -f$, so $-f'(x) = -f'(-x)$, i.e. $f'(-x) = f'(x)$: $f'$ is even. ∎

Check: $x^2$ is even, $2x$ is odd; $\sin$ is odd, $\cos$ is even.

Marks: chain rule 1 · even case 1 · odd case 1.

10

$|f|'(c)$ when $f(c) \ne 0$ and when $f(c) = 0$; $|x^2 - 1|$.

Know firstT07T06M03

f(c) ≠ 0. $|y|$ is differentiable at $y \ne 0$ with derivative $\operatorname{sgn}y$; by the chain rule $|f|'(c) = \operatorname{sgn}(f(c))\,f'(c)$.

f(c) = 0. $\dfrac{|f(x)|}{x - c} = \pm\left|\dfrac{f(x)}{x - c}\right|$ with the sign of $x - c$, and $\left|\frac{f(x)}{x - c}\right| \to |f'(c)|$. Right limit $|f'(c)|$, left limit $-|f'(c)|$: equal iff $f'(c) = 0$. So $|f|$ is differentiable at c iff $f'(c) = 0$.

$f(x) = x^2 - 1$: $|f|'(x) = 2x\operatorname{sgn}(x^2 - 1)$ = $2x$ for $|x| > 1$, $-2x$ for $|x| < 1$; fails at $x = \pm1$ since $f'(\pm1) = \pm2 \ne 0$.

Check: x = 2: 4; x = 0.5: $|f| = 1 - x^2$, derivative −1 ✓.

Marks: f(c) ≠ 0 1 · f(c) = 0 1 · example 1.

11

$h(x) = x|x|$ is differentiable on ℝ.

Know firstT07M03

$x > 0$: $h = x^2$, $h' = 2x$. $x < 0$: $h = -x^2$, $h' = -2x$. At 0: $\dfrac{h(x) - h(0)}{x - 0} = \dfrac{x|x|}{x} = |x| \to 0$ from both sides.

$h'(x) = 2|x|$ for all $x \in \mathbb R$ (continuous; but $h''(0)$ does not exist).

Marks: x ≠ 0 1 · x = 0 quotient 1 · answer 1.

12

Prove $D\tan x = \sec^2 x$ and $D\sec x = \sec x\tan x$; domain.

Know firstT08T03P02

On $\cos x \ne 0$, i.e. $x \ne (2k + 1)\pi/2$, use the quotient rule with $\sin' = \cos$, $\cos' = -\sin$:

$(\tan x)' = \dfrac{\cos x\cos x - \sin x(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x} = \dfrac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x$.

$(\sec x)' = \left(\dfrac{1}{\cos x}\right)' = \dfrac{0\cdot\cos x - 1\cdot(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x} = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\cdot\dfrac{1}{\cos x} = \sec x\tan x$.

Both valid for $x \ne (2k + 1)\pi/2$, $k \in \mathbb Z$. Check x = π/4: $\sec^2 = 2$ ✓.

Marks: quotient setup 1 · tan 1 · sec and domain 1.

13

For which $r > 0$ does $f'(0)$ exist, $f(x) = x^r\sin(1/x)$, $f(0) = 0$?

Know firstT09M04M07

Quotient at 0: $\dfrac{f(x) - f(0)}{x} = |x|^{r-1}\sin(1/x)$ (take $x > 0$, or $|x|^r$ for $x < 0$).

r > 1. $|x|^{r-1}\sin(1/x)| \le |x|^{r-1}$. Given ε > 0 take $\delta = \varepsilon^{1/(r-1)}$: then $0 < |x| < \delta$ gives quotient $< \varepsilon$. So $f'(0) = 0$.

r = 1. Quotient is $\sin(1/x)$. $x_n = \frac{1}{2n\pi} \to 0$ gives 0; $y_n = \frac{1}{2n\pi + \pi/2} \to 0$ gives $\sin(2n\pi + \pi/2) = 1$. No limit.

0 < r < 1. At $y_n = 1/(2n\pi + \pi/2)$: quotient $= y_n^{r-1} \to \infty$ (r = ½, n = 100: ≈ 25.1). Unbounded, no limit.

$f'(0)$ exists iff $r > 1$, and then $f'(0) = 0$.

Marks: quotient 1 · r > 1 with δ 1 · r ≤ 1 1.

14CALC-24 Q10 · related FYUGP

Critical points of $x^3 - 3x + 1$; which can be extrema.

Know firstT10P02

Theorem 6.2.1 (Interior Extremum). Let c be an interior point of the interval I at which $f : I \to \mathbb R$ has a relative extremum. If the derivative of f at c exists, then $f'(c) = 0$.

$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x - 1)(x + 1)$; $f'$ exists everywhere on ℝ (every point is interior). So any relative extremum has $f'(c) = 0$: $c = \pm1$. These are only candidates (converse false: $x^3$ at 0).

Sign of $f'$: $+$ on $(-\infty, -1)$, $-$ on $(-1, 1)$, $+$ on $(1, \infty)$, so −1 is a relative maximum and 1 a relative minimum.

Critical points $x = -1, 1$; $f(-1) = 3$ (max), $f(1) = -1$ (min).

Marks: theorem 1 · f′ = 0 1 · classification 1.

17CALC-24 Q9 · related FYUGP

Define increasing and strictly increasing; example with a vanishing derivative.

Know firstT14P03

Definition. $f : I \to \mathbb R$ is increasing on I if $x_1 < x_2$ in I implies $f(x_1) \le f(x_2)$; strictly increasing if $x_1 < x_2$ implies $f(x_1) < f(x_2)$. (In Bartle's sense a constant function is increasing.)

Example. $f(x) = x^3$: $x_1 < x_2 \Rightarrow x_1^3 < x_2^3$, so strictly increasing; yet $f'(0) = 0$. (So "$f' > 0$" is sufficient for strict increase, not necessary.)

Marks: both definitions 1.5 · example 1.5.

18CALC-24 Q20(1) · related FYUGP

Intervals of increase and decrease of $x^4 - 5x^3 + 9x^2$.

Know firstT14M05P13

$f'(x) = 4x^3 - 15x^2 + 18x = x(4x^2 - 15x + 18)$. The quadratic has discriminant $225 - 4\cdot4\cdot18 = 225 - 288 = -63 < 0$ and leading coefficient 4 > 0, so it is positive for every x. Hence $\operatorname{sign} f' = \operatorname{sign} x$.

By Theorem 6.2.7: $f' \le 0$ on $(-\infty, 0]$ gives decreasing; $f' \ge 0$ on $[0, \infty)$ gives increasing.

Decreasing on $(-\infty, 0]$, increasing on $[0, \infty)$; minimum $f(0) = 0$. Check: f(−1) = 15, f(0) = 0, f(1) = 5.

Marks: derivative 1 · quadratic sign 1 · intervals 1.

20

State Lemma 6.2.11 and prove part (a).

Know firstT22P04

Lemma 6.2.11. Let $f : I \to \mathbb R$ have a derivative at $c \in I$. (a) If $f'(c) > 0$, then there is $\delta > 0$ such that $f(x) > f(c)$ for $x \in I$ with $c < x < c + \delta$. (b) If $f'(c) < 0$, then there is $\delta > 0$ such that $f(x) > f(c)$ for $x \in I$ with $c - \delta < x < c$.

Proof of (a). Put $\varepsilon = f'(c)/2 > 0$. By the definition of $f'(c)$ there is $\delta > 0$ with $\left|\dfrac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} - f'(c)\right| < \dfrac{f'(c)}{2}$ for $x \in I$, $0 < |x - c| < \delta$; hence the quotient is $> \dfrac{f'(c)}{2} > 0$. For $c < x < c + \delta$, $x - c > 0$, so $f(x) - f(c) = (x - c)\cdot(\text{quotient}) > 0$. ∎

For (b): same δ, but now $x - c < 0$ and quotient < 0, product > 0.

Marks: statement 1 · δ from ε = f′(c)/2 1 · sign of product 1.
