Unit 5 · Superposition of waves

① Theory — points

  • Superposition principle: when two or more waves overlap, the resultant displacement is the algebraic (vector) sum of the individual displacements.
  • Each wave travels on unaffected after the overlap.
  • Two waves of the same frequency: \(y_1 = a_1\cos(\omega t - \phi_1)\), \(y_2 = a_2\cos(\omega t - \phi_2)\) give \(y = A\cos(\omega t - \phi)\), same frequency.
  • Resultant amplitude: \(A^2 = a_1^2 + a_2^2 + 2a_1a_2\cos\delta\), \(\delta = \phi_1 - \phi_2\).
  • Phase: \(\tan\phi = \frac{a_1\sin\phi_1 + a_2\sin\phi_2}{a_1\cos\phi_1 + a_2\cos\phi_2}\).
  • Constructive: \(\delta = 2n\pi\), \(A = a_1 + a_2\). Destructive: \(\delta = (2n+1)\pi\), \(A = |a_1 - a_2|\).
  • Equal amplitudes: \(A = 2a\cos(\delta/2)\), intensity \(I = 4I_0\cos^2(\delta/2)\).
  • Phasor (vector) method: add amplitudes as vectors at angle \(\delta\) — the cosine rule gives \(A\).
  • Path difference \(\Delta\) ↔ phase difference: \(\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta\).
Must remember
\(A^2 = a_1^2 + a_2^2 + 2a_1a_2\cos\delta\)
\(\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta\)

Unit 6 · Interference by division of wavefront; two point sources

① Theory — points

  • Interference: redistribution of intensity when coherent waves superpose; energy is conserved, only redistributed.
  • Division of wavefront: one wavefront split into two parts by slits, mirrors or a prism (Young, Fresnel mirrors, biprism, Lloyd).
  • Division of amplitude: one beam split by partial reflection (thin films, Newton's rings, Michelson).
  • Two coherent point sources \(S_1, S_2\) separated by \(d\): maxima where \(S_2P - S_1P = n\lambda\), minima where \(S_2P - S_1P = (n + \tfrac12)\lambda\).
  • Locus of constant path difference in a plane is a hyperbola with \(S_1, S_2\) as foci; in 3D a hyperboloid of revolution.
  • Nodal lines (dark) and antinodal lines (bright) are hyperbolas; on a distant screen they look like straight fringes.
  • Screen parallel to \(S_1S_2\): straight fringes; screen perpendicular to \(S_1S_2\): circular rings.
  • Condition for sustained pattern: coherent sources (constant phase difference), same frequency, nearly equal amplitudes, small \(d\).
Must remember
\(S_2P - S_1P = n\lambda \quad(\text{bright})\)
\(\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\)

② Key derivation

Hyperbolic equation of nodal and antinodal curves
Put equal, in-phase sources S₁ at (d/2, 0) and S₂ at (-d/2, 0), with midpoint O. P has coordinates (x,z). Find the curve with fixed path difference Δ = S₂P - S₁P. For the hyperbola calculation assume 0 < |Δ| < d. When Δ = 0 the curve is x = 0; when |Δ| > d there is no solution. Squaring loses the sign of Δ, so retain the branch with x and Δ of the same sign.

Distances — express S₁P and S₂P using coordinates (x, z)
\[S_2P - S_1P = \Delta\]
Start with constant path difference \(\Delta\) at point \(P(x, z)\) (Eq. 14.5).
\[[(x + \tfrac{d}{2})^2 + z^2]^{1/2} - [(x - \tfrac{d}{2})^2 + z^2]^{1/2} = \Delta\]
substitute by \(S_1P,\,S_2P\)
Substitute distances from \(S_1(d/2, 0)\) and \(S_2(-d/2, 0)\) to \(P(x, z)\).
Isolate root — move S₁P to the right before squaring
\[[(x + \tfrac{d}{2})^2 + z^2]^{1/2} = \Delta + [(x - \tfrac{d}{2})^2 + z^2]^{1/2}\]
add by \([(x - \tfrac{d}{2})^2 + z^2]^{1/2}\)
Add \(S_1P\) to both sides so only one square root sits on the left.
Square — square both sides and simplify to 2xd - Δ²
\[(x + \tfrac{d}{2})^2 + z^2 = (x - \tfrac{d}{2})^2 + z^2 + \Delta^2 + 2\Delta[(x - \tfrac{d}{2})^2 + z^2]^{1/2}\]

\[2xd - \Delta^2 = 2\Delta[(x - \tfrac{d}{2})^2 + z^2]^{1/2}\]

Cancel \(x^2 + d^2/4 + z^2\) and move \(\Delta^2\) to the left.

\[4x^2d^2-4xd\Delta^2+\Delta^4=4\Delta^2(x^2-xd+d^2/4+z^2)\]
rearrange
Square both sides and expand every bracket before cancelling the common -4xdΔ² term.
\[(d^2 - \Delta^2)x^2 - \Delta^2 z^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}\Delta^2(d^2 - \Delta^2)\]
Cancel the cross term and divide by 4
Collect the x² and z² terms on the left; factor Δ²(d²-Δ²)/4 on the right.
\[\frac{x^2}{\frac{1}{4}\Delta^2} - \frac{z^2}{\frac{1}{4}(d^2 - \Delta^2)} = 1\]
divide by \(\tfrac{1}{4}\Delta^2(d^2 - \Delta^2)\)
Divide through by the right-hand side to obtain the hyperbola (Eq. 14.6).

Unit 7 · Interference of light: Young's experiment, intensity

① Theory — points

  • Young's double slit: light from slit S falls on two narrow slits \(S_1, S_2\) (distance \(d\)); screen at distance \(D \gg d\).
  • Path difference at height \(x\) on screen: \(\Delta = S_2P - S_1P \approx \frac{xd}{D}\).
  • Bright fringes: \(x_n = \frac{n\lambda D}{d}\); dark: \(x_n = (n+\tfrac12)\frac{\lambda D}{d}\).
  • Fringe width \(\beta = \frac{\lambda D}{d}\): equally spaced, independent of n.
  • Intensity: \(I = I_1 + I_2 + 2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos\delta\); equal beams: \(I = 4I_0\cos^2\frac{\delta}{2}\), \(\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\frac{xd}{D}\).
  • \(I_{max} = 4I_0\), \(I_{min} = 0\); average intensity \(2I_0\) — energy conserved.
  • Two independent sources are incoherent: phase changes randomly in ~\(10^{-10}\) s, so intensities simply add (\(I = I_1 + I_2\)).
  • Coherence: temporal (finite wave-train length \(L = c\tau\)), spatial (finite source size). Path difference must be less than the coherence length.
  • Visibility \(V = \frac{I_{max} - I_{min}}{I_{max} + I_{min}}\).
Must remember
\(\beta = \frac{\lambda D}{d}\)
\(I = 4I_0\cos^2\frac{\delta}{2}\)

② Key derivation

Path difference and fringe width in Young's experiment
Two in-phase pinholes S₁ and S₂ are d apart, with S₁ above the midpoint. O is opposite their midpoint on screen LL′, distance D away. A bright point P is x_n above O. Assume D ≫ d and D ≫ |x_n|, so both ray lengths are approximately D. λ is the wavelength in the surrounding medium and n is integer fringe order. The figure exaggerates d and x_n; the derivation assumes D ≫ d, x_n.

Right triangles — write (S₁P)² and (S₂P)² from right triangles S₁N₁P and S₂N₂P, then subtract
\[(S_1P)^2 = D^2 + \left(x_n - \tfrac{d}{2}\right)^2\]
Pythagoras in right triangle \(S_1N_1P\). \(N_1\) is the foot of the perpendicular from \(S_1\) on the screen. The legs are \(S_1N_1 = D\) and \(N_1P = x_n - d/2\); the hypotenuse is \(S_1P\).
\[(S_2P)^2 = D^2 + \left(x_n + \tfrac{d}{2}\right)^2\]
Pythagoras in right triangle \(S_2N_2P\)
\(S_2\) is \(d/2\) below \(O\)'s level, so \(N_2P = x_n + d/2\); the other leg is again \(D\).
\[(S_2P)^2 - (S_1P)^2 = [D^2 + (x_n + \tfrac{d}{2})^2] - [D^2 + (x_n - \tfrac{d}{2})^2]\]
Subtract the first result from the second
Subtract \((S_1P)^2\) from \((S_2P)^2\) (Eq. 14.18).
\[(S_2P)^2 - (S_1P)^2 = 2x_n d\]
expand
Expand the squares; \(D^2 + x_n^2 + d^2/4\) cancels out.
Factor — use difference of squares to isolate S₂P - S₁P
\[(S_2P - S_1P)(S_2P + S_1P) = 2x_n d\]
factor
Factor the left-hand side as a difference of squares.
\[S_2P - S_1P = \frac{2x_n d}{S_2P + S_1P}\]
divide by \(S_2P + S_1P\)
Divide both sides by \(S_2P + S_1P\).
Paraxial — replace S₂P + S₁P by 2D when D ≫ d, x_n
\[S_2P - S_1P = \frac{x_n d}{D}\]
substitute by \(S_2P + S_1P \approx 2D\)
Replace \(S_2P + S_1P\) with \(2D\) and cancel the factor of 2 (Eq. 14.19).
Fringe width — set path difference equal to nλ and subtract adjacent maxima
\[\frac{x_n d}{D} = n\lambda\]
substitute by \(S_2P - S_1P = n\lambda\)
Set the path difference equal to \(n\lambda\) for the \(n\)-th bright fringe (Eq. 14.17).
\[x_n = \frac{n\lambda D}{d}\]
multiply by \(\frac{D}{d}\)
Multiply both sides by \(D/d\) to find the bright fringe position (Eq. 14.20).
\[x_{n+1}-x_n=\frac{(n+1)\lambda D}{d}-\frac{n\lambda D}{d}\]
rearrange
Write the next bright-fringe position and subtract the current one.
\[\beta = \frac{\lambda D}{d}\]
define by \(\beta = x_{n+1} - x_n\)
Subtract \(x_n\) from \(x_{n+1}\) to obtain the fringe width (Eq. 14.21).

Unit 8 · Fresnel's two-mirror arrangement and biprism

① Theory — points

  • Fresnel two mirrors: two plane mirrors inclined at a small angle \(\theta\) form two virtual images \(S_1, S_2\) of slit S — two coherent sources.
  • Virtual source separation (two mirrors): \(d = 2b\theta\), where \(b\) = distance of S from the mirror junction.
  • Fresnel biprism: two thin prisms of small angle \(\alpha\) base to base (obtuse angle ≈ 179°); refraction gives two virtual sources.
  • Each half deviates light by \(\delta = (n-1)\alpha\); virtual source separation \(d = 2a(n-1)\alpha\), \(a\) = slit-to-prism distance.
  • Fringe width \(\beta = \frac{\lambda D}{d}\), \(D\) = slit-to-eyepiece distance.
  • Measuring d — displacement method: a convex lens between biprism and eyepiece gives two positions; \(d = \sqrt{d_1 d_2}\).
  • Then \(\lambda = \frac{\beta d}{D}\).
  • White light: central fringe white, others coloured; used to locate the zero-order fringe.
  • Biprism is used to find wavelength, thickness of a thin plate (fringe shift), refractive index.
Must remember
\(d = 2a(n-1)\alpha\)
\(d = \sqrt{d_1 d_2}\)
\(\lambda = \frac{\beta d}{D}\)

② Key derivation

Virtual slit separation and wavelength in Fresnel's biprism
The two halves of a thin biprism deviate light oppositely, producing virtual slit images S₁,S₂. Each half has small angle α in radians and index n relative to air. The source slit S is distance a from the biprism. d = S₁S₂ is virtual-source separation; D is source-plane to screen distance and β is measured fringe width. After finding λ, the final steps explain how a movable convex lens measures d without knowing α. Angles and the separation of S₁ and S₂ are exaggerated in the figure.

Prism deviation — each half is a thin prism: δ = (n − 1)α
\[\delta = (n - 1)\alpha\]
Each half of the biprism is a thin prism of very small angle \(\alpha\). A thin prism of index \(n\) turns a ray through about \(\delta = (n-1)\alpha\), towards its base.
Virtual slits — right triangle SQS₁ gives SS₁ = a tan δ ≈ aδ; symmetry doubles it
\[\tan\delta = \frac{SS_1}{a}\]
Tangent in triangle \(SQS_1\)
The emerging ray, traced back from \(Q\), meets the plane of \(S\) at \(S_1\). It makes \(\delta\) with \(QS\). Since \(Q\) is close to the axis, \(SQ \approx a\) and the angle at \(S\) is nearly a right angle: \(SS_1\) is opposite \(\delta\) and \(a\) is adjacent.
\[SS_1 = a\tan\delta\]
Rearrange: multiply by \(a\)
Multiply both sides by \(a\).
\[SS_1 \approx a\delta\]
substitute by \(\tan\delta \approx \delta\)
\(\delta\) is very small (a few minutes of arc), so \(\tan\delta \approx \delta\) in radians.
\[SS_1 \approx a(n-1)\alpha\]
substitute by \(\delta = (n-1)\alpha\)
Apply the thin-prism deviation.
\[d = 2a(n - 1)\alpha\]
The lower half puts \(S_2\) the same distance below \(S\)
The lower half bends light the other way by the same \(\delta\), so \(SS_2 = SS_1\) and \(d = S_1S_2 = 2a(n-1)\alpha\).
Wavelength — S₁, S₂ are coherent: β = λD/d, solved for λ
\[\beta = \frac{\lambda D}{d}\]
Fringe width for two coherent sources
\(S_1\) and \(S_2\) come from the one slit, so they are coherent, as in Young's experiment. At distance \(D\) from them, at the crosswires, the fringes have width \(\beta = \lambda D/d\).
\[\frac{d\beta}{D} = \lambda\]
multiply by \(\frac{d}{D}\)
Multiply both sides by \(d / D\).
\[\lambda = \frac{d\beta}{D}\]
swap
Swap sides to obtain the biprism wavelength relation (Eq. 14.34).
Measuring d — two lens positions give d₁, d₂ with d = √(d₁d₂) and D = b₁ + b₂
\[\frac{d_1}{d} = \frac{b_1}{a_1}\]
Similar triangles through the centre of the lens at \(L_1\)
A convex lens between biprism and eyepiece shows sharp images of \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) at two positions. At \(L_1\), rays through the lens centre are undeviated, so triangles \(S_1O_1S_2\) and \(I_1O_1I_1'\) are similar: image gap over object gap equals image distance \(b_1\) over object distance \(a_1\).
\[d_1 = \frac{b_1}{a_1}d\]
Rearrange: multiply by \(d\)
Multiply both sides by \(d\).
\[\frac{d_2}{d} = \frac{a_1}{b_1}\]
Similar triangles at the second position \(L_2\)
The second sharp position is the conjugate one: object and image distances swap. Now the object distance is \(b_1\) and the image distance is \(b_2 = a_1\), so the image is reduced by the reciprocal ratio.
\[d_2 = \frac{a_1}{b_1}d\]
Rearrange: multiply by \(d\)
Multiply both sides by \(d\).
\[d_1d_2=d^2\]
Multiply the two results
Multiply \(d_1\) by \(d_2\): \(\frac{b_1}{a_1}\cdot\frac{a_1}{b_1} = 1\), so the lens distances cancel.
\[d=\sqrt{d_1d_2}\]
root
Take the positive root for the physical source separation.
\[D=b_1+b_2\]
substitute by \(D = a_1 + b_1,\; a_1 = b_2\)
From \(S_1S_2\) to the crosswires, \(D = a_1 + b_1\). At the conjugate position \(b_2 = a_1\), so \(D = b_1 + b_2\): both are lens-to-crosswire distances, easy to read off.

Unit 9 · White-light fringes, fringe displacement, Lloyd's mirror, Stokes relations

① Theory — points

  • White-light fringes: each colour makes its own pattern; at zero path difference all are bright → central white fringe, then a few coloured fringes, then uniform white.
  • Displacement of fringes: a thin plate (thickness \(t\), index \(n\)) in one beam adds optical path \((n-1)t\); whole pattern shifts toward that beam.
  • Shift \(x_0 = \frac{D}{d}(n-1)t = \frac{\beta}{\lambda}(n-1)t\); number of fringes shifted \(N = (n-1)t/\lambda\).
  • Use: thickness of a mica sheet, or \(n\) — locate central fringe with white light.
  • Lloyd's mirror: slit S and its virtual image S′ in a plane mirror (grazing incidence) act as two coherent sources.
  • In Lloyd's mirror the fringe at the mirror edge (zero geometric path difference) is dark → reflection from a denser medium gives a phase change of \(\pi\).
  • Stokes' relations (principle of optical reversibility): \(r^2 + tt' = 1\) and \(r' = -r\).
  • \(r' = -r\): reflection from one side differs by a phase \(\pi\) from reflection on the other side.
Must remember
\(x_0 = \frac{D}{d}(n-1)t\)
\(r' = -r,\quad tt' = 1 - r^2\)

② Key derivation

Fringes and the phase change in Lloyd's mirror
Slit S₁ is a height h above the plane of a mirror AO used at grazing incidence; S₂ is its image. The screen touches the far end O of the mirror, a distance D from the plane of S₁ and S₂; P is a height x above O. Ghatak §14.11 gives the conditions S₂P − S₁P = nλ (minima) and (n + ½)λ (maxima) from the observed dark central fringe; the fringe positions use the two-source path difference of Eq. 14.19.
tap any step to show it on the figure
Virtual source — S₂ is the mirror image of S₁: d = 2h
\[NS_2 = S_1N\]
Image in the mirror. The mirror plane, extended back to \(N\) under \(S_1\), sends \(S_1\) to its image \(S_2\): the image is as far below the plane as \(S_1\) is above it. Call this height \(h\).
\[d = 2h\]
Add the two heights
The two coherent sources are \(S_1\) and its image \(S_2\), separated by \(d = S_1S_2 = S_1N + NS_2 = 2h\).
Path difference — the reflected path equals S₂P; two sources give xd/D
\[S_1R + RP = S_2P\]
The reflected path, seen from the image
Right triangles \(S_1NR\) and \(S_2NR\) share \(NR\) and have \(S_1N = NS_2\), so \(S_1R = S_2R\). The reflected path \(S_1R + RP\) is therefore \(S_2R + RP\), and \(S_2\), \(R\), \(P\) lie on one line.
\[S_2P - S_1P = \frac{xd}{D}\]
Path difference for two sources, as in Young's experiment (Eq. 14.19)
\(S_1\) and \(S_2\) act like the two pinholes of Young's experiment, a distance \(d\) apart and \(D\) from the screen. \(x\) is the height of \(P\) above \(O\), the point level with the mirror.
Phase change — the dark central fringe shows a π change on reflection
\[\Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(S_2P - S_1P) + \pi\]
Reflection at the denser mirror adds \(\pi\)
With white light the central fringe (zero path difference) is found to be dark. So the reflected beam undergoes a sudden phase change of \(\pi\) on reflection, on top of the phase from its extra path.
\[S_2P - S_1P = n\lambda\]
substitute by \(\Delta\phi = (2n+1)\pi\)
Destructive interference needs \(\Delta\phi = (2n+1)\pi\). The \(\pi\) from reflection is already there, so the path difference must be a whole number of wavelengths: \(n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\) give minima.
\[\frac{x_n d}{D} = n\lambda\]
substitute by \(S_2P - S_1P = \frac{x_n d}{D}\)
Put the geometric path difference into the condition for the \(n\)-th dark fringe at height \(x_n\).
\[x_n = \frac{n\lambda D}{d}\]
multiply by \(\frac{D}{d}\)
Multiply by \(D/d\). For \(n = 0\), \(x_0 = 0\): the fringe at \(O\), where the screen touches the mirror, is dark.
\[S_2P - S_1P = \left(n + \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\lambda\]
Condition for maxima
Constructive interference needs \(\Delta\phi = 2n\pi\), so with the extra \(\pi\) the path difference must be a half-odd number of wavelengths: these are the bright fringes, halfway between the dark ones.
\[\beta = \frac{\lambda D}{2h}\]
substitute by \(\beta = x_{n+1} - x_n = \frac{\lambda D}{d},\; d = 2h\)
Successive dark fringes are \(\lambda D/d\) apart, as in Young's experiment; with \(d = 2h\) the fringe width is \(\lambda D/2h\). The pattern is Young's, shifted by half a fringe.

Unit 10 · Division of amplitude: thin films, cosine law, non-reflecting films

① Theory — points

  • Thin film: rays reflected from top and bottom surfaces interfere (division of amplitude).
  • Cosine law: optical path difference \(\Delta = 2n_2 d\cos\theta'\) (\(\theta'\) = angle of refraction inside the film).
  • One of the two reflections has a phase change \(\pi\) (film in air) → reflected: bright when \(2nd\cos\theta' = (m+\tfrac12)\lambda\), dark when \(2nd\cos\theta' = m\lambda\).
  • Transmitted pattern is complementary to the reflected one.
  • Fringes of equal inclination (Haidinger): film of constant thickness, fringes depend on \(\theta'\) — circles seen at infinity.
  • Fringes of equal thickness (Fizeau): film of varying thickness (wedge), fringes follow constant \(d\); wedge fringe width \(\beta = \frac{\lambda}{2n\alpha}\).
  • Colours of soap bubbles / oil films: white light, different \(\lambda\) satisfy the bright condition at different thickness.
  • Non-reflecting (anti-reflection) film: \(n_f d = \lambda/4\) (quarter-wave) and \(n_f = \sqrt{n_g}\) (equal amplitudes) → reflected beams cancel.
  • MgF₂ (\(n = 1.38\)) on glass — blooming of lenses.
Must remember
\(\Delta = 2n_2 d\cos\theta'\)
\(n_f d = \frac{\lambda}{4},\quad n_f = \sqrt{n_g}\)

② Key derivation

The cosine law for a plane parallel thin film
A parallel film of thickness d and index n₂ lies in index n₁. A ray first reflects at upper point B; another travels inside B → D → F before emerging parallel to it. C is the perpendicular projection of F onto the upper reflected ray, so BC must be subtracted when comparing equal outgoing wavefronts. θ is incidence angle and θ′ the internal angle, both from the normal. Reflect B across the lower face to B′; K is the perpendicular projection of B onto B′F. Δ below is propagation path difference; add any reflection phase reversals separately.
tap any step to show it on the figure
Optical path — write the extra optical path Δ = n₂(BD + DF) − n₁ BC
\[\Delta = n_2(BD + DF) - n_1 BC\]
Start with the optical path difference between the ray that crosses the film \(B \to D \to F\) and the ray reflected at \(B\) (Eq. 15.3). \(C\) is the foot of the perpendicular from \(F\), so both rays are compared on one wavefront \(FC\).
Image point B′ — reflect B in the lower face so BD + DF = B′F
\[BD = B'D\]
Mirror image \(B'\) of \(B\) in the lower face
\(BJ = JB' = d\) and \(DJ\) is common, so right triangles \(BDJ\) and \(B'DJ\) are congruent.
\[\Delta = n_2 B'F - n_1 BC\]
substitute by \(BD + DF = B'D + DF = B'F\)
\(B'\), \(D\) and \(F\) lie on one straight line, so \(B'D + DF = B'F\) (Eq. 15.7).

\[\sin\theta = \frac{BC}{BF}\]
Sine in right triangle \(BCF\)

\[BC = BF\sin\theta\]

Multiply both sides by \(BF\).
\[\sin\theta' = \frac{KF}{BF}\]

\(BK \perp B'F\) and \(B'F\) makes \(\theta'\) with the normal, so \(\angle KBF = \theta'\). The side opposite \(\theta'\) is \(KF\); the hypotenuse is \(BF\).
\[KF = BF\sin\theta'\]

Multiply both sides by \(BF\).
\[n_1\sin\theta = n_2\sin\theta'\]

Unit 11 · Newton's rings and the Michelson interferometer

① Theory — points

  • Newton's rings: plano-convex lens (radius \(R\)) on flat glass; air film of varying thickness → concentric circular fringes of equal thickness.
  • Centre is dark in reflected light (zero thickness, phase change \(\pi\)).
  • Film thickness at radius \(r\): \(t = \frac{r^2}{2R}\). Dark rings: \(2t = m\lambda\) → \(r_m^2 = m\lambda R\).
  • Radii \(\propto \sqrt{m}\): rings get closer outward. Bright rings: \(r_m^2 = (m + \tfrac12)\lambda R\).
  • Wavelength: \(\lambda = \frac{D_{m+p}^2 - D_m^2}{4pR}\). With liquid of index \(\mu\): \(D^2\) reduced by factor \(\mu\); \(\mu = \frac{(D_{m+p}^2 - D_m^2)_{air}}{(D_{m+p}^2 - D_m^2)_{liq}}\).
  • Michelson interferometer: beam splitter \(G_1\) (45°), compensating plate \(G_2\) (equal glass path, needed for white light), mirrors \(M_1, M_2\).
  • Equivalent to an air film between \(M_1\) and the image \(M_2'\): \(\Delta = 2d\cos\theta\) → circular fringes; tilted → straight fringes.
  • Wavelength: mirror moved \(d_0\), \(N\) fringes cross: \(2d_0 = N\lambda\).
  • Two close lines (Na D₁, D₂): mirror travel \(\Delta d\) between successive minima of visibility: \(\Delta\lambda = \frac{\lambda^2}{2\Delta d}\).
Must remember
\(r_m^2 = m\lambda R\)
\(\lambda = \frac{D_{m+p}^2 - D_m^2}{4pR}\)
\(2d_0 = N\lambda\)
\(\Delta\lambda = \frac{\lambda^2}{2\Delta d}\)

② Key derivation

Radii of dark rings and wavelength in Newton's rings
A plano-convex lens of radius of curvature \(R\) rests on flat glass at \(O\). At radius \(r_m\) from the centre the air film between lens and plate has thickness \(t\). \(C\) is the centre of curvature; \(N\) is the foot of the perpendicular from the lens point \(P\) on the axis. Light falls normally and is viewed in reflection.
tap any step to show it on the figure
Air film — dark-ring condition 2t = mλ
\[2t = m\lambda\]
Reflected light from the top and bottom of the air film (\(n = 1\), \(\cos\theta' = 1\)) differs in path by \(2t\); the reflection at the glass plate adds a \(\pi\) phase change, so a dark ring needs \(2t = m\lambda\) (Eq. 15.63).
Circle geometry — Pythagoras in △CNP relates r_m, t and R
\[CP^2 = CN^2 + NP^2\]
Pythagoras in right triangle \(CNP\)
\(N\) is the foot of the perpendicular from \(P\) on the axis, so triangle \(CNP\) is right-angled at \(N\). The hypotenuse is \(CP\).
\[R^2 = (R - t)^2 + r_m^2\]
substitute by \(CP = R,\; CN = R - t,\; NP = r_m\)
\(CP\) is a radius \(R\); \(CN = CO - NO = R - t\); \(NP = OQ = r_m\) is the ring radius.
\[R^2 = R^2 - 2Rt + t^2 + r_m^2\]
expand
Expand \((R - t)^2\).
\[r_m^2 = 2Rt - t^2\]
Cancel \(R^2\) and solve for \(r_m^2\)
\(R^2\) cancels on both sides (Eq. 15.64).
Thin film — drop t² since t ≪ R
\[r_m^2 \approx 2Rt\]
Neglect \(t^2\) since \(t \ll R\)
\(R \approx 100\text{ cm}\) while \(t \lesssim 10^{-3}\text{ cm}\), so \(t^2\) is negligible beside \(2Rt\) (Eq. 15.65).
Dark ring radius — r_m² = mλR
\[r_m^2 = m\lambda R\]
substitute by \(2t = m\lambda\)
Put the dark-ring condition into \(r_m^2 = R(2t)\) (Eq. 15.66). Radii grow as \(\sqrt{m}\), so rings crowd together outward.
Ring diameters — λ from D²ₘ₊ₚ − D²ₘ
\[D_m^2 = 4m\lambda R\]
substitute by \(r_m = D_m/2\)
Diameters are measured with the travelling microscope, so write \(D_m = 2r_m\): \(D_m^2 = 4r_m^2\).
\[D_{m+p}^2 = 4(m+p)\lambda R\]
substitute by \(m \to m+p\)
The same result for the ring \(p\) orders further out.
\[D_{m+p}^2 - D_m^2 = 4p\lambda R\]
subtract by \(D_m^2 = 4m\lambda R\)
Subtracting removes \(m\), so the unknown order of a ring and any dust gap at \(O\) drop out (Eq. 15.67).
\[\lambda = \frac{D_{m+p}^2 - D_m^2}{4pR}\]
divide by \(4pR\)
Wavelength from two ring diameters and the lens radius (Eq. 15.68).
