Unit 1 · Fermat's principle; laws of reflection and refraction

① Theory — points

  • Geometrical optics: light treated as rays; valid when apertures ≫ wavelength (λ → 0 limit); ignores diffraction.
  • Refractive index \(n = c/v\): ratio of speed in vacuum to speed in the medium.
  • Optical path length = \(n \times\) geometric length; for varying \(n\): \(\int n\,ds\). It equals the distance light would cover in vacuum in the same time.
  • Fermat's principle: the actual ray path between two points is the one for which the optical path is stationary (\(\delta \int n\,ds = 0\)) — minimum, maximum or constant.
  • Minimum: plane mirror, refraction at a plane surface. Maximum: some concave reflectors. Stationary/constant: ellipsoidal mirror (all paths from one focus to the other equal).
  • Reflection: angle of incidence = angle of reflection, incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in one plane.
  • Refraction (Snell): \(n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2\); incident, refracted ray and normal coplanar.
  • Paraboloidal mirror focuses parallel rays exactly at its focus (all optical paths equal); ellipsoid images one focus onto the other.
  • Ray goes towards the normal on entering a denser medium (\(n_2 > n_1\)).
Must remember
\(\delta\int n\,ds = 0\)
\(\theta_i = \theta_r\)
\(n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2\)

② Key derivation

Laws of refraction from Fermat's principle
Light goes from fixed A in index n₁ to fixed B in index n₂, crossing the flat boundary PQ at R. M and N are the perpendicular feet of A and B on PQ. Keep AM = h₁, BN = h₂ and MN = L fixed; vary MR = x. Both angles θ₁ and θ₂ are measured from the normal at R. L_op denotes optical path, not the fixed distance L. The ray and normal lie in the plane through A, B and their perpendiculars to the boundary.
tap any step to show it on the figure
Optical path — sum optical paths n₁ AR and n₂ RB
\[L_{op} = n_1 AR + n_2 RB\]
Write total optical path length from \(A\) to \(B\) via point \(R\) on \(PQ\). Fermat: the actual \(R\) makes \(L_{op}\) stationary as \(R\) slides along \(PQ\).
First medium — write AR from right triangle AMR
\[AR = \sqrt{x^2 + h_1^2}\]
Pythagoras in right triangle \(AMR\)
\(M\) is the foot of the perpendicular from \(A\). Legs \(AM = h_1\) and \(MR = x\); hypotenuse \(AR\).
\[L_{op} = n_1\sqrt{x^2 + h_1^2} + n_2 RB\]
substitute by \(AR = \sqrt{x^2 + h_1^2}\)
Put it into the optical path.
Second medium — write RB from right triangle RBN
\[RB = \sqrt{(L - x)^2 + h_2^2}\]
Pythagoras in right triangle \(RNB\)
\(N\) is the foot of the perpendicular from \(B\), with \(MN = L\). Legs \(RN = L - x\) and \(NB = h_2\); hypotenuse \(RB\).
\[L_{op} = n_1\sqrt{x^2 + h_1^2} + n_2\sqrt{(L - x)^2 + h_2^2}\]
substitute by \(RB = \sqrt{(L - x)^2 + h_2^2}\)
Put it in too (Eq. 3.4). Only \(x\) changes as \(R\) moves.
Stationary path — differentiate L_op with respect to x and set to zero
\[\frac{dL_{op}}{dx} = \frac{n_1 x}{\sqrt{x^2 + h_1^2}} - \frac{n_2(L - x)}{\sqrt{(L - x)^2 + h_2^2}}\]
differentiate
Differentiate \(L_{op}\) with respect to \(x\); the chain rule on \((L - x)^2\) gives a minus sign.
\[0 = \frac{n_1 x}{\sqrt{x^2 + h_1^2}} - \frac{n_2(L - x)}{\sqrt{(L - x)^2 + h_2^2}}\]
evaluate by \(\frac{dL_{op}}{dx} = 0\)
Set \(dL_{op}/dx = 0\) for an extremum optical path (Eq. 3.5).
Incidence angle — substitute sin θ₁ in medium n₁
\[\sin\theta_1 = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + h_1^2}}\]
Sine in right triangle \(AMR\)
\(AM\) is parallel to the normal at \(R\), so \(\angle MAR = \theta_1\). The side opposite it is \(MR = x\); the hypotenuse is \(AR\).
\[0 = n_1\sin\theta_1 - \frac{n_2(L - x)}{\sqrt{(L - x)^2 + h_2^2}}\]
substitute by \(\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + h_1^2}} = \sin\theta_1\)
Replace the first fraction by \(\sin\theta_1\).
Refraction angle — substitute sin θ₂ in medium n₂ to reach Snell's law
\[\sin\theta_2 = \frac{L - x}{\sqrt{(L - x)^2 + h_2^2}}\]
Sine in right triangle \(RNB\)
\(NB\) is parallel to the normal, so \(\angle NBR = \theta_2\). Opposite it: \(RN = L - x\); hypotenuse: \(RB\).
\[0 = n_1\sin\theta_1 - n_2\sin\theta_2\]
substitute by \(\frac{L - x}{\sqrt{(L - x)^2 + h_2^2}} = \sin\theta_2\)
Replace the second fraction by \(\sin\theta_2\).
\[n_2\sin\theta_2 = n_1\sin\theta_1\]
add by \(n_2\sin\theta_2\)
Add \(n_2\sin\theta_2\) to both sides.
\[n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2\]
swap
Swap sides to obtain Snell's law of refraction (Eq. 3.6).

Unit 2 · Refraction and reflection at a single spherical surface

① Theory — points

  • Paraxial rays: rays close to the axis making small angles, so \(\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta \approx \theta\).
  • Sign convention (Cartesian): distances measured from the pole; positive to the right (direction of light), negative to the left; heights up positive.
  • Object distance \(u\) is negative for a real object on the left; radius \(R\) positive if the centre of curvature is to the right.
  • Gaussian formula for one refracting surface: \(\frac{n_2}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R}\).
  • Power of the surface \(P = (n_2 - n_1)/R\).
  • Derived from Fermat (optical path stationary) or from Snell's law with small angles.
  • Focal lengths: \(f_1 = -\frac{n_1 R}{n_2 - n_1}\) (object focus), \(f_2 = \frac{n_2 R}{n_2 - n_1}\) (image focus); \(\frac{f_2}{f_1} = -\frac{n_2}{n_1}\).
  • Spherical mirror (reflection = refraction with \(n_2 = -n_1\)): \(\frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v} = \frac{2}{R} = \frac{1}{f}\), so \(f = R/2\).
  • Concave mirror: centre of curvature on the left, \(R < 0\), so \(f < 0\) (real focus in front of the mirror).
Must remember
\(\frac{n_2}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R}\)
\(\frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v} = \frac{2}{R}\)

② Key derivation

The Gaussian formula for a single spherical surface
Light travels O → S → I from index n₁ into n₂. P is the surface pole, C its centre, and SC the normal at S. The positive lengths are OP = x, PI = y, PC = r and ray height SD = h. In this drawing O is left of P; C and I are right. Thus u = -x, v = y and R = r. Small angles α₁, α₂ are made with the axis; β is the tilt of SC. The surface sag PD is neglected in the paraxial approximation.
tap any step to show it on the figure
Snell's law — start from Snell's law and apply the paraxial approximation
\[n_1\sin\phi_1 = n_2\sin\phi_2\]
Start from Snell's law at point \(S\) on spherical surface \(SPM\) (Eq. 4.1). \(\phi_1\) is between the incident ray and the normal \(CS\); \(\phi_2\) is between the refracted ray and the normal.
\[n_1\phi_1 = n_2\phi_2\]
substitute by \(\sin\phi_1 \approx \phi_1,\,\sin\phi_2 \approx \phi_2\)
Use the paraxial approximation \(\sin\phi \approx \phi\) for small angles in radians.
Incident angle — express φ₁ from exterior angle of triangle OSC
\[n_1(\beta + \alpha_1) = n_2\phi_2\]
substitute by \(\phi_1 = \beta + \alpha_1\)
In triangle \(OSC\), exterior angle \(\phi_1\) equals \(\beta + \alpha_1\).
\[\tan\alpha_1 = \frac{h}{x}\]
Tangent in right triangle \(SDO\)
\(D\) is the foot of the perpendicular from \(S\). Opposite \(\alpha_1\): \(SD = h\). Adjacent: \(OD \approx OP = x\) (the sag \(PD\) is neglected for paraxial rays).
\[\tan\beta = \frac{h}{r}\]
Tangent in right triangle \(SDC\)
Opposite \(\beta\): \(SD = h\). Adjacent: \(DC \approx PC = r\).
\[n_1\left(\frac{h}{r} + \frac{h}{x}\right) = n_2\phi_2\]
substitute by \(\alpha_1 \approx \tan\alpha_1 = \frac{h}{x},\; \beta \approx \tan\beta = \frac{h}{r}\)
Small angles: each angle equals its tangent (Eq. 4.2).
Refracted angle — express φ₂ from exterior angle of triangle ISC
\[n_1\left(\frac{h}{r} + \frac{h}{x}\right) = n_2(\beta - \alpha_2)\]
substitute by \(\phi_2 = \beta - \alpha_2\)
In triangle \(ISC\), exterior angle \(\beta = \phi_2 + \alpha_2\), so \(\phi_2 = \beta - \alpha_2\).
\[\tan\alpha_2 = \frac{h}{y}\]
Tangent in right triangle \(SDI\)
Opposite \(\alpha_2\): \(SD = h\). Adjacent: \(DI \approx PI = y\).
\[n_1\left(\frac{h}{r} + \frac{h}{x}\right) = n_2\left(\frac{h}{r} - \frac{h}{y}\right)\]
substitute by \(\beta \approx \frac{h}{r},\; \alpha_2 \approx \tan\alpha_2 = \frac{h}{y}\)
Substitute \(\beta \approx h/r\) and \(\alpha_2 \approx \tan\alpha_2 = h/y\) (Eq. 4.3).
Cancel height — divide by h and collect distance terms
\[\frac{n_1}{r} + \frac{n_1}{x} = \frac{n_2}{r} - \frac{n_2}{y}\]
divide by \(h\)
Divide both sides by \(h\) so the ray height cancels out.
\[\frac{n_2}{y} + \frac{n_1}{x} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{r}\]
rearrange
Collect \(x, y\) terms on the left and \(r\) terms on the right (Eq. 4.4).
Sign convention — apply u = -x, v = y, and R = r
\[\frac{n_2}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R}\]
substitute by \(x = -u,\,y = v,\,r = R\)
Apply the sign convention \(u = -x, v = y, R = r\) to get the Gaussian formula (Eq. 4.5).

Unit 3 · Thin lens: foci, focal length, lensmaker's formula

① Theory — points

  • Thin lens: thickness negligible compared with radii and object/image distances; both surfaces treated at the same point.
  • Apply the single-surface formula twice: image of surface 1 becomes the object for surface 2.
  • Lens formula: \(\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}\).
  • Lensmaker's formula: \(\frac{1}{f} = \left(\frac{n_2}{n_1} - 1\right)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right)\); in air \(\frac1f = (n-1)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right)\).
  • First (object) focus \(F_1\): object point whose image is at infinity. Second (image) focus \(F_2\): image of an object at infinity.
  • Same medium on both sides: the two focal lengths are equal in magnitude, \(f_1 = -f_2\).
  • Different media \(n_1\) (left) and \(n_3\) (right): \(\frac{f_2}{f_1} = -\frac{n_3}{n_1}\).
  • Converging lens (f > 0) thicker at centre; diverging (f < 0) thinner at centre.
  • Power \(P = 1/f\) (in dioptres when f in metres); thin lenses in contact: \(P = P_1 + P_2\).
Must remember
\(\frac1v - \frac1u = \frac1f\)
\(\frac1f = (n-1)\left(\frac1{R_1} - \frac1{R_2}\right)\)

② Key derivation

The thin lens and Lensmaker's formula
Lens of index \(n_2\) in medium \(n_1\) with radii \(R_1, R_2\), intermediate image \(Q\) at \(v'\), final image \(I\) at \(v\), and \(n = n_2 / n_1\) (Fig. 4.5).
tap any step to show it on the figure
First surface — refraction at R₁ from n₁ into n₂ forms image Q at v'
\[\frac{n_2}{v'} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R_1}\]
First surface \(R_1\) refracts light from \(n_1\) into \(n_2\), forming \(Q\) at \(v'\) (Eq. 4.8). Alone, the first surface would bring the ray to \(Q\).
Second surface — refraction at R₂ from n₂ into n₁ takes Q at v' to I at v
\[\frac{n_1}{v} - \frac{n_2}{v'} = \frac{n_1 - n_2}{R_2}\]
Refraction at the second surface (Eq. 4.9)
Point \(Q\) at \(v'\) acts as object for second surface \(R_2\) from \(n_2\) into \(n_1\) (Eq. 4.9).
Combine surfaces — add the two surface equations to eliminate v'
\[\frac{n_1}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R_1} + \frac{n_1 - n_2}{R_2}\]
Add the two surface equations
Add Eqs. (4.8) and (4.9) so \(+n_2/v'\) and \(-n_2/v'\) cancel out.
\[\frac{n_1}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = (n_2 - n_1)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right)\]
factor
Factor \((n_2 - n_1)\) on the right, flipping the sign on \(1/R_2\).
Relative index — divide by n₁ and define n = n₂ / n₁
\[\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \left(\frac{n_2}{n_1} - 1\right)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right)\]
divide by \(n_1\)
Divide both sides by surrounding refractive index \(n_1\).
\[\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = (n - 1)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right)\]
substitute by \(n = \frac{n_2}{n_1}\)
Write \(n = n_2 / n_1\) to obtain the thin lens formula (Eq. 4.10).
Focal length — define focal length f by Lensmaker's formula
\[\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}\]
define by \(\frac{1}{f} = (n - 1)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right)\)
Define focal length \(f\) by Eq. (4.12) to get Eq. (4.11).

Unit 4 · Newton's formula and lateral magnification

① Theory — points

  • Newton's formula: distances measured from the foci: \(x_1 x_2 = f_1 f_2\); for a lens in air \(x_1 x_2 = -f^2\) (sign convention).
  • \(x_1\) = distance of object from first focus, \(x_2\) = distance of image from second focus.
  • Obtained by putting \(u = -(f + x_1)\), \(v = f + x_2\) in the lens formula (or from similar triangles).
  • Lateral magnification \(m = \frac{h_2}{h_1}\) (image height / object height).
  • Single spherical surface: \(m = \frac{n_1 v}{n_2 u}\). Thin lens in air: \(m = \frac{v}{u}\). Mirror: \(m = -\frac{v}{u}\).
  • In Newtonian form: \(m = -\frac{f}{x_1} = -\frac{x_2}{f}\).
  • \(m\) negative → image inverted; \(|m| > 1\) → magnified.
  • Derivation of \(m\): Snell's law at the pole with small angles, \(n_1 \frac{h_1}{u} = n_2 \frac{h_2}{v}\) (Lagrange-type relation).
Must remember
\(x_1 x_2 = f_1 f_2\)
\(m = \frac{h_2}{h_1} = \frac{n_1 v}{n_2 u}\)

② Key derivation

The Newton formula
A converging thin lens at P forms an inverted image B of object tip A. O and I are their feet on the axis. The signed heights are OA = y > 0 and IB = y′ < 0. K and L are the upper and lower ray crossings at the lens. F₁ and F₂ are the foci, at signed distances f₁ and f₂ from P. Measure x₁ from F₁ to O and x₂ from F₂ to I, positive rightward. In this drawing x₁ and f₁ are negative, x₂ and f₂ positive.
tap any step to show it on the figure
First focus — use similar triangles AOF₁ and LPF₁ at F₁
\[\tan\psi_1 = \frac{y}{-x_1}\]
Tangent in right triangle \(AOF_1\). Opposite \(\psi_1\) at \(F_1\): \(OA = y\). Adjacent: \(F_1O\), of length \(-x_1\) (\(x_1\) is negative here).
\[\tan\psi_1 = \frac{-y'}{-f_1}\]
The same angle in right triangle \(LPF_1\)
\(AL\) is one straight line through \(F_1\), so the angle at \(F_1\) in triangle \(LPF_1\) is the same \(\psi_1\) (vertically opposite). Opposite: \(PL\), of length \(-y'\); adjacent: \(F_1P\), of length \(-f_1\).
\[\frac{-y'}{y} = \frac{-f_1}{-x_1}\]
Equate the two tangents of \(\psi_1\)
Similar right triangles \(AOF_1\) and \(LPF_1\) share the angle at \(F_1\) (Eq. 4.17).
\[\frac{-y'}{y} = \frac{f_1}{x_1}\]
simplify
Cancel the minus signs in the numerator and denominator on the right.
Second focus — use similar triangles KPF₂ and BIF₂ at F₂
\[\tan\psi_2 = \frac{y}{f_2}\]
Tangent in right triangle \(KPF_2\)
The parallel ray meets the lens at \(K\), a height \(PK = y\). Adjacent to \(\psi_2\) at \(F_2\): \(PF_2 = f_2\).
\[\tan\psi_2 = \frac{-y'}{x_2}\]
The same angle in right triangle \(BIF_2\)
\(KB\) passes straight through \(F_2\), so the angle at \(F_2\) in triangle \(BIF_2\) is also \(\psi_2\). Opposite: \(IB = -y'\); adjacent: \(F_2I = x_2\).
\[\frac{-y'}{y} = \frac{x_2}{f_2}\]
Equate the two tangents of \(\psi_2\)
In similar triangles \(KPF_2\) and \(BIF_2\) around \(F_2\), \(-y'/y = x_2/f_2\) (Eq. 4.18).
Equate ratios — equate the two height ratios and cross-multiply
\[\frac{f_1}{x_1} = \frac{x_2}{f_2}\]
substitute by \(\frac{-y'}{y} = \frac{f_1}{x_1}\)
Equate the two expressions for \(-y'/y\) from Eqs. (4.17) and (4.18).
\[f_1 f_2 = x_1 x_2\]
multiply by \(x_1 f_2\)
Multiply both sides by \(x_1 f_2\) to get the Newtonian lens formula (Eq. 4.19).
Same medium — set f₁ = -f and f₂ = f for the same surrounding medium
\[-f^2 = x_1 x_2\]
substitute by \(f_1 = -f,\,f_2 = f\)
When both sides have the same medium, Eq. (4.13) gives \(f_1 = -f\) and \(f_2 = f\).
\[x_1 x_2 = -f^2\]
swap
Thus \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) must always have opposite signs (Eq. 4.20).
