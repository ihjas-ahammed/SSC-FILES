Unit 17 · Polarisation: types of polarised light

① Theory — points

  • Polarisation: removing the symmetry of a light wave about its direction of propagation; describes the orientation of the electric vector \(\vec E\).
  • Only transverse waves can be polarised → polarisation proves light is transverse (E ⟂ B ⟂ direction).
  • Unpolarised light: E vibrates in all planes ⟂ to the beam; equivalent to two incoherent perpendicular components of equal intensity.
  • Plane (linearly) polarised: E in a single plane; two coherent perpendicular components in phase or 180° out of phase.
  • Circularly polarised: two coherent perpendicular components, equal amplitudes, phase difference 90°; E has constant magnitude and rotates.
  • Elliptically polarised: unequal amplitudes and/or phase difference other than 0, π; E-tip traces an ellipse.
  • Right-handed: E rotates clockwise as seen by an observer looking back towards the source; left-handed: anticlockwise.
  • Partially polarised: mixture of plane-polarised and unpolarised light.
  • Plane of vibration (contains E) ⟂ plane of polarisation. Diagrams: dots = E ⟂ paper, arrows = E in the paper.
  • Two waves polarised at right angles do not interfere (Fresnel–Arago).
Must remember
\(E_y = E_1\cos\omega t,\quad E_z = E_2\cos(\omega t + \delta)\)
\(\delta = \tfrac{\pi}{2},\quad E_1 = E_2 \Rightarrow \text{circular}\)

② Key derivation

Linear, circular and elliptical polarisation from two perpendicular waves
Light travels toward you along \(x\). \(z\) and \(y\) are the two perpendicular directions in which \(\vec E\) can vibrate; \(E_z\), \(E_y\) are its components, \(\delta\) their phase difference, \(\omega\) the angular frequency. Amplitudes are drawn at convenient lengths. The book describes these cases in words (Figs 20.5 to 20.12); the equations make the same argument exact.

Two waves — two coherent perpendicular waves
\[E_z = E_1\cos\omega t\]
Resolve the light into two coherent waves along \(z\) and \(y\). The \(z\)-wave has amplitude \(E_1\).
\[E_y = E_2\cos(\omega t+\delta)\]
Add the \(y\)-wave with phase difference \(\delta\)
The \(y\)-wave has amplitude \(E_2\) and runs a phase \(\delta\) ahead of the \(z\)-wave.
In phase: line — in phase or 180° apart gives a fixed direction
\[E_y = \frac{E_2}{E_1}E_z\]
substitute by \(\delta = 0\)
With \(\delta=0\) both waves carry the same \(\cos\omega t\), so \(E_y\) is a fixed multiple of \(E_z\).
\[\tan\theta = \frac{E_2}{E_1}\]
Tangent in the right triangle with legs \(E_1\), \(E_2\)
In the right triangle the angle \(\theta\) of \(\vec E\) from the \(z\)-axis has opposite side \(E_2\) and adjacent side \(E_1\). It never changes.
\[E_y = -\frac{E_2}{E_1}E_z\]
substitute by \(\delta = \pi\)
For \(\delta=\pi\) (\(180^\circ\) out of phase) \(\cos(\omega t+\pi)=-\cos\omega t\). The line tilts the other way, still plane polarised.
Equal, 90° apart: circle — equal amplitudes, 90° apart: constant size, turning direction
\[E_z = E_0\cos\omega t\]
substitute by \(E_1 = E_0\)
Now take equal amplitudes: \(E_1=E_0\).
\[E_y = E_0\cos(\omega t-90^\circ)\]
substitute by \(E_2 = E_0,\ \delta = -90^\circ\)
Take \(E_2=E_0\) and put the \(y\)-wave \(90^\circ\) out of phase (\(\delta=-90^\circ\)).
\[E_y = E_0\sin\omega t\]
identity by \(\cos(x-90^\circ)=\sin x\)
A cosine delayed by a quarter cycle is a sine: when \(E_z\) is largest, \(E_y\) is zero.
\[E^2 = E_z^2 + E_y^2\]
Pythagoras in the right triangle \(E_z\), \(E_y\), \(E\)
The two components are the legs of a right triangle and the resultant \(E\) is its hypotenuse.
\[E^2 = E_0^2\cos^2\omega t + E_0^2\sin^2\omega t\]
substitute by \(E_z = E_0\cos\omega t,\ E_y = E_0\sin\omega t\)
Put in the two components.
\[E^2 = E_0^2\]
identity by \(\cos^2\omega t+\sin^2\omega t = 1\)
\(\cos^2+\sin^2=1\): the size of \(\vec E\) is constant, so its tip lies on a circle of radius \(E_0\).
\[\tan\varphi = \frac{E_y}{E_z}\]
Tangent in the right triangle with legs \(E_z\), \(E_y\)
Let \(\varphi\) be the angle of \(\vec E\) from the \(z\)-axis: opposite side \(E_y\), adjacent side \(E_z\).
\[\tan\varphi = \tan\omega t\]
substitute by \(\frac{E_y}{E_z} = \frac{\sin\omega t}{\cos\omega t}\)
\(E_y/E_z=\sin\omega t/\cos\omega t=\tan\omega t\).
\[\varphi = \omega t\]
apply
The angle grows steadily with time: \(\vec E\) turns at constant rate \(\omega\), a full turn per period. This is circular polarisation.
Unequal: ellipse — unequal amplitudes: elliptical
\[\left(\frac{E_z}{E_1}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{E_y}{E_2}\right)^2 = 1\]
identity by \(\cos^2\omega t+\sin^2\omega t = 1\)
If \(E_1\neq E_2\) (and \(\delta=-90^\circ\)), divide each component by its own amplitude: the tip traces an ellipse. Other phases \(\delta\) also give ellipses.

Unit 18 · Production of plane polarised light: pile of plates, scattering

① Theory — points

  • Brewster's law: at the polarising angle \(\tan\theta_B = \mu\); reflected and refracted rays are then perpendicular (\(\theta_B + \theta_r = 90^\circ\)).
  • At \(\theta_B\) the reflected light is completely plane polarised (E ⟂ plane of incidence); the transmitted light is only partially polarised.
  • Pile of plates: ~15 glass plates in a tube, at Brewster's angle to the beam (plates inclined ≈ 33° to the tube axis for glass, since \(\theta_B \approx 57^\circ\)).
  • Each surface reflects away part of the s-component (⟂ plane of incidence); after many plates the transmitted beam is nearly pure p-component — polarised parallel to the plane of incidence.
  • Polarisation by scattering: natural light scattered by ultramicroscopic particles is partially polarised.
  • An oscillating electron radiates most perpendicular to its vibration and nothing along it → light scattered at 90° is plane polarised.
  • Its E-vector is perpendicular to the plane containing the incident and the scattered directions.
  • Blue sky light is strongly polarised at 90° from the sun, but not completely because of multiple scattering.
Must remember
\(\tan\theta_B = \mu\)
\(\theta_B + \theta_r = 90^\circ\)
\(90^\circ - \theta_B \approx 33^\circ\)

Unit 19 · Polariser, analyser and Malus' law

① Theory — points

  • Polariser: converts unpolarised light into plane polarised light (Polaroid by selective absorption; Nicol prism by double refraction).
  • Analyser: identical device used to examine the light; characterised by its transmission axis.
  • Unpolarised light through an ideal polariser: \(I_1 = I_0/2\), whatever the orientation.
  • Malus' law (1809): \(I = I_1\cos^2\theta\), θ = angle between plane of vibration and analyser axis. Amplitude passed = \(E\cos\theta\).
  • \(\theta = 0^\circ, 180^\circ\): maximum; \(\theta = 90^\circ, 270^\circ\): zero (crossed polarisers).
  • Rotating an analyser: plane polarised → two maxima and two zeros per turn; partially polarised / elliptical → max and min, never zero; unpolarised / circular → constant.
  • Three polarisers: crossed pair with a third at 45° between them pass \(I_0/8\).
  • Degree of polarisation \(P = \frac{I_{max} - I_{min}}{I_{max} + I_{min}}\).
Must remember
\(I = I_1\cos^2\theta\)
\(I = \frac{I_0}{2}\cos^2\theta \quad (\text{unpolarised input})\)

② Key derivation

Malus's law for intensity transmitted by an analyser
Incident linearly polarised wave of amplitude \(E_0\) along \(x\) (intensity \(I_0 = K E_0^2\)) incident on analyser \(P_2\) at angle \(\theta\) (Eq. 22.9, Fig. 22.6).

\[E_\perp = E_0\sin\theta\]

The perpendicular component \(E_0\sin\theta\) is absorbed by \(P_2\), so only \(E_\parallel\) emerges.

\[E_\parallel^2 = E_0^2\cos^2\theta\]
Square both sides of E_∥ = E₀ cos θ
Square the emergent amplitude \(E_\parallel\) since intensity is proportional to amplitude squared.
Intensity ratio — multiply by proportionality constant K so I = K E_∥²
\[K E_\parallel^2 = K E_0^2\cos^2\theta\]

Multiply both sides by the intensity proportionality constant \(K\).
\[I = K E_0^2\cos^2\theta\]

Unit 20 · Double refraction and Huygens' theory

① Theory — points

  • Anisotropic crystal: refractive index depends on direction. Uniaxial: calcite, quartz, tourmaline; biaxial: mica, topaz.
  • Double refraction: an incident ray splits into an ordinary (o) ray obeying Snell's law and an extraordinary (e) ray that does not.
  • Both rays are plane polarised in mutually perpendicular planes: o vibrates ⟂ its principal section, e vibrates in it.
  • Optic axis: direction along which o and e travel with the same speed — no double refraction. In calcite it bisects a blunt corner.
  • Principal section: plane containing the optic axis and ⟂ to a pair of opposite faces. Principal plane: contains the optic axis and the o- or e-ray.
  • Huygens: o-wavelet = sphere (speed \(v_o\) all directions); e-wavelet = ellipsoid of revolution about the optic axis; they touch along the optic axis.
  • Negative crystal (calcite): \(\mu_e < \mu_o\), \(v_e > v_o\), ellipsoid outside sphere; \(\mu_e = c/(v_e)_{max}\).
  • Positive crystal (quartz): \(\mu_e > \mu_o\), \(v_e < v_o\), ellipsoid inside sphere; \(\mu_e = c/(v_e)_{min}\).
  • Wavefronts: common tangents to the wavelets. Optic axis ⟂ to the face (normal incidence): o and e travel together with the same speed — no separation.
  • Optic axis parallel to the face: both travel along the same direction but with different speeds → phase difference (basis of wave plates).
Must remember
\(\mu_o = \frac{c}{v_o}\)
\(\text{negative: } \mu_e = \frac{c}{(v_e)_{max}}\)
\(\text{positive: } \mu_e = \frac{c}{(v_e)_{min}}\)

② Key derivation

Extraordinary ray velocity surface and refractive index
This calculation describes the ray velocity surface used in Huygens' construction, not the phase-index surface. The optic axis is z. θ is the angle of the extraordinary energy ray to z, and ρ = v_re is its speed. The velocity ellipsoid has semiaxes a = c/n_o along z and b = c/n_e along x. Its section has coordinates z = ρ cos θ and x = ρ sin θ. Define ray index n_re = c/v_re. The starting velocity law is the textbook's material relation; the steps show why it gives an ellipsoid and how to obtain n_re.

Velocity law — start from the extraordinary ray velocity equation at angle θ
\[\frac{1}{v_{re}^2} = \frac{\sin^2\theta}{(c / n_e)^2} + \frac{\cos^2\theta}{(c / n_o)^2}\]
Start from the extraordinary ray velocity equation at angle \(\theta\) to the optic axis (Eq. 22.37).
Polar ellipse — set a = c/n_o, b = c/n_e, ρ = v_{re} and convert to Cartesian (z, x)
\[\frac{1}{\rho^2} = \frac{\cos^2\theta}{a^2} + \frac{\sin^2\theta}{b^2}\]
substitute by \(a = \frac{c}{n_o},\,b = \frac{c}{n_e},\,\rho = v_{re}\)
Set semi-axes \(a = c/n_o\), \(b = c/n_e\), and polar radius \(\rho = v_{re}\) (Eq. 22.39).
\[1 = \frac{\rho^2\cos^2\theta}{a^2} + \frac{\rho^2\sin^2\theta}{b^2}\]
multiply by \(\rho^2\)
Multiply both sides by \(\rho^2\).
\[1 = \frac{z^2}{a^2} + \frac{x^2}{b^2}\]
substitute by \(z = \rho\cos\theta,\,x = \rho\sin\theta\)
Thus plotting \(v_{re}(\theta)\) in 3D gives an ellipsoid of revolution (Eq. 22.38).
Ray index — multiply Eq. (22.37) by c² and substitute n_{re} = c / v_{re}
\[\frac{c^2}{v_{re}^2} = n_o^2\cos^2\theta + n_e^2\sin^2\theta\]
Multiply Eq. (22.37) by c²
Multiply both sides of Eq. (22.37) by \(c^2\) to express \((c/v_{re})^2\).
\[n_{re}^2 = n_o^2\cos^2\theta + n_e^2\sin^2\theta\]
substitute by \(n_{re} = \frac{c}{v_{re}}\)
Substitute \(n_{re} = c / v_{re}\) on the left-hand side.
Result — take the square root to obtain the extraordinary ray refractive index
\[n_{re} = \sqrt{n_o^2\cos^2\theta + n_e^2\sin^2\theta}\]
root
Take the positive square root to get the extraordinary ray refractive index (Eq. 22.45).

Unit 21 · Retarders: quarter- and half-wave plates; superposition of perpendicular waves

① Theory — points

  • Retarder: uniform birefringent plate with optic axis parallel to its faces; o and e travel normally through it with different speeds.
  • Optical path difference \((\mu_o - \mu_e)d\); phase difference \(\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mu_o - \mu_e)d\).
  • Quarter-wave plate: \((\mu_o - \mu_e)d = \lambda/4\), \(\delta = \pi/2\) → \(d = \frac{\lambda}{4(\mu_o - \mu_e)}\). Makes elliptical/circular light from plane light.
  • Half-wave plate: \((\mu_o - \mu_e)d = \lambda/2\), \(\delta = \pi\) → \(d = \frac{\lambda}{2(\mu_o - \mu_e)}\). Rotates the plane of plane light by \(2\theta\); reverses handedness.
  • The e and o waves are polarised at right angles → they do not form fringes; they combine into a polarisation state.
  • Superposition \(E_y = E_1\cos\phi\), \(E_z = E_2\cos(\phi + \delta)\) gives the general ellipse: \(\frac{y^2}{E_1^2} + \frac{z^2}{E_2^2} - \frac{2yz}{E_1E_2}\cos\delta = \sin^2\delta\).
  • \(\delta = 0\): \(z = \frac{E_2}{E_1}y\); \(\delta = \pi\): \(z = -\frac{E_2}{E_1}y\) — straight lines → plane polarised.
  • \(\delta = \pi/2\): \(\frac{y^2}{E_1^2} + \frac{z^2}{E_2^2} = 1\) — ellipse on the axes; circle if \(E_1 = E_2\).
  • Any other δ: oblique ellipse → elliptically polarised.
Must remember
\(\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mu_o - \mu_e)d\)
\(d_{QWP} = \frac{\lambda}{4(\mu_o - \mu_e)}\)
\(\frac{y^2}{E_1^2} + \frac{z^2}{E_2^2} - \frac{2yz}{E_1E_2}\cos\delta = \sin^2\delta\)

Bring the \(\sin^2\delta\) term from the right to the left and collect the two \(E_y^2\) terms (Eq. 20.42).
\[\cos^2\delta + \sin^2\delta = 1\]

The same right-triangle identity as before, now for the angle \(\delta\): hypotenuse 1, adjacent leg \(\cos\delta\), opposite leg \(\sin\delta\).

\[\frac{E_y^2}{E_1^2} + \frac{E_z^2}{E_2^2} - \frac{2E_yE_z}{E_1E_2}\cos\delta = \sin^2\delta\]

Unit 22 · Production and analysis of polarised light

① Theory — points

  • Elliptical light: polariser + QWP with E at \(\theta \neq 45^\circ\) to the optic axis; amplitudes \(E\cos\theta\) (e) and \(E\sin\theta\) (o) unequal, phase 90°.
  • Circular light: polariser + QWP with E at 45° to the optic axis → equal amplitudes, phase 90°.
  • Step 1: rotate an analyser in the beam.
  • • Two extinctions per turn → plane polarised. • Max and min, never zero → elliptical or partially polarised. • Constant → circular or unpolarised.
  • Step 2: put a QWP before the analyser and rotate the analyser again.
  • Elliptical (QWP axes along the ellipse axes): QWP adds 90° → total 0 or 180° → plane light → two extinctions. Partially polarised: still max and min, no zero.
  • Circular: QWP converts it into plane light → two complete extinctions. Unpolarised: intensity stays constant.
  • Analyser alone cannot distinguish circular from unpolarised, or elliptical from partially polarised — the QWP is essential.
Must remember
\(\theta = 45^\circ \Rightarrow E_o = E_e \Rightarrow \text{circular}\)
\(\theta \neq 45^\circ \Rightarrow \text{elliptical}\)

② Key derivation

When E_1 = E_2 = E_0 the box becomes a square and the ellipse a circle of radius E_0 (drawn with E_0 = E_2).

Put \(E_y = 0\): then \(E_z^2/E_2^2 = 1\). The curve crosses the \(z\)-axis at \(\pm E_2\).

substitute by \(\cos\delta = 0 \text{ in } \tan 2\alpha = \frac{2E_1E_2\cos\delta}{E_1^2-E_2^2}\)
The book's tilt formula \(\tan 2\alpha = 2E_1E_2\cos\delta/(E_1^2-E_2^2)\) also gives zero when \(\cos\delta = 0\), so \(\alpha = 0\). The major and minor axes coincide with the \(y\)- and \(z\)-axes: the light is elliptically polarised.

Equal amplitudes, each \(E_0\)
Now take equal amplitudes and call each one \(E_0\), with \(\delta = \pi/2\) still.
\[\frac{E_y^2}{E_0^2}+\frac{E_z^2}{E_0^2} = 1\]
substitute by \(E_1 = E_2 = E_0\)
Put \(E_1 = E_2 = E_0\) into the ellipse equation (20.46).
\[E_y^2 + E_z^2 = E_0^2\]
multiply by \(E_0^2\)
Multiply both sides by \(E_0^2\) (Eq. 20.47). This is a circle of radius \(E_0\), so the resultant light is circularly polarised.
