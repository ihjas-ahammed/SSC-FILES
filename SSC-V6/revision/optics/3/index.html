Unit 12 · Fraunhofer diffraction by a single slit

① Theory — points

  • Diffraction: bending/spreading of waves round obstacles and apertures\quad explained by Huygens–Fresnel: every point of a wavefront is a source of secondary wavelets that interfere.
  • Fraunhofer: source and screen effectively at infinity (plane wavefronts, lenses used). Fresnel: source and/or screen at finite distance (curved wavefronts).
  • Single slit of width \(b\): divide into strips, add with phase\quad \(\beta = \frac{\pi b\sin\theta}{\lambda}\).
  • Intensity: \(I = I_0\frac{\sin^2\beta}{\beta^2}\).
  • Minima: \(\beta = m\pi\) → \(b\sin\theta = m\lambda\), \(m = \pm1, \pm2, \dots\) (not \(m = 0\)).
  • Central maximum at \(\theta = 0\)\quad secondary maxima where \(\tan\beta = \beta\): \(\beta \approx 1.43\pi, 2.46\pi, \dots\)
  • Secondary maxima intensities ≈ 4.5 %, 1.6 %, 0.8 % of the central one.
  • Central maximum is twice as wide as the others\quad angular half-width \(\sin\theta = \lambda/b\) — narrower slit → wider pattern.
  • Linear half-width on the focal plane of a lens of focal length \(f\): \(x = f\lambda/b\).
Must remember
\(I = I_0\frac{\sin^2\beta}{\beta^2},\quad \beta = \frac{\pi b\sin\theta}{\lambda}\)
\(b\sin\theta = m\lambda\quad(\text{minima})\)
\(\tan\beta = \beta\)

② Key derivation

Single-slit Fraunhofer amplitude and intensity
Uniform light of wavelength λ falls normally on slit width b. Replace the slit by n points separated by Δ, so b = (n-1)Δ. Each contributes field amplitude a at P. The lens brings rays leaving at angle θ to the same point P. Their adjacent phase step is φ. Define β = πb sin θ/λ, half the phase difference across the slit. In the continuous limit a tends to zero while A = na stays finite. I₀ is the on-axis intensity of this one slit.
tap any step to show it on the figure
Phase step — right triangle A₁A₂K gives the path difference Δ sin θ, then the phase step φ
\[\sin\theta = \frac{A_2K}{\Delta}\]
Sine in right triangle \(A_1A_2K\). \(K\) is the foot of the perpendicular from \(A_1\) on the ray from \(A_2\). \(A_1K\) is a wavefront across the parallel rays, so the angle at \(A_1\) equals \(\theta\). The side opposite \(\theta\) is \(A_2K\), the hypotenuse is \(A_1A_2 = \Delta\).
\[A_2K = \Delta\sin\theta\]
Rearrange: multiply by \(\Delta\)
Multiply by \(\Delta\). Beyond the wavefront \(A_1K\) the two rays travel equal optical paths to \(P\) (the lens adds no path difference), so \(A_2K\) is the whole path difference.
\[\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta\sin\theta\]
Path difference to phase: multiply by \(2\pi/\lambda\)
A path difference of \(\lambda\) is a phase of \(2\pi\), so adjacent sources differ in phase by \(\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta\sin\theta\) (Eq. 18.2).
Sum wavelets — add the n cosine waves and sum the trigonometric series
\[E = a[\cos\omega t + \cos(\omega t - \phi) + \dots + \cos(\omega t - (n - 1)\phi)]\]
Superpose the n secondary wavelets at P (Eq. 18.3)
Each point source sends a wave of amplitude \(a\) shifted in phase by \(\phi\) from its neighbour (Eq. 18.3).
\[E_\theta = a\frac{\sin(n\phi / 2)}{\sin(\phi / 2)}\]
identity by \(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\cos(\omega t - k\phi) = \frac{\sin(n\phi/2)}{\sin(\phi/2)}\cos(\omega t - \tfrac{1}{2}(n-1)\phi)\)
Write the resultant amplitude \(E_\theta\) of the \(n\) interfering point sources (Eq. 18.6).
Continuous limit — let n → ∞ and Δ → 0 with nΔ → b so nφ/2 → β
\[\frac{n\phi}{2}\longrightarrow\frac{\pi b\sin\theta}{\lambda}\]
substitute by \(n\Delta \to b\)
This is a limiting relation: finite n gives nΔ rather than exactly b.
\[\beta = \frac{\pi b\sin\theta}{\lambda}\]
define by \(\beta = \frac{\pi b\sin\theta}{\lambda}\)
Define the dimensionless diffraction variable \(\beta\) as half the phase difference across the whole slit (Eq. 18.8).
\[E_\theta = A\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\]
substitute by \(\sin(\phi / 2) \approx \frac{\beta}{n},\,A = na\)
Replace \(\sin(\phi/2)\) by \(\beta/n\) and set \(A = na\) for the central amplitude at \(\theta = 0\) (Eq. 18.7).
\[E = A\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\cos(\omega t - \beta)\]
Include the harmonic time factor cos(ωt - β) (Eq. 18.9)
Since \(\frac{1}{2}(n-1)\phi \to \beta\), the resultant electric field at \(P\) is Eq. (18.9).
Intensity law — square the field amplitude to obtain the sinc-squared intensity
\[I = I_0\frac{\sin^2\beta}{\beta^2}\]
Square the amplitude and set I₀ ∝ A² (Eq. 18.10)
This is the single-slit Fraunhofer intensity distribution, where \(I_0\) is the intensity at \(\theta = 0\) (Eq. 18.10).
\[\beta = m\pi\]
Zeros of intensity
\(I = 0\) where \(\sin\beta = 0\) but \(\beta \neq 0\) (at \(\beta = 0\) the ratio \(\sin\beta/\beta \to 1\): the central maximum). So \(\beta = m\pi\), \(m = \pm1, \pm2, \ldots\)
\[b\sin\theta = m\lambda\]
substitute by \(\beta = \frac{\pi b\sin\theta}{\lambda}\)
Put in \(\beta\) and multiply by \(\lambda/\pi\): the minima of the single-slit pattern.
\[\frac{dI}{d\beta}=2I_0\frac{\sin\beta(\beta\cos\beta-\sin\beta)}{\beta^3}\]
Differentiate \(I = I_0\sin^2\beta/\beta^2\) with respect to \(\beta\)
Differentiate the intensity using the product and quotient rules, away from β = 0.
\[\beta\cos\beta-\sin\beta=0\]
rearrange
For a nonzero side maximum, sin β is not zero, so set the other factor to zero.
\[\tan\beta=\beta\]
Divide by \(\cos\beta\)
Divide by cos β to locate the side peaks; the central value is found separately by a limit.

Unit 13 · Fraunhofer diffraction by two slits

① Theory — points

  • Two slits each of width \(b\), centre-to-centre separation \(d\).
  • Intensity: \(I = 4I_0\frac{\sin^2\beta}{\beta^2}\cos^2\gamma\), with \(\beta = \frac{\pi b\sin\theta}{\lambda}\), \(\gamma = \frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}\).
  • \(\frac{\sin^2\beta}{\beta^2}\): diffraction term (single slit envelope). \(\cos^2\gamma\): interference term (two-slit fringes).
  • Interference maxima: \(d\sin\theta = n\lambda\)\quad interference minima: \(d\sin\theta = (n + \tfrac12)\lambda\).
  • Diffraction minima: \(b\sin\theta = m\lambda\).
  • Missing orders: an interference maximum falls on a diffraction minimum when \(\frac{d}{b} = \frac{n}{m}\)\quad e.g. \(d = 2b\) → orders 2, 4, 6 … missing.
  • Number of interference fringes inside the central diffraction maximum ≈ \(2d/b\) (minus missing orders at the edges).
  • As \(b \to 0\): pattern → Young's \(\cos^2\) fringes of equal intensity.
Must remember
\(I = 4I_0\frac{\sin^2\beta}{\beta^2}\cos^2\gamma\)
\(\gamma = \frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}\)
\(\frac{d}{b} = \frac{n}{m}\quad(\text{missing orders})\)

② Key derivation

Two-slit Fraunhofer field and intensity distribution
Two slits of width \(b\) separated by distance \(d\), phase lag \(\Phi_1 = 2\gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d\sin\theta\) between corresponding points \((A_1, B_1)\) (Eqs. 18.44–18.45, Fig. 18.29).
tap any step to show it on the figure
Slit fields — write the diffracted field E₁ from the first slit of width b
\[E_1 = A\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\cos(\omega t - \beta)\]
The first slit of width \(b\) produces the single-slit field \(E_1\) at point \(P\) (Eq. 18.9).
Phase lag — right triangle A₁B₁K gives d sin θ, so slit 2 lags by Φ₁
\[\sin\theta = \frac{B_1K}{d}\]
Sine in right triangle \(A_1B_1K\)
\(A_1\) and \(B_1\) are corresponding points (the same place in each slit), \(d\) apart. \(K\) is the foot of the perpendicular from \(A_1\) on the ray from \(B_1\), so \(A_1K\) is a wavefront and the angle at \(A_1\) is \(\theta\). Opposite \(\theta\): \(B_1K\); hypotenuse: \(d\).
\[B_1K = d\sin\theta\]
Rearrange: multiply by \(d\)
Multiply by \(d\). Past the wavefront \(A_1K\) both rays have equal paths to \(P\), so \(B_1K\) is the path difference between corresponding points.
\[\Phi_1 = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d\sin\theta\]
Path difference to phase: multiply by \(2\pi/\lambda\)
Every point of slit 2 lags its corresponding point in slit 1 by the same phase \(\Phi_1 = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d\sin\theta\).
\[E_2 = A\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\cos(\omega t - \beta - \Phi_1)\]
Slit 2: the same field, delayed by \(\Phi_1\)
Slit 2 has the same width \(b\), so it gives the same single-slit amplitude, only \(\Phi_1\) behind.
Superpose — add E₁ + E₂ and factor with the cosine sum identity
\[E = A\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}[\cos(\omega t - \beta) + \cos(\omega t - \beta - \Phi_1)]\]
Add the two fields: \(E = E_1 + E_2\)
Superpose \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) and factor out the common single-slit amplitude \(A(\sin\beta/\beta)\).
\[E = 2A\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\cos\left(\frac{\Phi_1}{2}\right)\cos\left(\omega t - \beta - \frac{1}{2}\Phi_1\right)\]
identity by \(\cos C + \cos D = 2\cos\tfrac{C-D}{2}\cos\tfrac{C+D}{2}\)
Combine the two cosines using half the phase difference \(\Phi_1 / 2\).
Product law — define γ = Φ₁/2 and square the amplitude to get intensity
\[\gamma = \frac{\pi}{\lambda}d\sin\theta\]
define by \(\gamma = \frac{\Phi_1}{2} = \frac{\pi}{\lambda}d\sin\theta\)
Define the interference phase parameter \(\gamma = \Phi_1 / 2\) (Eq. 18.44).
\[E = 2A\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\cos\gamma\cos(\omega t - \beta - \gamma)\]
substitute by \(\frac{\Phi_1}{2} = \gamma\)
Substitute \(\gamma = \Phi_1 / 2\) into the resultant electric field.
\[I = 4I_0\frac{\sin^2\beta}{\beta^2}\cos^2\gamma\]
Square the amplitude and set I₀ ∝ A² (Eq. 18.45)
The resultant intensity is the product of the single-slit diffraction pattern and two-slit interference pattern (Eq. 18.45).

Unit 14 · N-slit diffraction and the plane grating

① Theory — points

  • N parallel slits (width \(b\), spacing \(d\)): \(I = I_0\frac{\sin^2\beta}{\beta^2}\frac{\sin^2 N\gamma}{\sin^2\gamma}\).
  • Principal maxima: \(\gamma = n\pi\) → \(d\sin\theta = n\lambda\) (grating equation)\quad intensity \(\propto N^2\).
  • Minima: \(N\gamma = p\pi\), \(p \neq 0, N, 2N, \dots\) → \(N - 1\) minima and \(N - 2\) secondary maxima between principal maxima.
  • Larger N → principal maxima sharper and brighter\quad angular half-width \(\Delta\theta = \frac{\lambda}{Nd\cos\theta}\).
  • Plane diffraction grating: many equidistant slits\quad \(d = (a + b)\) = grating element\quad lines per unit length \(= 1/d\).
  • Maximum order: \(n_{max} < d/\lambda\) (since \(\sin\theta \le 1\)). Oblique incidence: \(d(\sin\theta + \sin i) = n\lambda\).
  • Angular dispersion: \(\frac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = \frac{n}{d\cos\theta}\) — greater in higher order, finer grating.
  • Resolving power: \(R = \frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = nN\) (Rayleigh criterion: max of one on first min of the other).
  • Grating spectra: violet deviated least, red most (opposite to prism)\quad several orders formed.
Must remember
\(d\sin\theta = n\lambda\)
\(\frac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = \frac{n}{d\cos\theta}\)
\(\frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = nN\)

② Key derivation

N-slit intensity, principal maxima, and angular half-width
N identical slits of width b and centre spacing d are illuminated equally and in phase at normal incidence. A is the on-axis amplitude from one slit and I₀ its intensity. Define β = πb sin θ/λ, γ = πd sin θ/λ and Φ₁ = 2γ. At order m, d sin θ_m = mλ. The half-width Δθ_m is the angular distance from the narrow principal peak to its nearest zero, assuming large N; it is not the width at half peak intensity.
tap any step to show it on the figure
Sum N slits — right triangle S₁S₂K gives the lag d sin θ; add the N fields
\[\sin\theta = \frac{S_2K}{d}\]
Sine in right triangle \(S_1S_2K\). Neighbouring slits are \(d\) apart. \(K\) is the foot of the perpendicular from \(S_1\) on the ray from \(S_2\), so the angle at \(S_1\) is \(\theta\): \(S_2K\) is opposite it and \(d\) is the hypotenuse.
\[\Phi_1 = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d\sin\theta\]
Path difference \(S_2K = d\sin\theta\), times \(2\pi/\lambda\)
Each slit lags the one above by the path \(d\sin\theta\), that is by the phase \(\Phi_1 = 2\gamma\).
\[E = A\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}[\cos(\omega t - \beta) + \cos(\omega t - \beta - \Phi_1) + \dots + \cos(\omega t - \beta - (N - 1)\Phi_1)]\]
Add the \(N\) slit fields at \(P\) (Eq. 18.48)
Sum the diffracted fields from all \(N\) parallel slits at screen point \(P\) (Eq. 18.48).
\[E = A\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\frac{\sin N\gamma}{\sin\gamma}\cos\left[\omega t - \beta - \frac{1}{2}(N - 1)\Phi_1\right]\]
identity by \(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\cos(\alpha - 2k\gamma) = \frac{\sin N\gamma}{\sin\gamma}\cos(\alpha - (N-1)\gamma)\)
With \(\gamma = \Phi_1 / 2 = (\pi d\sin\theta)/\lambda\), sum the \(N\) cosines into closed form (Eq. 18.49).
\[I = I_0\frac{\sin^2\beta}{\beta^2}\frac{\sin^2 N\gamma}{\sin^2\gamma}\]
Square the amplitude to obtain intensity I (Eq. 18.50)
This reduces to Eq. (18.10) for \(N = 1\) and to Eq. (18.45) for \(N = 2\) (Eq. 18.50).
Principal maxima — take the limit γ → mπ using L'Hôpital's rule to find peak intensity
\[I_{\max} = N^2 I_0\frac{\sin^2\beta}{\beta^2}\]
evaluate by \(\gamma \to m\pi\)
At \(\gamma = m\pi\) (\(d\sin\theta_m = m\lambda\)), \(\lim_{\gamma\to m\pi}\frac{\sin N\gamma}{\sin\gamma} = \pm N\), so peak intensity scales as \(N^2\) (Eq. 18.53).
Adjacent minimum — write the condition for the first minimum at θ_m ± Δθ_m
\[d\sin(\theta_m \pm \Delta\theta_m) = m\lambda \pm \frac{\lambda}{N}\]
First minimum beside the \(m\)-th maximum (Eq. 18.61)
The first secondary minimum beside \(d\sin\theta_m = m\lambda\) occurs at path difference \(m\lambda \pm \lambda/N\) (Eq. 18.61).
Angular half-width — expand sin(θ_m ± Δθ_m) for small Δθ_m and solve for Δθ_m
\[\sin(\theta_m \pm \Delta\theta_m) = \sin\theta_m\cos\Delta\theta_m \pm \cos\theta_m\sin\Delta\theta_m\]
Sine of a sum
Expand the sine of \(\theta_m \pm \Delta\theta_m\) before making any approximation.
\[\sin(\theta_m \pm \Delta\theta_m) \approx \sin\theta_m \pm \Delta\theta_m\cos\theta_m\]
substitute by \(\cos\Delta\theta_m \approx 1,\; \sin\Delta\theta_m \approx \Delta\theta_m\)
\(\Delta\theta_m\) is very small for large \(N\), so \(\cos\Delta\theta_m \approx 1\) and \(\sin\Delta\theta_m \approx \Delta\theta_m\) (Eq. 18.62).
\[d\sin\theta_m \pm d\Delta\theta_m\cos\theta_m = m\lambda \pm \frac{\lambda}{N}\]
Apply it to the minimum condition
Substitute the small-angle expansion Eq. (18.62) into Eq. (18.61).
\[d\Delta\theta_m\cos\theta_m = \frac{\lambda}{N}\]
subtract by \(d\sin\theta_m = m\lambda\)
Cancel \(d\sin\theta_m = m\lambda\) from both sides and take the positive sign.
\[\Delta\theta_m = \frac{\lambda}{Nd\cos\theta_m}\]
divide by \(d\cos\theta_m\)
Divide by d cos θ_m; the factor N was already in the denominator on the right, giving the half-width to the first zero, not the half-intensity width.

Unit 15 · Fresnel half-period zones and the zone plate

① Theory — points

  • Fresnel half-period zones: wavefront divided into annular zones whose distances from P differ successively by \(\lambda/2\)\quad adjacent zones contribute in opposite phase.
  • Radius of n-th zone: \(r_n = \sqrt{n\lambda d}\) (plane wave, P at distance d)\quad \(r_n \propto \sqrt{n}\).
  • Area of each zone \(\approx \pi\lambda d\): nearly equal, so amplitudes fall only slowly (obliquity).
  • Resultant amplitude: \(A = a_1 - a_2 + a_3 - \dots \approx \frac{a_1}{2}\) — the whole wavefront gives half the first zone's amplitude (Schuster's method).
  • Rectilinear propagation explained: \(\lambda\) small → zones tiny, obstacle blocks many zones.
  • Zone plate: alternate zones blackened\quad transparent zones add in phase → bright focus.
  • Zone plate acts like a converging lens: \(\frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b} = \frac{n\lambda}{r_n^2}\); principal focal length \(f = \frac{r_n^2}{n\lambda} = \frac{r_1^2}{\lambda}\).
  • Unlike a lens it has multiple foci at \(f/3, f/5, \dots\)\quad focal length \(\propto 1/\lambda\) (violet focuses farther — opposite to a lens).
  • Circular aperture/disc: centre bright or dark depending on whether an odd or even number of zones is exposed\quad centre of a disc's shadow always bright (Poisson spot).
Must remember
\(r_n = \sqrt{n\lambda d}\)
\(A_n \approx \pi\lambda d\)
\(f = \frac{r_1^2}{\lambda}\)

② Key derivation

Radius, equal area, and Schuster sum of Fresnel half-period zones
A normally incident plane wave meets wavefront WW′. O is its point nearest the on-axis observer P, with OP = d. Draw circles whose rim distances to P are d+nλ/2. Their radii are r_n, with r₀ = 0. Assume nλ ≪ d, so the zones have nearly equal areas A_n. Adjacent zone contributions alternate in phase; u_n denotes their positive amplitude magnitude. The Schuster sum below is an approximation using slowly decreasing u_n, not an exact equality of every neighbouring contribution.

\[r_n^2 = n\lambda d + \frac{n^2\lambda^2}{4}\]

\[r_n\approx\sqrt{n\lambda d}\]

For nλ ≪ d, neglect n²λ²/4 compared with nλd, then take the positive root.

\[A_n = \pi r_n^2 - \pi r_{n-1}^2\]
Write area A_n of the n-th annular ring (Eq. 20.2)
The \(n\)-th half-period zone is the annular region between the \(n\)-th and \((n-1)\)-th circles.
\[A_n\approx\pi\lambda d\]

\[u(P) = u_1 - u_2 + u_3 - u_4 + \dots\]

Due to increased obliquity \(\chi\), amplitudes decrease slowly: \(u_1 > u_2 > u_3 > \dots\) (Eqs. 20.3–20.5).

\[u(P) = \frac{u_1}{2} + \left(\frac{u_1}{2} - u_2 + \frac{u_3}{2}\right) + \left(\frac{u_3}{2} - u_4 + \frac{u_5}{2}\right) + \dots\]

Rewrite Eq. (20.3) by splitting odd terms into halves and bracketing around each even term (Eq. 20.6).
\[u(P)\approx\frac{u_1}{2}\]
Use u_n ≈ (u_{n-1} + u_{n+1})/2 and u_m → 0 (Eq. 20.12)
For a full wavefront the distant-zone contribution tends to zero; the nearly cancelling brackets leave approximately u₁/2.

Unit 16 · Fresnel diffraction by a straight edge

① Theory — points

  • Straight edge: a plane wave partly blocked by an opaque edge\quad observed at finite distance — Fresnel diffraction.
  • Use the dimensionless variable \(v = x\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda d}}\) (x = distance from geometrical shadow edge, d = edge-to-screen distance).
  • Fresnel integrals \(C(v) = \int_0^v\cos\frac{\pi u^2}{2}du\), \(S(v) = \int_0^v\sin\frac{\pi u^2}{2}du\)\quad \(C(\infty) = S(\infty) = \tfrac12\).
  • Intensity: \(I = \frac{I_0}{2}\left\{\left[\tfrac12 - C(v_0)\right]^2 + \left[\tfrac12 - S(v_0)\right]^2\right\}\) (Ghatak's form).
  • Cornu spiral: plot of \(S\) vs \(C\)\quad amplitude ∝ length of the chord from the point \(v_0\) to the spiral's end \((\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\).
  • At the geometrical shadow edge (\(v = 0\)): \(I = I_0/4\) (amplitude half).
  • Inside the shadow: intensity falls off smoothly and monotonically — no fringes.
  • Illuminated side: fringes of decreasing contrast\quad maximum ≈ \(1.37I_0\) just outside the edge\quad fringes get closer away from the edge.
  • Fringe positions scale with \(\sqrt{\lambda d}\).
Must remember
\(v = x\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda d}}\)
\(I(0) = \frac{I_0}{4}\)
\(I_{max} \approx 1.37 I_0\)

② Key derivation

Straight-edge Fresnel diffraction field and intensity
A plane wave of wavelength λ meets an opaque half-plane; only η ≥ 0 is open. M has coordinates (ξ,η) in that plane. The screen is distance d away, and P is height y above the geometric shadow boundary O. Thus y > 0 is the lit side. Use k = 2π/λ and i² = -1. The complex field u(P) encodes amplitude and phase; intensity is proportional to |u(P)|². Do not confuse this field with the dimensionless integration variable u. Define u = √(2/(λd))ξ and v = √(2/(λd))(η-y); the lower limit v₀ is at η = 0.

Paraxial distance — Pythagoras for r = MP, then the binomial approximation for d ≫ ξ, η − y
\[r = \left[\xi^2 + (\eta - y)^2 + d^2\right]^{1/2}\]

\[r = d\left[1 + \frac{\xi^2 + (\eta - y)^2}{d^2}\right]^{1/2}\]
Take \(d^2\) out of the bracket
Factor \(d^2\) out of the square root, so the bracket is \(1\) plus a small quantity \(x = [\xi^2 + (\eta - y)^2]/d^2\).
\[(1 + x)^{1/2} \approx 1 + \frac{x}{2}\]

\[r\approx d+\frac{\xi^2+(\eta-y)^2}{2d}\]
substitute by \((1 + x)^{1/2} \approx 1 + \tfrac{x}{2}\)

Add the wavelets from the open half-plane
The Fresnel diffraction integral adds the phase contributions from the open half-plane; replace the slow amplitude factor 1/r by 1/d.
Fresnel variables — define dimensionless variables u, v and lower limit v₀ at η = 0
\[v = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda d}}\,(\eta - y)\]
define by \(u = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda d}}\,\xi\)
Dimensionless variables make the phase \(k[\xi^2 + (\eta - y)^2]/2d\) equal to \(\pi(u^2 + v^2)/2\) (Eq. 20.51).
\[v_0 = -\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda d}}\,y\]
substitute by \(\eta = 0\)
At the straight edge \(\eta = 0\), the lower limit of \(v\) is \(v_0 = -\sqrt{2/(\lambda d)}\,y\) (Eqs. 20.51, 20.53).
\[d\xi\,d\eta=\frac{\lambda d}{2}\,du\,dv\]
Change of variables in the area element
Each coordinate substitution contributes √(λd/2), giving this area factor.
\[u(P) = -\frac{i}{2}u_0\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\exp\left(\frac{i\pi u^2}{2}\right)du\int_{v_0}^{\infty}\exp\left(\frac{i\pi v^2}{2}\right)dv\]
Write the Fresnel diffraction integral over u and v (Eq. 20.52)
