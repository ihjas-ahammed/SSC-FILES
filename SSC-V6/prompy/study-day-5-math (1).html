1.0 High School (Plus Two) Prerequisite Recall

Warm-Up Session

Before starting university-level Complex Analysis, let us quickly recall four simple tools from Plus Two mathematics that we use on every page today.

calculate1. Quadratic Formula & Negative Discriminant

For $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, the roots are $x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$. In school algebra, when $b^2 - 4ac < 0$ (such as $x^2 + 2x + 2 = 0$), we had $\sqrt{-1}$, which has no solution on the real number line $\mathbb{R}$.

straighten2. Distance & Pythagoras Theorem

In the $xy$-plane, a point $(x,y)$ is at distance $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ from the origin $(0,0)$. The distance between $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ is $\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}$.

architecture3. Trigonometry Addition Formulas

For any two angles $A$ and $B$: $$\cos(A \pm B) = \cos A\cos B \mp \sin A\sin B$$ $$\sin(A \pm B) = \sin A\cos B \pm \cos A\sin B$$

functions4. Binomial Expansion & Difference of Squares

$(A+B)(A-B) = A^2 - B^2$, and for positive integers $n$: $$(A+B)^n = A^n + \binom{n}{1}A^{n-1}B + \binom{n}{2}A^{n-2}B^2 + \dots + B^n$$

1.1 Complex Numbers and Their Properties

Zill Section 1.1 (pp. 3–7)

In the 16th century, mathematicians trying to solve equations like $x^2 + 2x + 2 = 0$ and $x^3 = 6x + 4$ encountered square roots of negative numbers. Later, Carl Friedrich Gauss and Augustin Louis Cauchy put these numbers on a solid logical foundation by defining the imaginary unit $i$ through the rule: $$i^2 = -1$$

menu_bookDefinition 1.1.1 & 1.1.2: Complex Number, Parts, and Equality

A complex number is any number of the form $z = a + ib$ (or $a + bi$), where $a$ and $b$ are real numbers and $i$ is the imaginary unit ($i^2 = -1$).

  • Real part: $\text{Re}(z) = a$ (a real number).
  • Imaginary part: $\text{Im}(z) = b$ (also a real number!).
  • Pure imaginary number: When $a = 0$ and $b \neq 0$, such as $z = 6i$.
  • Equality (Def 1.1.2): $z_1 = a_1 + ib_1$ and $z_2 = a_2 + ib_2$ are equal ($z_1 = z_2$) if and only if $a_1 = a_2$ and $b_1 = b_2$ (that is, $\text{Re}(z_1)=\text{Re}(z_2)$ and $\text{Im}(z_1)=\text{Im}(z_2)$).
warning_amberImportant Exam Warning on $\text{Im}(z)$ and Order

1. Imaginary part has no $i$: For $z = 4 - 9i$, $\text{Re}(z) = 4$ and $\text{Im}(z) = -9$ (never write $-9i$).
2. No inequality order in $\mathbb{C}$: Unlike real numbers, we cannot compare two non-real complex numbers using $<$ or $>$. Writing $2 + 3i < 5 + 8i$ is meaningless in $\mathbb{C}$! Only moduli $|z_1| < |z_2|$ or real numbers can be compared.

boltEveryday Kerala Example: Two-Counter Bakery Bill & AC Power

Think of a bakery counter in Ernakulam where a customer buys tea (tracked in the first column $a$) and snacks (tracked in the second column $b$). Two bills $z_1$ and $z_2$ are identical only if both the tea amount matches ($a_1 = a_2$) and the snack amount matches ($b_1 = b_2$). In electrical engineering across the Kerala power grid, voltage and impedance are written as $Z = R + iX$ (resistance $R$ plus reactance $X$), where $i^2 = -1$ tracks the $90^\circ$ phase shift of alternating current!

Arithmetic Operations, Zero, Unity, Inverses, and Conjugate

For $z_1 = a_1 + ib_1$ and $z_2 = a_2 + ib_2$, complex numbers obey the familiar commutative, associative, and distributive laws:

add_circle_outlineAddition, Subtraction & Multiplication

Add/Subtract: Combine real parts and combine imaginary parts: $$z_1 \pm z_2 = (a_1 \pm a_2) + i(b_1 \pm b_2)$$ Multiply: Expand using distributive law and replace $i^2 = -1$: $$z_1 z_2 = (a_1a_2 - b_1b_2) + i(b_1a_2 + a_1b_2)$$ Example 1: $(2+4i)(-3+8i) = -6 + 16i - 12i + 32i^2 = -38 + 4i$.

sync_altZero, Unity, Conjugate & Inverses

Zero (Additive Identity): $0 = 0 + 0i$, with $z + 0 = z$. Additive inverse of $z=a+ib$ is $-z = -a - ib$.
Unity (Multiplicative Identity): $1 = 1 + 0i$, with $z \cdot 1 = z$.
Conjugate $\bar{z}$: Flip the sign of the imaginary part: $\bar{z} = a - ib$.
$\overline{z_1 \pm z_2} = \bar{z}_1 \pm \bar{z}_2$, $\overline{z_1z_2} = \bar{z}_1\bar{z}_2$, $\overline{(z_1/z_2)} = \bar{z}_1/\bar{z}_2$, $\bar{\bar{z}} = z$.

verifiedEssential Conjugate Identities (Equations 3–6 in Zill)

For any $z = a + ib$ and its conjugate $\bar{z} = a - ib$: $$z + \bar{z} = 2a \implies \text{Re}(z) = \frac{z + \bar{z}}{2}, \qquad z - \bar{z} = 2ib \implies \text{Im}(z) = \frac{z - \bar{z}}{2i}$$ $$z\bar{z} = (a + ib)(a - ib) = a^2 - i^2b^2 = a^2 + b^2 \quad (\text{always a non-negative real number!})$$

1.2 Complex Plane, Modulus, and Triangle Inequality

Zill Section 1.2 (pp. 10–13)

Every complex number $z = x + iy$ corresponds to a unique ordered pair $(x,y)$ in the coordinate plane, called the complex plane or $z$-plane. The horizontal $x$-axis is the real axis and the vertical $y$-axis is the imaginary axis. Viewing $z$ as a position vector from $(0,0)$ to $(x,y)$ gives us a natural notion of length and distance.

straightenDefinition 1.2.1: Modulus & Distance

The modulus (or absolute value) of $z = x + iy$ is the distance from the origin to $(x,y)$: $$|z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = \sqrt{z\bar{z}} \implies |z|^2 = x^2 + y^2 = z\bar{z}$$ The distance between two points $z_1 = x_1 + iy_1$ and $z_2 = x_2 + iy_2$ is: $$|z_2 - z_1| = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$$ Example 1: $|2 - 3i| = \sqrt{2^2 + (-3)^2} = \sqrt{13}$, and $|-9i| = 9$.

change_historyModulus & Triangle Inequalities

Product & Quotient: $|z_1 z_2| = |z_1||z_2|$, $\left|\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right| = \frac{|z_1|}{|z_2|}$, $|z^2| = |z|^2$.
Triangle Inequality (Eq. 6): $|z_1 + z_2| \le |z_1| + |z_2|$
Reverse Triangle Inequality (Eq. 8 & 10): $$\big||z_1| - |z_2|\big| \le |z_1 + z_2| \quad \text{and} \quad \big||z_1| - |z_2|\big| \le |z_1 - z_2|$$

directions_boatEveryday Kerala Example: Backwater Ferry & Locus $|z| = |z - i|$

1. Triangle Inequality on Backwaters: If a boat travels straight from jetty $O$ to $z_1 + z_2$, the direct distance $|z_1 + z_2|$ is always less than or equal to taking two separate legs $|z_1| + |z_2|$.
2. Equidistant Boundary (Zill Example 2): What is the set of points satisfying $|z| = |z - i|$? It means the distance from $z$ to $(0,0)$ equals the distance from $z$ to $(0,1)$. Squaring gives $x^2 + y^2 = x^2 + (y-1)^2 \implies 2y = 1 \implies y = \frac{1}{2}$, the horizontal line halfway between $0$ and $i$!
3. Upper Bound (Zill Example 3): To find an upper bound for $\left|\frac{-1}{z^4+3z^2+2}\right|$ when $|z|=2$, factor the denominator as $(z^2+1)(z^2+2)$. By the reverse triangle inequality, $|z^2+1||z^2+2| \ge (|z|^2-1)(|z|^2-2) = (4-1)(4-2) = 6$. Thus the fraction is at most $\frac{1}{6}$.

1.3 Polar Form of Complex Numbers & De Moivre's Formula

Zill Section 1.3 (pp. 16–20)

Using polar coordinates $x = r\cos\theta$ and $y = r\sin\theta$ where $r = |z| \ge 0$ is the modulus and $\theta = \arg(z)$ is the angle in radians from the positive real axis, any nonzero complex number $z = x + iy$ takes the polar form: $$z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$$

exploreArgument $\arg(z)$ vs Principal Argument $\text{Arg}(z)$

Argument $\arg(z)$: The infinite set of angles $\theta$ such that $\cos\theta = x/r$ and $\sin\theta = y/r$.
Principal Argument $\text{Arg}(z)$: The unique angle $\Theta$ in the half-open interval: $$-\pi < \text{Arg}(z) \le \pi$$ They are related by $\arg(z) = \text{Arg}(z) + 2n\pi$ ($n = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$).
Zill Example 1: For $z = -\sqrt{3} - i$ (in Quadrant III), $r = 2$ and $\tan^{-1}(1/\sqrt{3}) = \pi/6$. Adding $\pi$ gives $\theta = 7\pi/6$, while subtracting $\pi$ gives the principal argument $\text{Arg}(z) = \frac{\pi}{6} - \pi = -\frac{5\pi}{6}$.

auto_awesomePolar Multiplication, Division & De Moivre

For $z_1 = r_1(\cos\theta_1 + i\sin\theta_1)$ and $z_2 = r_2(\cos\theta_2 + i\sin\theta_2)$: $$z_1 z_2 = r_1 r_2[\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) + i\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)]$$ $$\frac{z_1}{z_2} = \frac{r_1}{r_2}[\cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2) + i\sin(\theta_1 - \theta_2)]$$ Integer Powers: $z^n = r^n(\cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta)$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
De Moivre's Formula ($r=1$): $$(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta$$

report_problemCrucial Exam Trap: $\arg(z_1z_2)$ vs $\text{Arg}(z_1z_2)$

As sets of angles, $\arg(z_1z_2) = \arg(z_1) + \arg(z_2)$ and $\arg(z_1/z_2) = \arg(z_1) - \arg(z_2)$ always hold. However, for the principal argument, $\text{Arg}(z_1z_2)$ is not always equal to $\text{Arg}(z_1) + \text{Arg}(z_2)$ because the sum of two angles in $(-\pi,\pi]$ can jump outside $(-\pi,\pi]$! For example, in Zill Example 2, $z_1 = i$ ($\text{Arg}=\pi/2$) and $z_2 = -\sqrt{3}-i$ ($\text{Arg}=-5\pi/6$) give $\text{Arg}(z_1) - \text{Arg}(z_2) = 4\pi/3 \notin (-\pi,\pi]$, so $\text{Arg}(z_1/z_2) = 4\pi/3 - 2\pi = -2\pi/3$.

1.4 Powers and Roots of Complex Numbers

Zill Section 1.4 (pp. 22–25)

A complex number $w$ is an $n$-th root of a nonzero complex number $z$ if $w^n = z$. Every nonzero complex number $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$ has exactly $n$ distinct $n$-th roots.

starsFormula (4): The $n$ Distinct $n$-th Roots & Principal $n$-th Root

For $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) \neq 0$, the $n$ distinct roots of $w^n = z$ are given by: $$w_k = \sqrt[n]{r}\left[\cos\left(\frac{\theta + 2k\pi}{n}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{\theta + 2k\pi}{n}\right)\right], \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots, n - 1$$ Principal $n$-th root ($w_0$): The unique root obtained when we choose $\theta = \text{Arg}(z)$ and set $k = 0$.
Geometric Spacing: All $n$ roots lie on a circle of radius $\sqrt[n]{r}$ centered at the origin, spaced equally at angular intervals of $\frac{2\pi}{n}$ radians (forming the vertices of a regular $n$-sided polygon!).

filter_3Zill Example 1: Three Cube Roots of $z = i$

Here $r = 1$ and $\theta = \text{Arg}(i) = \pi/2$, with $n = 3$: $$w_k = \cos\left(\frac{\pi/2 + 2k\pi}{3}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{\pi/2 + 2k\pi}{3}\right)$$ • $k=0$ (Principal root): $w_0 = \cos\frac{\pi}{6} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \frac{1}{2}i$
• $k=1$: $w_1 = \cos\frac{5\pi}{6} + i\sin\frac{5\pi}{6} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \frac{1}{2}i$
• $k=2$: $w_2 = \cos\frac{3\pi}{2} + i\sin\frac{3\pi}{2} = -i$.

donut_large$n$-th Roots of Unity ($w^n = 1$)

For $z = 1$ ($r=1, \theta=0$), the $n$ roots of unity are $1, \omega_n, \omega_n^2, \dots, \omega_n^{n-1}$ where: $$\omega_n = \cos\frac{2\pi}{n} + i\sin\frac{2\pi}{n}$$ Their sum is always zero for $n \ge 2$: $$1 + \omega_n + \omega_n^2 + \dots + \omega_n^{n-1} = 0$$

local_parkingSimple 3D Intuition: Multi-Floor Spiral Ramp in a Kochi Mall

Why does a single complex number $z$ produce $n$ different roots when we divide the angle by $n$? Imagine a spiral car ramp in a multi-level shopping mall in Kochi. On the ground floor ($2\text{D}$ map), level $k=0$ ($\theta$), level $k=1$ ($\theta + 2\pi$), and level $k=2$ ($\theta + 4\pi$) sit directly on top of the exact same spot $z$. But when you divide your height/angle by $n$ ($\frac{\theta + 2k\pi}{n}$), each floor $k=0, 1, \dots, n-1$ lands at a different angle around the circle! Fraleigh "Plus Two Recall" Notes

Our structured notes highlight five specific Plus Two topics that form the foundation for binary operations, groups, and subgroups. Review them below before testing yourself.

dataset1. Sets, Number Systems & Ordered Pairs

• $S \times S = \{(a,b) \mid a,b \in S\}$ is the set of ordered pairs.
• $\mathbb{Z}^+$ = positive integers $\{1,2,3,\dots\}$; $\mathbb{R}^*$ = nonzero reals $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$; $\mathbb{R}^+$ = positive reals.

account_tree2. Function Composition & Bijections

• $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$ means "apply $g$ first, then $f$".
• A function $f: A \to B$ has an inverse $f^{-1}$ with $f \circ f^{-1} = \iota$ if and only if $f$ is one-to-one (injective) and onto (surjective).

calculate3. Number Laws vs Subtraction

Addition $+$ and multiplication $\cdot$ on $\mathbb{R}$ are commutative and associative, with identities $0$ and $1$. Subtraction $-$ is neither commutative ($2-3 \neq 3-2$) nor associative ($(2-3)-4 \neq 2-(3-4)$), and $0$ is only a right identity ($a-0=a$, $0-a \neq a$).

apps4. Matrices & Determinant of a Product

• Zero matrix $O$ is additive identity ($A+O=A$), but $OA = O \neq I_n$, so $O$ has no multiplicative inverse.
• $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$, and $A$ is invertible $\iff \det(A) \neq 0$. If $\det(A)=1$, then $\det(A^{-1})=1$.

2.1 Binary Operations, Closure, and Warnings

Fraleigh 8th Sec 1 (1.1–1.10, 1.26–1.30) / 7th Sec 2
menu_bookDefinition 1.1: Binary Operation

A binary operation $*$ on a set $S$ is a function from $S \times S$ into $S$. For each ordered pair $(a,b) \in S \times S$, the value $*((a,b))$ is written $a * b$.
Two Conditions (Section 1.3):
1. Everywhere & Well-Defined: Exactly one element is assigned to every ordered pair $(a,b) \in S \times S$.
2. Closed: The assigned result $a * b$ must belong to $S$.

lockDefinition 1.4: Closed Subset & Induced Operation

Let $*$ be a binary operation on $S$ and let $H \subseteq S$ be a subset. $H$ is closed under $*$ if for all $a,b \in H$, we also have $a * b \in H$.
When $H$ is closed under $*$, restricting $*$ to inputs from $H$ gives the induced operation on $H$.

routeEveryday Kerala Example (Fraleigh Example 1.8 Remark): GPS Route Selection

On $\mathbb{Z}^+$, define $a * b = \min\{a, b\}$ (the smaller of $a$ and $b$, so $2 * 11 = 2$ and $15 * 10 = 10$). As Fraleigh notes, this exact binary operation is used every day by a GPS navigation app when choosing between two highway routes from Thrissur to Kochi: it compares travel times $(a, b)$ and keeps the shorter route $\min\{a,b\}$!

Complete Catalogue of Book Examples & Warnings (1.2 – 1.10 & 1.26 – 1.30)

check_circle_outlineClosure Examples (1.2 – 1.7)

• Ex 1.3: Matrix addition $A+B$ is not a binary operation on the set $M(\mathbb{R})$ of all matrices of all sizes, because $A+B$ is undefined when $A$ and $B$ have different sizes.
• Ex 1.5: Ordinary addition $+$ on $\mathbb{R}$ does not induce an operation on $\mathbb{R}^*$ (nonzero reals) because $2, -2 \in \mathbb{R}^*$, but $2 + (-2) = 0 \notin \mathbb{R}^*$.
• Ex 1.6: Let $H = \{n^2 \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}^+\}$. $H$ is not closed under addition ($1^2 + 2^2 = 5 \notin H$), but $H$ is closed under multiplication: $r=n^2, s=m^2 \implies rs = (nm)^2 \in H$.
• Ex 1.7: Real-valued functions $F$ on $\mathbb{R}$ are closed under $+, -, \cdot,$ and $\circ$.

error_outlineWhen a Rule Fails (Examples 1.26 – 1.30)

• Ex 1.26 ($a * b = a/b$): On $\mathbb{Q}$, fails Condition 1 at $b=0$ (not everywhere defined). On $\mathbb{Q}^*$, both hold (valid operation!). On $\mathbb{Z}^+$, fails Condition 2 ($1/2 \notin \mathbb{Z}^+$, not closed).
• Ex 1.27: $f * g = f/g$ on $F$ fails closure because $g(x)=x^2$ is $0$ at $x=0$, so $h(0)$ is undefined.
• Ex 1.28: $f * g =$ "a function greater than both $f$ and $g$" is not well-defined (many functions qualify).
• Ex 1.29 & 1.30: Among 20 people of distinct heights, $a*b=$ "tallest person in $S$" is a valid binary operation, but $a*b=$ "shortest person taller than both $a$ and $b$" is not everywhere defined (fails if $a$ or $b$ is the tallest person).

2.2 Commutative & Associative Operations, Identities, Inverses & Tables

Fraleigh 8th Sec 1 (1.11–1.25) / 7th Sec 2

Let us compare the three operations defined on $\mathbb{Z}^+$ in Examples 1.8, 1.9, and 1.10:
• Example 1.8: $a * b = \min\{a,b\}$ — Both commutative and associative.
• Example 1.9: $a *' b = a$ (always picks the first input) — Not commutative ($5 *' 7 = 5 \neq 7 = 7 *' 5$), though it is associative.
• Example 1.10: $a *'' b = \min\{a,b\} + 2$ — Commutative, but not associative: $$(2 *'' 5) *'' 9 = 4 *'' 9 = 6 \quad \neq \quad 2 *'' (5 *'' 9) = 2 *'' 7 = 4$$

swap_horizDef 1.11, Def 1.12 & Thm 1.13

Commutative (Def 1.11): $a * b = b * a$ for all $a,b \in S$.
Associative (Def 1.12): $(a * b) * c = a * (b * c)$ for all $a,b,c \in S$.
Theorem 1.13 (Composition is Associative): For maps $f,g,h: S \to S$, $f \circ (g \circ h) = (f \circ g) \circ h$ because both evaluate to $f(g(h(x)))$ at every $x \in S$. Consequently, matrix multiplication is associative!

fingerprintIdentity (Def 1.14, Thm 1.15) & Inverses (Def 1.17)

Identity Element (Def 1.14): $e \in S$ is an identity for $*$ if $a * e = e * a = a$ for all $a \in S$.
Theorem 1.15 (Uniqueness): A set with a binary operation has at most one identity ($e = e * e' = e'$).
Inverse (Def 1.17): Given identity $e$, $x'$ is an inverse of $x$ if $x * x' = x' * x = e$.
Example 1.16 & 1.18 (Functions $F$ on $\mathbb{R}$): Identity is $\iota(x)=x$ for $\circ$, $m(x)=1$ for $\cdot$, $a(x)=0$ for $+$, and none for $-$. Under $\circ$, $f$ has an inverse $f'$ with $f \circ f' = f' \circ f = \iota$ $\iff f$ is one-to-one and onto.

2.3 Isomorphic Binary Structures & Structural Properties

Fraleigh 7th Sec 3 / 8th Sec 2 (2.14–2.15)

Two binary algebraic structures $\langle S, * \rangle$ and $\langle S', *' \rangle$ are isomorphic (written $S \simeq S'$) if they have the exact same algebraic structure and differ only in the names/labels of their elements.

verified_userDefinition 2.22: Isomorphism

A map $f: S \to S'$ between $\langle S, * \rangle$ and $\langle S', *' \rangle$ is an isomorphism if:
1. Bijection (Relabelling): $f$ is one-to-one (injective) and onto $S'$ (surjective).
2. Homomorphism Property: For all $a, b \in S$: $$f(a * b) = f(a) *' f(b)$$ Example 2.23: $f: \langle \mathbb{Z}, + \rangle \to \langle 2\mathbb{Z}, + \rangle$ given by $f(m) = 2m$ is an isomorphism because $2a=2b \implies a=b$ (1-to-1), every even integer is $2c$ (onto), and $f(a+b) = 2(a+b) = 2a + 2b = f(a) + f(b)$.

fact_checkStructural Properties under Isomorphism

A structural property depends only on the operation table, not on element names. Under an isomorphism $\phi: S \to S'$, every structural property is preserved:
• Same cardinality $|S| = |S'|$.
• $S$ is commutative/associative $\iff S'$ is.
• $e$ is an identity for $S \iff \phi(e)$ is an identity for $S'$.
• Equation $x * x = a$ has $k$ solutions in $S \iff y *' y = \phi(a)$ has $k$ solutions in $S'$.
How to prove $S \not\simeq S'$: Find one structural property true in $S$ that fails in $S'$!

translateEveryday Kerala Example: Malayalam vs English Metro Station Map

Consider the Kochi Metro route map printed in Malayalam script ($S$) and in English script ($S'$). Translating each station name $\phi(a)$ is a one-to-one onto relabelling, and the travel connection operation between any two stations satisfies $\phi(a * b) = \phi(a) *' \phi(b)$. The two maps are isomorphic!

2.4 Groups: Axioms, Abelian & Non-Abelian Examples

Fraleigh 8th Sec 2 (2.1–2.15) / 7th Sec 4 (4.1–4.14)

Why can we always solve $5 + x = 2$ in $\mathbb{R}$ and $2x = 3$ in $\mathbb{R}^*$? Because both structures have closure, associativity, an identity element, and inverses for every element! Abstracting these exact properties gives the definition of a group.

menu_bookDefinition 2.1 & 2.3: Group $\langle G, * \rangle$ and Abelian Group

A group $\langle G, * \rangle$ is a set $G$, closed under a binary operation $*$, such that:
• $\mathcal{G}_1$ (Associativity): $(a * b) * c = a * (b * c)$ for all $a, b, c \in G$.
• $\mathcal{G}_2$ (Identity): There is an element $e \in G$ such that $e * x = x * e = x$ for all $x \in G$.
• $\mathcal{G}_3$ (Inverses): For each $a \in G$, there is an element $a' \in G$ with $a * a' = a' * a = e$.
Definition 2.3: A group $G$ is abelian if its binary operation $*$ is commutative ($a * b = b * a$ for all $a,b \in G$).

check_circleAbelian & Non-Abelian Group Examples

• Abelian under $+$: $\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}$, real functions $F$, vector spaces $V$, and $m \times n$ matrices $M_{m\times n}(\mathbb{R})$ (Ex 2.6, 2.9–2.11).
• Abelian under $\cdot$: $\mathbb{Q}^+, \mathbb{R}^+$ and $\mathbb{Q}^*, \mathbb{R}^*, \mathbb{C}^*$ (Ex 2.8).
• Twisted Product (Ex 2.15): On $\mathbb{Q}^+$, $a * b = \frac{ab}{2}$ is an abelian group with identity $e = 2$ (since $a*2 = 2a/2 = a$) and inverse $a' = \frac{4}{a}$ (since $a * \frac{4}{a} = \frac{4a}{2a} = 2 = e$).
• Non-Abelian Groups: Plane isometries $T$ under composition (Ex 2.13) and $GL(n, \mathbb{R})$ = invertible $n \times n$ real matrices under multiplication (Ex 2.14).

cancelImportant Non-Examples (Why They Fail)

• $\langle \mathbb{R}, \cdot \rangle$ (Ex 2.2): Not a group ($0$ has no multiplicative inverse).
• $\langle \mathbb{Z}^+, + \rangle$ (Ex 2.4): Not a group (no identity element $0 \notin \mathbb{Z}^+$).
• Nonnegative integers under $+$ (Ex 2.5): Identity is $0$, but $2$ has no inverse ($-2$ is missing).
• $\langle \mathbb{Z}^+, \cdot \rangle$ (Ex 2.7): Identity is $1$, but $3$ has no inverse ($1/3 \notin \mathbb{Z}^+$).
• $\langle M_n(\mathbb{R}), \cdot \rangle$ (Ex 2.12): All $n \times n$ matrices under multiplication fail $\mathcal{G}_3$ (zero matrix $O$ and singular matrices have no inverse).

2.5 Elementary Properties of Groups, Finite Groups & Group Tables

Fraleigh 8th Sec 2 (2.16–2.25) / 7th Sec 4 (4.15+)
gavelTheorems 2.16 – 2.19: Core Group Laws

• Thm 2.16 (Cancellation Laws): $a * b = a * c \implies b = c$ (left) and $b * a = c * a \implies b = c$ (right).
• Thm 2.17 (Linear Equations): $a * x = b$ has unique solution $x = a' * b$; $y * a = b$ has unique solution $y = b * a'$.
• Thm 2.18 (Unique Identity & Inverse): Identity $e$ is unique, and each $a \in G$ has a unique inverse $a'$.
• Corollary 2.19 (Inverse of a Product): For all $a, b \in G$, $(a * b)' = b' * a'$ (reverse order!).
• Sec 2.13 (Semigroups, Monoids & One-Sided Axioms): A semigroup has an associative operation; a monoid is a semigroup with identity. Also, associativity + left identity ($e*x=x$) + left inverse ($a'*a=e$) automatically imply two-sided group axioms!

view_moduleGroup Table Test & Small Order Groups

Result (Group Table Test): In a finite group table, each element of $G$ appears exactly once in each row and column (by Theorem 2.17!).
Groups of Order 1, 2, 3, 4 up to Isomorphism:
• Order 1: $\{e\}$ (1 group).
• Order 2: $\{e, a\}$ with $a*a=e$, isomorphic to $\langle \{1,-1\},\times \rangle$ (1 group).
• Order 3: $\{e, a, b\}$ forced by the once-per-row/col rule to be Table 2.25 ($a*a=b, a*b=e, b*b=a$), so any two groups of order 3 are isomorphic (1 group, $\mathbb{Z}_3$).
• Order 4 (Ex 20 / Ex 5.7): Exactly 2 non-isomorphic groups of order 4 (both abelian): cyclic $\mathbb{Z}_4$ and the Klein 4-group $V$.

checkroomEveryday Kerala Example: Corollary 2.19 (Shoes & Socks / Temple Footwear Counter)

Why is the inverse of $a * b$ equal to $b' * a'$ in reverse order? When getting ready in the morning, if you first put on your socks ($a$) and then put on your shoes ($b$), to undo the process at the temple entrance you must first remove your shoes ($b'$) and then remove your socks ($a'$) — so $(a * b)' = b' * a'$!

2.6 Subgroups, Subgroup Lattices, and Subgroup Criteria

Fraleigh Section 5 (5.1–5.18) / Syllabus Unit 7
menu_bookDefinitions 5.1 & 5.2: Subgroups

Subgroup (Def 5.1): A subset $H$ of a group $G$ that is closed under the binary operation of $G$ and is a group under the induced operation is a subgroup of $G$, written $H \le G$ (or $H < G$ if $H \neq G$).
Improper vs Proper (Def 5.2): $G$ itself is the improper subgroup of $G$; all other subgroups are proper.
Trivial vs Nontrivial (Def 5.2): $\{e\}$ is the trivial subgroup; all other subgroups are nontrivial.
Identity & Inverse Remark: If $H \le G$, the identity of $H$ is the same as the identity $e$ of $G$, and the inverse of $a \in H$ is the same as $a^{-1}$ in $G$.

verifiedThe Three Subgroup Criteria (Theorems 5.12, 5.15, 5.16)

1. Theorem 5.12 (Standard 3-Step Test): $H \subseteq G$ is a subgroup $\iff$
(1) $H$ is closed under the operation of $G$, (2) $e \in H$, and (3) $a \in H \implies a^{-1} \in H$.
2. Theorem 5.15 (1-Step Subgroup Test): A nonempty subset $H \subseteq G$ is a subgroup $\iff$ for all $a, b \in H$, we have $ab^{-1} \in H$.
3. Theorem 5.16 (Finite Subgroup Test): A finite nonempty subset $H \subseteq G$ is a subgroup $\iff H$ is closed under the operation of $G$!

priority_highBook Examples 5.3–5.7 & 5.11–5.18 (Must Know!)

• Same Operation Required: $\langle \mathbb{Z}, + \rangle < \langle \mathbb{R}, + \rangle$ and $\langle \mathbb{Q}^+, \cdot \rangle < \langle \mathbb{R}^+, \cdot \rangle$, but $\langle \mathbb{Q}^+, \cdot \rangle \not\le \langle \mathbb{R}, + \rangle$ because the operations differ!
• Roots of Unity Chain (Ex 5.5 & 5.17): $U_n \le U \le \mathbb{C}^*$, where $U_n = \{z \in \mathbb{C} \mid z^n = 1\}$ is finite and closed ($z_1^n z_2^n = 1$), so $U_n \le \mathbb{C}^*$ by Theorem 5.16.
• Matrices of Determinant 1 (Ex 5.14): $T = \{A \in GL(n,\mathbb{C}) \mid \det(A)=1\} \le GL(n,\mathbb{C})$ by Theorem 5.12 since $\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B)=1$, $\det(I_n)=1$, and $\det(A^{-1})=1$.
• $\mathbb{Z}_4$ vs Klein 4-Group $V$ (Ex 5.7): In $\mathbb{Z}_4 = \{0,1,2,3\}$, the only nontrivial proper subgroup is $\{0,2\}$ ($\{0,3\}$ is not closed since $3+3=2$). In $V = \{e,a,b,c\}$ (where $a^2=b^2=c^2=e$ and $ab=c$), there are three nontrivial proper subgroups: $\{e,a\}, \{e,b\}, \{e,c\}$ ($\{e,a,b\}$ is not closed since $ab=c$).

2.7 Cyclic Subgroups and Generators

Fraleigh Section 5 (5.19–5.26) / Syllabus Unit 8

If we pick a single element $a \in G$ and take all its integer powers $a^n$ ($n \in \mathbb{Z}$, where $a^0 = e$ and $a^{-n} = (a^{-1})^n$; written $na$ in additive notation), we automatically get a subgroup of $G$!

starsTheorem 5.19, Def 5.20 & Def 5.22

Theorem 5.19 & Def 5.20: For any $a \in G$, the set $$\langle a \rangle = \{a^n \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$$ is a subgroup of $G$, called the cyclic subgroup of $G$ generated by $a$, and it is the smallest subgroup of $G$ containing $a$.
Definition 5.22 (Generator & Cyclic Group): An element $a$ generates $G$ if $\langle a \rangle = G$. A group $G$ is cyclic if there is some element $a \in G$ that generates $G$.

playlist_add_checkBook Examples 5.21 – 5.26 & Minimal vs Smallest

• Minimal vs Smallest (Footnote p. 55): In the Klein 4-group $V$, $\{e,a\}, \{e,b\}, \{e,c\}$ are all minimal nontrivial subgroups (no smaller nontrivial subgroup sits inside them), but $V$ has no smallest nontrivial subgroup!
• Ex 5.23: $\mathbb{Z}_4$ is cyclic with generators $1$ and $3$ ($\langle 1 \rangle = \langle 3 \rangle = \mathbb{Z}_4$, while $\langle 2 \rangle = \{0,2\}$). The Klein 4-group $V$ is not cyclic because $\langle a \rangle, \langle b \rangle, \langle c \rangle$ only have 2 elements.
• Ex 5.24 & 5.25: $\langle \mathbb{Z}, + \rangle$ is cyclic with generators $1$ and $-1$ only; $\langle n \rangle = n\mathbb{Z}$ (note $6\mathbb{Z} < 3\mathbb{Z}$). $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is cyclic ($1$ and $n-1$ are always generators).
• Ex 5.26: $U_n = \langle \zeta \rangle$ with $\zeta = \cos\frac{2\pi}{n} + i\sin\frac{2\pi}{n}$ is cyclic!
