The quadrilateral also is now split into two triangles. The top parts of the split angles are angles of the top triangle; the bottom parts of the angles are angles of the bottom triangle. The sum of the angles of each triangle is 180°; two triangles means twice this which is 2 × 180° = 360°. So what do we see here? The sum of all the angles of a quadrilateral is 360°.

Another division We can divide a polygon into triangles in another fashion, by drawing lines from a point inside the polygon to the vertices.

In any polygon, the sum of the angles is a multiple of 180°, right? Is 900 a multiple of 180? To check this, we will have to divide 900 by 180. First, we can remove the common factors (the section Common factors of the lesson Within Numbers in the class 5 textbook). We can split 900 and 180 like this: 900 = 9 × 10 × 10 180 = 9 × 10 × 2 So that 900 ÷ 180 = 10 ÷ 2 = 5 So, 900° = 5 × 180°

An n-sided polygon gives n triangles like this, right? The sum of the angles of these triangles is = n × 180° Among all these angles, those other than the angles at O add up to the sum of the angles of the polygon; and the angles at O add up to 360°, as we have seen earlier. Thus the sum of the angles of the polygon is (n × 180°) − (2 × 180°) = (n − 2) × 180°

So, the sum of outer angles is (5 × 180°) − (3 × 180°) = (5 − 3) × 180° = 2 × 180° = 360° We can do such computations for any polygon. In general, for a polygon of n vertices, if we pair the inner angle and an outer angle at each vertex, we can compute like this: Sum of all angles is n × 180° Sum of inner angles is (n − 2) × 180° The sum of outer angles is (n × 180°) – ((n − 2) × 180°)= (n−(n−2)) × 180° = 2 × 180° = 360° (Recall the section Addition and Subtraction of the lesson, Shorthand Math in the Class 7 textbook). Regular polygons We have seen that if the sides of a triangle are of the same length, then the size of its angles also are the same; and on the other hand, if the angles of a triangle are of the same size, then the sides are of the same length (the section Isosceles triangles of the lesson Equal Triangles). But in a general polygon, even if the sides are of the same length, the angles may not be of the same size; and even if the angles are of the same size, the sides may not be of the same length. For example, any rhombus has sides of the same length, but the angles may be different; and any rectangle has all angles are right; but the lengths of the sides may be different:
