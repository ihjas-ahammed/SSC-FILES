The whole module in five sentences

  1. A resistor dissipates energy, a capacitor stores electric energy and an inductor stores magnetic energy.
  2. After a DC switch changes position, stored quantities approach their new values through a transient.
  3. An ideal LC circuit exchanges energy between its two stores and oscillates rather than settling.
  4. Sinusoidal steady-state circuits are simpler when voltage and current are represented by phasors.
  5. At resonance reactances cancel, while average power is determined by the in-phase part of current.

P1

School physics bridge

Three elements, three jobs

A resistor obeys $v_R=Ri$ and turns electrical energy into heat. A capacitor obeys $q=Cv_C$ and stores $U_C=q^2/(2C)$. An ideal inductor obeys $v_L=L\,di/dt$ and stores $U_L=Li^2/2$. R is in ohms, C in farads, L in henries.

Kirchhoff before algebra

At a node, incoming current equals outgoing current: charge does not pile up in the ideal wire junction. Around a loop the sum of voltage rises and drops is zero. Draw a current arrow first. A passive voltage drop is positive from the terminal where that chosen current enters to the terminal where it leaves.

What can change instantly?

With finite voltage, inductor current is continuous because an instantaneous change would need unbounded $L\,di/dt$. With finite current, capacitor voltage is continuous because $i=C\,dv_C/dt$. A resistor has no stored state and can change its voltage/current immediately in the ideal model.

DC and AC are different questions

A transient asks what happens just after switching and includes initial conditions. Sinusoidal steady state asks what remains after any decaying transient has died away. For ideal DC steady state an inductor acts as a short circuit and a capacitor as an open circuit; these are not general statements at switching time.

Prefixes

$1\ \mathrm{mH}=10^{-3}$ H, $1\ \mathrm{\mu F}=10^{-6}$ F, and $1\ \mathrm{ms}=10^{-3}$ s. Multiply L and C in SI units before taking a square root.

M1

Mathematics from the beginning

Exponentials and the time constant

The number $e\simeq2.718$ has a special exponential function: $d(e^x)/dx=e^x$. Thus $d(e^{-t/\tau})/dt=-e^{-t/\tau}/\tau$. The exponent is dimensionless, so τ must be a time. At t=τ, the exponential is about 0.368; a growth curve $1-e^{-t/\tau}$ has reached 0.632 of its final value.

Solving a first-order equation

For $dy/dt=(y_\infty-y)/\tau$, put $w=y_\infty-y$. Then $dw/dt=-w/\tau$. Separate: $dw/w=-dt/\tau$. Integration gives $\ln w=-t/\tau+\text{constant}$, or $w=w_0e^{-t/\tau}$. Hence $y=y_\infty+(y_0-y_\infty)e^{-t/\tau}$. A logarithm reverses an exponential.

Oscillations are second-order

$d^2(\cos\omega t)/dt^2=-\omega^2\cos\omega t$. Therefore $d^2q/dt^2+\omega_0^2q=0$ is solved by a cosine plus a sine. Two initial conditions, q(0) and i(0), fix the two constants. A spring's displacement obeys the same form; an LC circuit swaps stored energy instead.

The j operator

Define $j^2=-1$. A complex number $a+jb$ is drawn as a point with horizontal a and vertical b. Multiplying by j rotates it +90°. Its magnitude is $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ and its angle satisfies $\tan\phi=b/a$, with the quadrant checked.

Euler's formula $e^{j\theta}=\cos\theta+j\sin\theta$ packages a sinusoid. Represent $v(t)=\operatorname{Re}[\widetilde V e^{j\omega t}]$; the complex coefficient is a phasor. Differentiation becomes multiplication by jω. We use rms phasor magnitudes in circuit calculations; time-domain peak is $\sqrt2$ times rms.

Adding and dividing phasors

Series impedances add. Parallel admittances $Y=1/Z$ add. To divide by $a+jb$, multiply top and bottom by $a-jb$: $(a+jb)(a-jb)=a^2+b^2$. Example: $1/(3+j4)=(3-j4)/25$, with magnitude 1/5.

Cycle averages

$\langle\sin^2\omega t\rangle=1/2$. Also $\langle\cos\omega t\cos(\omega t-\phi)\rangle=\cos\phi/2$. The oscillating double-frequency term averages to zero. This is why rms values and a power factor appear in average power.

T1

LR current growth

Purpose: An inductor initially opposes the change of current; resistance sets the final DC value.

Before you start

Use P1 for the physical quantities and M1 for the mathematics. A capacitor obeys $q=Cv_C$ and an inductor obeys $v_L=L\,di/dt$ using passive signs. Current is positive into the positive-labelled capacitor plate, so $i=dq/dt$.

The idea

An inductor initially opposes the change of current; resistance sets the final DC value.

{{fig:transient}}
Kirchhoff gives $L\,di/dt+Ri=V$.Why: The source balances inductive and resistive drops.
The final derivative is zero, giving $i_\infty=V/R$.Why: At steady DC there is no changing flux.
The remaining distance to that final value decays exponentially with time L/R.Why: The first-order equation has a constant coefficient.
At t=0, i=0 and $v_L=V$; at long time, $v_L=0$.Why: The current cannot jump but its derivative can.
Result

$i=(V/R)(1-e^{-t/\tau_L})$, $\tau_L=L/R$, for i(0)=0 and a DC step V.

Exam trap

Increasing R decreases the time constant and also decreases the final current at fixed V.

Check yourself

At one LR time constant, what fraction of final current flows?
Answer: $1-e^{-1}\simeq0.632$.

This topic supports Q1, Q2, Q21, Q22.

Work it through · Q1

Skill: LR time constant. Model · syllabus-based

Define the LR time constant, state its unit and explain its growth interpretation.

$\tau_L=L/R$ has units H/Ω = s. After one τ, initially zero current has risen to $1-e^{-1}=0.632$ of V/R. Equivalently the gap remaining to its final value is e⁻¹ of its original gap.

Check the endpoint

$\tau_L=L/R$; 63.2% rise in one time constant.

Work it through · Q2

Skill: Current continuity at switching. Model · syllabus-based

Why does current in an ideal inductor remain continuous when a finite DC voltage is suddenly applied?

The passive voltage is $v_L=L\,di/dt$. A finite voltage gives a finite slope, not an instantaneous finite jump. The initial current is therefore the pre-switch value even though the applied voltage changes abruptly.

Check the endpoint

Finite inductor voltage implies continuous current.

Work it through · Q21

Skill: LR step solution. Model · syllabus-based

Derive current growth in a series LR circuit connected to constant V at t=0, with i(0)=0.

Kirchhoff gives $L\dot i+Ri=V$.Why: Source voltage equals the two passive drops.
Write $\dot i=(R/L)(V/R-i)$.Why: This exposes the final value and decay coefficient.
The general solution is $i=V/R+Ke^{-Rt/L}$.Why: The deviation from the final value satisfies a decay equation.
At t=0, $0=V/R+K$, so K=−V/R.Why: Apply the initial inductor current.
Check i(0)=0, i(∞)=V/R and initial slope V/L.Why: All three physical limits agree.
Check the endpoint

$i(t)=(V/R)(1-e^{-Rt/L})$.

Work it through · Q22

Skill: LR transient numerical. Model · syllabus-based

A 12 V step drives R=6 Ω and L=3 H from zero current. Find τ, i and inductor voltage at t=0.50 s.

$\tau=L/R=0.50$ s and $I_\infty=V/R=2$ A.Why: Determine time and current scales first.
At one τ, $i=2(1-e^{-1})=1.264$ A.Why: Use the growth solution.
$v_L=V-Ri=12-6(1.264)=4.415$ V.Why: The source voltage is shared between R and L.
Equivalently $v_L=Ve^{-1}$.Why: The inductor's remaining drop decays exponentially.
Check the endpoint

τ=0.50 s; i≈1.264 A; $v_L\approx4.415$ V.

T2

LR current decay

Purpose: After removing the source, an existing inductor current continues through the closed resistor path and dies away.

Before you start

Use P1 for the physical quantities and M1 for the mathematics. A capacitor obeys $q=Cv_C$ and an inductor obeys $v_L=L\,di/dt$ using passive signs. Current is positive into the positive-labelled capacitor plate, so $i=dq/dt$.

The idea

After removing the source, an existing inductor current continues through the closed resistor path and dies away.

{{fig:transient}}
Choose the discharge loop so current initially retains its original direction.Why: Inductor current is continuous.
Kirchhoff gives $L\,di/dt+Ri=0$.Why: There is no source supplying a rise.
Solve with i(0)=I₀ to obtain exponential decay.Why: The derivative is proportional to negative current.
Stored field energy becomes resistor heat.Why: An ideal inductor has no internal loss here.
Result

$i=I_0e^{-tR/L}$; $U_L=LI_0^2e^{-2tR/L}/2$.

Exam trap

Simply opening the current path can create a large voltage; the ideal decay formula assumes a closed R-L path.

Check yourself

When current has fallen to half, what fraction of magnetic energy remains?
Answer: One quarter.

This topic supports Q3, Q4, Q23, Q24.

Work it through · Q3

Skill: LR half-current energy. Model · syllabus-based

During LR decay, how much magnetic energy remains when the current is half its initial value?

$U_L=Li^2/2$. Therefore $U/U_0=(i/I_0)^2=(1/2)^2=1/4$. The remaining three quarters has been dissipated in the resistor for the ideal closed discharge path.

Check the endpoint

25% remains.

Work it through · Q4

Skill: Discharge path condition. Model · syllabus-based

Why must the LR decay formula specify a closed discharge path?

The formula solves $L\dot i+Ri=0$ with a finite resistor path. If a switch simply opens an inductor carrying current, a large voltage can develop to maintain continuity, potentially producing arcing. That situation is not the assumed ideal R-L loop.

Check the endpoint

A defined resistor path is part of the exponential-decay model.

Work it through · Q23

Skill: LR decay solution and heat. Model · syllabus-based

Derive LR decay from initial current I₀ and show the total resistor heat equals the initial magnetic energy.

$L\dot i+Ri=0$ gives $di/i=-(R/L)dt$.Why: No source remains in the closed loop.
Integrate and impose i(0)=I₀: $i=I_0e^{-Rt/L}$.Why: The constant fixes the initial current.
Heat is $W=\int_0^\infty Ri^2dt=RI_0^2\int_0^\infty e^{-2Rt/L}dt$.Why: Joule power is Ri².
The integral equals L/(2R), giving $W=LI_0^2/2$.Why: The exponential vanishes at infinity.
Check the endpoint

All initial magnetic energy becomes resistor heat: $W=LI_0^2/2$.

Work it through · Q24

Skill: Decay timing with logarithm. Model · syllabus-based

An LR circuit has L=2 H and R=10 Ω. How long does current take to fall to 10% of its initial value?

$i/I_0=e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau=L/R=0.20$ s.Why: Use the current decay time, not the energy decay time.
Set $0.10=e^{-t/0.20}$ and take logarithms: $\ln0.10=-t/0.20$.Why: A logarithm reverses the exponential.
$t=0.20\ln10=0.4605$ s.Why: The negative log becomes a positive elapsed time.
Check the endpoint

Current reaches 10% at about 0.461 s.

T3

RC charging and discharging

Purpose: A resistor limits how quickly a capacitor's voltage changes.

Before you start

Use P1 for the physical quantities and M1 for the mathematics. A capacitor obeys $q=Cv_C$ and an inductor obeys $v_L=L\,di/dt$ using passive signs. Current is positive into the positive-labelled capacitor plate, so $i=dq/dt$.

The idea

A resistor limits how quickly a capacitor's voltage changes.

{{fig:rc_circuit}}
Use $Ri+q/C=V$ and $i=dq/dt$.Why: Kirchhoff plus charge storage gives a first-order equation.
Initially an uncharged capacitor has zero voltage; after charging it holds the source voltage.Why: Capacitor voltage is continuous at switching.
Remove the source into a closed R-C path and set the right side to zero.Why: Stored electric energy drives discharge.
The negative discharge current means q decreases in the chosen capacitor-charge convention.Why: Current signs depend on the arrow definition.
Result

Charging: $q=CV(1-e^{-t/RC})$, $i=(V/R)e^{-t/RC}$. Discharging: $q=q_0e^{-t/RC}$, $i=-q_0e^{-t/RC}/(RC)$ with i=dq/dt.

Exam trap

Charge grows during charging while current decays; do not copy the same time dependence for both.

Check yourself

At t=RC during charging, are q and i at the same fraction of their initial/final scales?
Answer: No: q is 0.632 of final; i is 0.368 of initial.

This topic supports Q5, Q6, Q25, Q26, Q27, Q37.

Work it through · Q5

Skill: RC time constant. Model · syllabus-based

An RC circuit has R=20 kΩ and C=50 μF. Find τ and the charging fraction at t=τ.

$\tau=RC=(2.0\times10^4)(50\times10^{-6})=1.0$ s. The capacitor voltage and charge reach $1-e^{-1}=0.632$ of their final values, while charging current falls to 0.368 of its initial value.

Check the endpoint

τ=1.0 s; charge fraction 63.2%.

Work it through · Q6

Skill: Capacitor continuity. Model · syllabus-based

Which capacitor quantity remains continuous under finite current at switching, and why?

Capacitor voltage remains continuous because $i=C\,dv_C/dt$. A finite instantaneous voltage jump would need an impulse of unbounded current in the ideal model. Charge q=Cv is likewise continuous.

Check the endpoint

Finite current implies continuous capacitor voltage and charge.

Work it through · Q25

Skill: RC charge solution. Model · syllabus-based

Derive q(t), i(t) and capacitor voltage when an initially uncharged series RC circuit is connected to DC V.

$R\dot q+q/C=V$.Why: Use Kirchhoff and i=dq/dt.
The final charge is CV and the time constant is RC.Why: Compare to the standard first-order equation.
$q=CV(1-e^{-t/RC})$ meets q(0)=0.Why: The deviation from CV decays exponentially.
Differentiate to get $i=(V/R)e^{-t/RC}$ and divide q by C for $v_C=V(1-e^{-t/RC})$.Why: Current is charge rate and voltage is charge per capacitance.
Initially i=V/R while $v_C=0$; finally i=0 while $v_C=V$.Why: These limits check the solution.

Charge and voltage grow; charging current decays.

Work it through · Q26

Skill: RC discharge sign and numerical. Model · syllabus-based

A 100 μF capacitor initially at 10 V discharges through 20 kΩ. Using i=dq/dt for its initially positive plate, find q, v and i after 2 s.

$\tau=RC=2$ s and $q_0=CV_0=1.0\times10^{-3}$ C.Why: Convert microfarads to farads.
$q=q_0e^{-1}=3.679\times10^{-4}$ C and $v=10e^{-1}=3.679$ V.Why: The source-free charge decays.
$i=-q_0e^{-1}/\tau=-1.839\times10^{-4}$ A.Why: The chosen positive plate loses charge.
A current arrow in the discharge direction would instead have positive magnitude 0.1839 mA.Why: A negative sign describes the reference convention.

q≈0.3679 mC; v≈3.679 V; i≈−0.1839 mA with i=dq/dt.

Work it through · Q27

Skill: RC charging energy budget. Model · syllabus-based

For charging an initially uncharged capacitor from an ideal constant source V through R, find source work, final stored energy and resistor heat.

Total charge supplied is CV, so source work is $W_s=V\int i\,dt=V(CV)=CV^2$.Why: The source voltage stays constant throughout charging.
Final stored energy is $U_C=CV^2/2$.Why: Capacitor energy is the integral of its rising voltage over charge.
The difference $W_R=W_s-U_C=CV^2/2$ is resistor heat.Why: Energy is conserved.
The result is independent of positive R, although R changes the charging duration.Why: A smaller R raises current but shortens the transient.
Check the endpoint

Half the source energy is stored; half is dissipated in this ideal resistive charging process.

Work it through · Q37

Skill: Comparing first-order transients. Model · syllabus-based

Derive and compare LR growth/decay and RC charge/discharge, emphasizing initial conditions, time constants and limiting behaviour.

LR obeys $L\dot i+Ri=V$; RC obeys $R\dot q+q/C=V$.Why: Use Kirchhoff with the element relations.
Each has form $\dot y=(y_\infty-y)/\tau$ and solution $y=y_\infty+(y_0-y_\infty)e^{-t/\tau}$.Why: A deviation from steady state decays exponentially.
For LR, $\tau=L/R$ and $i_\infty=V/R$; for RC, $\tau=RC$ and $q_\infty=CV$.Why: Compare coefficients.
Removing the source into a closed discharge path sets the final state to zero, giving $i=I_0e^{-tR/L}$ or $q=q_0e^{-t/RC}$.Why: Stored energy drives a decaying response.
Inductor current and capacitor voltage remain continuous at switching under finite drive.Why: Their derivatives are limited by finite voltage or current.
During RC charging, current is $V e^{-t/RC}/R$, while charge grows; during LR growth, current itself rises.Why: Current equals charge derivative only in the capacitor state equation.
Check t=0, t=∞ and units of τ in every solution.Why: These three checks catch sign and reciprocal errors.
Check the endpoint

LR time scale is L/R; RC time scale is RC; both are first-order responses with specified initial states.

T4

Free LC oscillations

Purpose: An ideal capacitor and inductor swap electric and magnetic energy indefinitely.

Before you start

Use P1 for the physical quantities and M1 for the mathematics. A capacitor obeys $q=Cv_C$ and an inductor obeys $v_L=L\,di/dt$ using passive signs. Current is positive into the positive-labelled capacitor plate, so $i=dq/dt$.

The idea

An ideal capacitor and inductor swap electric and magnetic energy indefinitely.

{{fig:lc_energy}}
Kirchhoff in the isolated loop gives $L\,di/dt+q/C=0$.Why: There is no resistor or source.
Use i=dq/dt to get a second-order equation.Why: Capacitor charge is the state variable.
At maximum charge current is zero; a quarter-cycle later charge is zero and current magnitude is greatest.Why: The energy moves between C and L.
The sum $q^2/(2C)+Li^2/2$ stays constant.Why: There is no dissipative element.
Result

$\ddot q+q/(LC)=0$; $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$; for q(0)=q₀, i(0)=0: $q=q_0\cos\omega_0t$, $i=-\omega_0q_0\sin\omega_0t$.

Exam trap

An ideal LC circuit has no exponential decay. Real resistance makes the oscillation damped.

Check yourself

When q=0 in a free LC oscillation, has all circuit energy vanished?
Answer: No. It is stored in the inductor.

This topic supports Q7, Q8, Q28, Q38.

Work it through · Q7

Skill: LC natural frequency. Model · syllabus-based

Write the natural angular frequency and frequency of an ideal LC circuit, and distinguish the units.

$\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$ in rad/s, while $f_0=\omega_0/(2\pi)=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ in hertz. Confusing the two loses a factor of 2π.

Check the endpoint

Angular frequency and ordinary frequency differ by 2π.

Work it through · Q8

Skill: Quarter-cycle energy exchange. Model · syllabus-based

An isolated LC circuit starts with a charged capacitor and zero current. Where is its energy after a quarter-cycle?

At $t=T/4$, $q=q_0\cos(\pi/2)=0$, so capacitor energy is zero. Current magnitude is maximal and all energy is magnetic, $Li^2/2=q_0^2/(2C)$.

Check the endpoint

All stored energy is in the inductor.

Work it through · Q28

Skill: Free LC solution and numerical. Model · syllabus-based

An ideal isolated circuit has L=0.10 H, C=10 μF, initial capacitor voltage 20 V and zero current. Find q(t), i(t), frequency and peak current.

$q_0=CV_0=2.0\times10^{-4}$ C and $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}=1000$ rad/s.Why: Use SI units in LC.
$q=q_0\cos1000t$ and $i=-\omega_0q_0\sin1000t$.Why: Apply initial q and zero initial current.
Thus $i=-0.20\sin1000t$ A.Why: Current is dq/dt.
$f_0=1000/(2\pi)=159.15$ Hz.Why: Convert angular to ordinary frequency.
Check the endpoint

$q=2.0\times10^{-4}\cos1000t$ C, $i=-0.20\sin1000t$ A; peak current 0.20 A.

Work it through · Q38

Skill: Free versus driven LC and conservation. Model · syllabus-based

Develop free LC oscillation and compare it with an initially uncharged LC circuit subjected to a DC step. Explain energy in both cases.

Free LC gives $L\ddot q+q/C=0$, so for q(0)=q₀ and i(0)=0, $q=q_0\cos\omega_0t$ and $i=-\omega_0q_0\sin\omega_0t$.Why: The natural frequency is $1/\sqrt{LC}$.
Substituting into $U=q^2/(2C)+Li^2/2$ gives $U=q_0^2/(2C)$.Why: Sine and cosine squared sum to one and $L\omega_0^2=1/C$.
Energy alternates between C and L without loss.Why: No resistance or external source is present.
For a DC step the equation is $L\ddot q+q/C=V$, with zero initial q and i.Why: The source remains connected and changes the forcing.
The solution is $q=CV(1-\cos\omega_0t)$ and $i=CV\omega_0\sin\omega_0t$.Why: Shift q by CV and apply initial conditions.
Now the stored energy changes by source power Vi: $dU/dt=Vi$.Why: Multiply the circuit equation by i.
At half a cycle q=2CV, stored energy is $2CV^2$, exactly source work $Vq=2CV^2$; on the return the ideal source can receive energy.Why: Overshoot is consistent with conservation.
Check the endpoint

Free LC has constant total stored energy; the driven LC step exchanges energy with the source and does not settle.

T5

LC response to a DC step

Purpose: A constant source connected to an ideal LC series circuit does not create a settling exponential.

Before you start

Use P1 for the physical quantities and M1 for the mathematics. A capacitor obeys $q=Cv_C$ and an inductor obeys $v_L=L\,di/dt$ using passive signs. Current is positive into the positive-labelled capacitor plate, so $i=dq/dt$.

The idea

A constant source connected to an ideal LC series circuit does not create a settling exponential.

Write $L\ddot q+q/C=V$.Why: The source remains in the loop.
Subtract the equilibrium charge CV: set x=q−CV.Why: This turns the equation into $\ddot x+\omega_0^2x=0$.
Initial conditions are x(0)=−CV and $\dot x(0)=0$.Why: The capacitor was uncharged and the inductor carried no current.
Solve to obtain q and i; capacitor voltage oscillates between 0 and 2V.Why: Without resistance there is no mechanism to settle.
Result

For initially uncharged C and zero current: $q=CV(1-\cos\omega_0t)$, $i=CV\omega_0\sin\omega_0t$.

Exam trap

This driven step response differs from free LC discharge; the source exchanges energy with the circuit.

Check yourself

Does an ideal LC step current approach a nonzero constant?
Answer: No. It oscillates about zero.

This topic supports Q9, Q29.

Work it through · Q9

Skill: Ideal LC step overshoot. Model · syllabus-based

An uncharged capacitor and ideal inductor are connected in series to a DC source V at t=0. What is the maximum capacitor voltage?

$v_C=V(1-\cos\omega_0t)$ under zero initial current and charge. At half a natural period, cosine is −1 and $v_C=2V$. The ideal circuit does not settle because it lacks resistance.

Check the endpoint

Maximum capacitor voltage is 2V.

Work it through · Q29

Skill: Driven ideal LC step derivation. Model · syllabus-based

Derive the ideal series LC response to a DC step V with zero initial charge and current. Explain the voltage overshoot.

$L\ddot q+q/C=V$.Why: The source remains connected.
Set x=q−CV to obtain $\ddot x+\omega_0^2x=0$.Why: The constant forcing shifts the centre of oscillation.
Since x(0)=−CV and $\dot x(0)=0$, $x=-CV\cos\omega_0t$.Why: Use both initial conditions.
Therefore $q=CV(1-\cos\omega_0t)$ and $i=CV\omega_0\sin\omega_0t$.Why: Differentiate charge for current.
At $\omega_0t=\pi$, $v_C=2V$ and i=0.Why: The ideal inductor's stored energy and source work drive the overshoot.
No resistor removes the oscillation energy.Why: The circuit does not settle in this ideal model.
Check the endpoint

The capacitor voltage oscillates from 0 to 2V; this is distinct from free discharge.

T6

Rotating-coil AC generator

Purpose: Rotation changes flux linkage sinusoidally and produces an alternating emf.

Before you start

Use P1 for the physical quantities and M1 for the mathematics. A capacitor obeys $q=Cv_C$ and an inductor obeys $v_L=L\,di/dt$ using passive signs. Current is positive into the positive-labelled capacitor plate, so $i=dq/dt$.

The idea

Rotation changes flux linkage sinusoidally and produces an alternating emf.

The linked flux is $NBA\cos\omega t$.Why: Area and field are fixed while orientation changes.
Differentiate and apply Faraday's minus sign.Why: The emf is the negative flux-linkage rate.
One complete rotation gives one sinusoidal cycle for this ideal two-pole geometry.Why: The orientation repeats after 2π radians.
Result

$\mathcal E=NBA\omega\sin\omega t$, peak $\mathcal E_0=NBA\omega$, when the coil normal starts along B.

Exam trap

Maximum flux occurs at zero emf; maximum emf occurs where flux crosses zero.

Check yourself

If angular speed doubles, how do frequency and peak emf change?
Answer: Both double.

This topic supports Q10, Q11, Q30.

Work it through · Q10

Skill: Generator peak dependence. Model · syllabus-based

State the peak emf of a rotating N-turn coil and explain its dependence on angular speed.

For area A in uniform B, $\mathcal E_0=NBA\omega$. Faster rotation increases the rate of change of linked flux, so peak emf is proportional to ω. The result assumes rotation about an axis in the coil plane perpendicular to B.

Check the endpoint

$\mathcal E_0=NBA\omega$.

Work it through · Q11

Skill: Flux-emf timing. Model · syllabus-based

At what coil orientations are flux and emf maximum in an ideal AC generator?

Flux magnitude is maximum when the coil normal is parallel or antiparallel to B; its derivative and emf are then zero. Emf magnitude is maximum when the normal is perpendicular to B, where flux crosses zero fastest.

Check the endpoint

Flux and emf maxima are separated by a quarter-cycle.

T7

Pure R, L and C on AC

Purpose: Resistance keeps voltage and current in phase; reactive elements shift them by a quarter-cycle.

Before you start

Use P1 for the physical quantities and M1 for the mathematics. A capacitor obeys $q=Cv_C$ and an inductor obeys $v_L=L\,di/dt$ using passive signs. Current is positive into the positive-labelled capacitor plate, so $i=dq/dt$.

The idea

Resistance keeps voltage and current in phase; reactive elements shift them by a quarter-cycle.

{{fig:phasor}}
For R, v=Ri, so their waveforms align.Why: No derivative or integral shifts phase.
For L, $v=L\,di/dt$: voltage leads current by 90°.Why: A derivative advances sinusoidal phase.
For C, $i=C\,dv/dt$: current leads voltage by 90°.Why: Here current is the derivative.
Ideal L and C exchange energy with the source but have zero mean power.Why: Their instantaneous power alternates sign equally.
Result

$Z_R=R$, $Z_L=j\omega L$, $Z_C=1/(j\omega C)=-j/(\omega C)$.

Exam trap

“ELI the ICE man”: in L, E leads I; in C, I leads E. State which quantity leads.

Check yourself

What happens to capacitive reactance when frequency doubles?
Answer: It halves.

This topic supports Q12, Q13, Q14, Q31.

Work it through · Q12

Skill: Pure resistor phase. Model · syllabus-based

Describe voltage, current and average power for a pure resistor driven by sinusoidal voltage.

Current is in phase with voltage because $i=v/R$. $I_{\rm rms}=V_{\rm rms}/R$ and $P=V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}=I_{\rm rms}^2R$. Instantaneous power is nonnegative.

Check the endpoint

A resistor dissipates positive mean power with power factor one.

Work it through · Q13

Skill: Pure inductor phase. Model · syllabus-based

State the reactance and phase relation for an ideal inductor on sinusoidal AC.

$X_L=\omega L$ and $Z_L=j\omega L$. Voltage leads current by 90°, or current lags voltage by 90°. Average power is zero because stored magnetic energy is returned each cycle.

Check the endpoint

Inductive current lags voltage by 90°.

Work it through · Q14

Skill: Pure capacitor phase. Model · syllabus-based

State the reactance and phase relation for an ideal capacitor on sinusoidal AC.

$X_C=1/(\omega C)$ as a positive magnitude and $Z_C=-jX_C$. Current leads voltage by 90° because $i=C\,dv/dt$. Mean power is zero for an ideal capacitor.

Check the endpoint

Capacitive current leads voltage by 90°.

Work it through · Q31

Skill: Deriving reactive impedances. Model · syllabus-based

Derive the impedances of an ideal inductor and capacitor using j and the common time factor $e^{j\omega t}$.

Differentiation of a sinusoidal phasor expression multiplies it by jω.Why: The derivative of $e^{j\omega t}$ is $j\omega e^{j\omega t}$.

For C, $\widetilde I=j\omega C\widetilde V$, giving $Z_C=1/(j\omega C)=-j/(\omega C)$.Why: Use $i=C\,dv/dt$ and $1/j=-j$.

T8

Complex impedance and series RL or RC

Purpose: The j operator keeps magnitude and phase together during circuit addition.

Before you start

Use P1 for the physical quantities and M1 for the mathematics. A capacitor obeys $q=Cv_C$ and an inductor obeys $v_L=L\,di/dt$ using passive signs. Current is positive into the positive-labelled capacitor plate, so $i=dq/dt$.

The idea

The j operator keeps magnitude and phase together during circuit addition.

{{fig:phasor}}
Use the common current as phasor reference in a series circuit.Why: The same current passes through each element.
Resistive voltage lies along I; inductive voltage is +90° and capacitive voltage −90°.Why: Use the pure-element phase laws.
Add perpendicular components, then use Pythagoras for magnitude.Why: Voltages with different phases do not add as scalar magnitudes.
The impedance angle is the voltage lead relative to current.Why: A positive angle means current lags the source.
Result

$Z_{RL}=R+j\omega L$; $Z_{RC}=R-j/(\omega C)$; $\widetilde I=\widetilde V/Z$.

Exam trap

Use j for the imaginary unit so i can denote current; phasors describe sinusoidal steady state at one frequency.

Check yourself

A series RC circuit has negative impedance angle. Does current lead or lag?
Answer: It leads source voltage.

This topic supports Q15, Q16, Q32, Q33.

Work it through · Q15

Skill: Meaning of j. Model · syllabus-based

What does multiplying a phasor by j do, and why is j used rather than i here?

Since $j^2=-1$, multiplication by j rotates a complex-plane vector +90° without changing its magnitude. The letter j avoids conflict with the time-dependent current i. It represents phase mathematically rather than a second physical current.

Check the endpoint

j denotes a +90° phase rotation.

Work it through · Q16

Skill: Phasor voltage addition. Model · syllabus-based

Why can magnitudes of resistor and inductor voltages not be added directly in a series RL circuit?

They differ in phase by 90°. With current as reference, $\widetilde V=V_R+jV_L$, so $V=\sqrt{V_R^2+V_L^2}$, not $V_R+V_L$. Their instantaneous values still obey Kirchhoff's law.

Check the endpoint

Add voltages as phasors, then take the magnitude.

T9

Parallel LC and antiresonance

Purpose: Parallel branches share voltage; their reactive currents can cancel at the input.

Before you start

Use P1 for the physical quantities and M1 for the mathematics. A capacitor obeys $q=Cv_C$ and an inductor obeys $v_L=L\,di/dt$ using passive signs. Current is positive into the positive-labelled capacitor plate, so $i=dq/dt$.

The idea

Parallel branches share voltage; their reactive currents can cancel at the input.

Add branch currents $I_C=j\omega CV$ and $I_L=V/(j\omega L)$.Why: Parallel elements see the same phasor voltage.
Factor V to identify admittance.Why: Current divided by voltage is Y.
At resonance the imaginary contributions cancel.Why: The branch currents have equal magnitudes and opposite phases.
The source current is zero although branch currents are not.Why: Current circulates between L and C.
Result

$Y=j(\omega C-1/(\omega L))$; at $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$, ideal input Y=0 and input impedance diverges.

Exam trap

This ideal parallel LC result differs from series resonance. Real coil resistance gives finite impedance and losses.

Check yourself

At ideal parallel resonance, can inductor current be nonzero while source current is zero?
Answer: Yes; capacitor current cancels it at the input.

This topic supports Q17, Q34.

Work it through · Q17

Skill: Parallel resonance input. Model · syllabus-based

What are ideal input current and impedance at parallel LC resonance under an AC voltage source?

At $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$, $Y=j(\omega C-1/\omega L)=0$. Thus source current is zero and input impedance tends to infinity. Opposite nonzero branch currents can circulate.

Check the endpoint

Ideal parallel resonance gives zero input current and infinite impedance.

T10

Series LCR and resonance

Purpose: In series, L and C reactances cancel at resonance, leaving only R.

Before you start

Use P1 for the physical quantities and M1 for the mathematics. A capacitor obeys $q=Cv_C$ and an inductor obeys $v_L=L\,di/dt$ using passive signs. Current is positive into the positive-labelled capacitor plate, so $i=dq/dt$.

The idea

In series, L and C reactances cancel at resonance, leaving only R.

{{fig:resonance}}
Add the three impedances.Why: The same current flows through every element.
Set the imaginary part to zero for resonance.Why: Voltage and current become in phase.
Then impedance magnitude is minimum, R, and current is maximum for fixed source voltage.Why: The reactive contributions cancel as phasors.
Individual L and C voltages can be large even though their sum vanishes.Why: Equal opposite phasors need not be individually small.
Result

$Z=R+j(\omega L-1/(\omega C))$; $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$; at resonance $I_{\rm rms}=V_{\rm rms}/R$.

Exam trap

Below resonance the circuit is capacitive; above it the circuit is inductive.

Check yourself

Does zero net reactance mean zero L and C voltages?
Answer: No; their nonzero phasors cancel.

Work it through · Q18

Skill: Series resonance condition. Model · syllabus-based

State the resonance condition and input impedance of a series LCR circuit.

Resonance occurs when $\omega L=1/(\omega C)$, giving $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$. Reactive impedances cancel, so Z=R, current is in phase with voltage and its amplitude is maximal at fixed V.

Check the endpoint

At series resonance, Z=R.

Work it through · Q19

Skill: Above and below resonance. Model · syllabus-based

State whether a series LCR circuit is capacitive or inductive below and above its resonance frequency.

Below resonance, $1/(\omega C)>\omega L$, so net reactance is negative and current leads. Above resonance, $\omega L>1/(\omega C)$, so net reactance is positive and current lags.

Check the endpoint

Below: capacitive. Above: inductive.

Skill: Series and parallel resonance comparison. Model · syllabus-based

Use complex impedance/admittance to explain and contrast series LCR resonance and ideal parallel LC resonance, including current and energy flow.

Series impedance is $Z_s=R+j(\omega L-1/\omega C)$.Why: Common current permits impedance addition.

T11

Power in AC

Purpose: Only the part of current in phase with voltage transfers net energy each cycle.

Before you start

Use P1 for the physical quantities and M1 for the mathematics. A capacitor obeys $q=Cv_C$ and an inductor obeys $v_L=L\,di/dt$ using passive signs. Current is positive into the positive-labelled capacitor plate, so $i=dq/dt$.

The idea

Only the part of current in phase with voltage transfers net energy each cycle.

Write $v=V_0\cos\omega t$ and $i=I_0\cos(\omega t-\phi)$.Why: Define positive φ as current lag.
Multiply and apply the cosine product identity.Why: Separate a constant term from a double-frequency term.
Average over a whole cycle.Why: The oscillating term integrates to zero.
Replace peaks by rms values.Why: Each rms is peak divided by $\sqrt2$.
Result

$P=V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}\cos\phi=I_{\rm rms}^2R$ for a series load with real resistance R.

Exam trap

Apparent power VI in VA is not always real power in W. The power factor is cos φ.

Check yourself

An ideal inductor draws rms current. Is its mean power necessarily positive?
Answer: No. Its phase angle is 90°, so mean power is zero.

This topic supports Q20, Q36, Q40.

Work it through · Q20

Skill: Power factor definition. Model · syllabus-based

Define power factor and distinguish real power from apparent power.

Power factor is $\cos\phi$, where φ is the phase difference of voltage and current for a sinusoidal load. Apparent power is $S_{\rm app}=V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}$ in VA; real mean power is $P=S_{\rm app}\cos\phi$ in W.

Check the endpoint

Only the in-phase contribution transfers net energy per cycle.

Work it through · Q40

Skill: Derivation of average AC power. Model · syllabus-based

Derive average power for sinusoidal voltage and current with phase difference, then discuss pure R, L, C and series LCR at resonance.

Let $v=V_0\cos\omega t$ and $i=I_0\cos(\omega t-\phi)$.Why: φ is the voltage lead over current.
Use $2\cos A\cos B=\cos(A-B)+\cos(A+B)$ to obtain $p=(V_0I_0/2)[\cos\phi+\cos(2\omega t-\phi)]$.Why: Power is the instantaneous product vi.
Average over a full cycle: the second cosine contributes zero.Why: It completes an integer number of cycles.
Since $V_{\rm rms}=V_0/\sqrt2$ and $I_{\rm rms}=I_0/\sqrt2$, $P=V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}\cos\phi$.Why: Convert peaks to rms values.
For pure R, φ=0 and P=I²R; for pure L or C, φ=±π/2 and P=0.Why: Reactive elements return the energy they store in the ideal model.
At series LCR resonance φ=0, so all input mean power is dissipated in R, although reactive voltages and stored energies are nonzero.Why: Cancellation of reactances gives unity power factor.
Check the endpoint

Average AC power equals rms voltage × rms current × power factor.

Formula and condition sheet

T1 · LR current growth

$i=(V/R)(1-e^{-t/\tau_L})$, $\tau_L=L/R$, for i(0)=0 and a DC step V.

T2 · LR current decay

$i=I_0e^{-tR/L}$; $U_L=LI_0^2e^{-2tR/L}/2$.

T3 · RC charging and discharging

Charging: $q=CV(1-e^{-t/RC})$, $i=(V/R)e^{-t/RC}$. Discharging: $q=q_0e^{-t/RC}$, $i=-q_0e^{-t/RC}/(RC)$ with i=dq/dt.

T4 · Free LC oscillations

$\ddot q+q/(LC)=0$; $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$; for q(0)=q₀, i(0)=0: $q=q_0\cos\omega_0t$, $i=-\omega_0q_0\sin\omega_0t$.

T5 · LC response to a DC step

For initially uncharged C and zero current: $q=CV(1-\cos\omega_0t)$, $i=CV\omega_0\sin\omega_0t$.

T6 · Rotating-coil AC generator

$\mathcal E=NBA\omega\sin\omega t$, peak $\mathcal E_0=NBA\omega$, when the coil normal starts along B.

T7 · Pure R, L and C on AC

$Z_R=R$, $Z_L=j\omega L$, $Z_C=1/(j\omega C)=-j/(\omega C)$.

T8 · Complex impedance and series RL or RC

$Z_{RL}=R+j\omega L$; $Z_{RC}=R-j/(\omega C)$; $\widetilde I=\widetilde V/Z$.

T9 · Parallel LC and antiresonance

$Y=j(\omega C-1/(\omega L))$; at $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$, ideal input Y=0 and input impedance diverges.

T10 · Series LCR and resonance

$Z=R+j(\omega L-1/(\omega C))$; $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$; at resonance $I_{\rm rms}=V_{\rm rms}/R$.
