Separation of variables, time-independent Schrödinger equation, properties of stationary states, and energy expectation values.

Example 2.1 Superposition of Two Stationary States

Suppose a particle starts out in a linear combination of just two stationary states:

$$\Psi(x, 0) = c_1\psi_1(x) + c_2\psi_2(x).$$

(To keep things simple assume that the constants $c_n$ and the states $\psi_n(x)$ are real.) What is the wave function $\Psi(x, t)$ at subsequent times? Find the probability density $|\Psi(x, t)|^2$, and describe its motion.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Construct the full time-dependent wave function $\Psi(x, t)$.

The time-dependent Schrödinger equation is linear. For any stationary state $\psi_n(x)$ satisfying $\hat{H}\psi_n = E_n\psi_n$, its time evolution is given by multiplying by the phase factor $e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}$. Therefore, the linear combination evolves as:

$$\Psi(x, t) = c_1\psi_1(x)e^{-iE_1 t/\hbar} + c_2\psi_2(x)e^{-iE_2 t/\hbar}.$$

Step 2: Calculate the probability density $|\Psi(x, t)|^2 = \Psi^*(x, t)\Psi(x, t)$ step by step.

Since $c_1, c_2, \psi_1(x),$ and $\psi_2(x)$ are real, the complex conjugate is $\Psi^*(x, t) = c_1\psi_1(x)e^{iE_1 t/\hbar} + c_2\psi_2(x)e^{iE_2 t/\hbar}$.

Multiplying out the terms using the aligned multi-line environment:

$$\begin{aligned} |\Psi(x, t)|^2 &= \left(c_1\psi_1 e^{iE_1 t/\hbar} + c_2\psi_2 e^{iE_2 t/\hbar}\right)\left(c_1\psi_1 e^{-iE_1 t/\hbar} + c_2\psi_2 e^{-iE_2 t/\hbar}\right) \\ &= c_1^2\psi_1^2 e^{i(E_1 - E_1)t/\hbar} + c_2^2\psi_2^2 e^{i(E_2 - E_2)t/\hbar} + c_1 c_2 \psi_1 \psi_2 e^{i(E_1 - E_2)t/\hbar} + c_1 c_2 \psi_1 \psi_2 e^{-i(E_1 - E_2)t/\hbar} \\ &= c_1^2\psi_1(x)^2 + c_2^2\psi_2(x)^2 + c_1 c_2 \psi_1(x)\psi_2(x)\left[e^{-i(E_2 - E_1)t/\hbar} + e^{i(E_2 - E_1)t/\hbar}\right] \\ &= c_1^2\psi_1(x)^2 + c_2^2\psi_2(x)^2 + 2c_1c_2\psi_1(x)\psi_2(x)\cos\left(\frac{E_2 - E_1}{\hbar}t\right). \end{aligned}$$

Step 3: Physical interpretation of the motion.

While an individual stationary state has a time-independent probability density ($|\Psi_n|^2 = |\psi_n|^2$), a superposition of two stationary states has an interference cross-term that oscillates sinusoidally in time with angular frequency:

$$\omega = \frac{E_2 - E_1}{\hbar}.$$

The probability density sloshes back and forth across the potential well at frequency $\omega$.

Final Result / Conclusion:
$\Psi(x, t) = c_1\psi_1(x)e^{-iE_1 t/\hbar} + c_2\psi_2(x)e^{-iE_2 t/\hbar}$ and $|\Psi(x, t)|^2 = c_1^2\psi_1^2 + c_2^2\psi_2^2 + 2c_1c_2\psi_1\psi_2\cos\left(\frac{E_2 - E_1}{\hbar}t\right)$, oscillating with frequency $\omega = \frac{E_2 - E_1}{\hbar}$.
Problem 2.1 General Theorems for Normalizable Stationary States ★

Prove the following three theorems:

  • (a)
    For normalizable solutions, the separation constant $E$ must be real. Hint: Write $E$ as $E_0 + i\Gamma$ (with $E_0$ and $\Gamma$ real), and show that if conservation of probability $\frac{d}{dt}\int |\Psi|^2 dx = 0$ is to hold for all $t$, $\Gamma$ must be zero.
  • (b)
    The time-independent wave function $\psi(x)$ can always be taken to be real (unlike $\Psi(x, t)$, which is necessarily complex). This doesn’t mean that every solution to the time-independent Schrödinger equation is real; what it says is that if you’ve got one that is not, it can always be expressed as a linear combination of solutions (with the same energy) that are. So you might as well stick to $\psi$s that are real. Hint: If $\psi(x)$ satisfies $\hat{H}\psi = E\psi$, for a given $E$, so too does its complex conjugate $\psi^*$, and hence also the real linear combinations $(\psi + \psi^*)$ and $i(\psi - \psi^*)$.
  • (c)
    If $V(x)$ is an even function (that is, $V(-x) = V(x)$) then $\psi(x)$ can always be taken to be either even or odd. Hint: If $\psi(x)$ satisfies the time-independent Schrödinger equation for a given $E$, so too does $\psi(-x)$, and hence also the even and odd linear combinations $\psi(x) \pm \psi(-x)$.
Detailed Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Proof that $E$ is purely real

Let $E = E_0 + i\Gamma$ where $E_0, \Gamma \in \mathbb{R}$.

$$\begin{aligned} \Psi(x, t) &= \psi(x) e^{-iEt/\hbar} = \psi(x) e^{-i(E_0 + i\Gamma)t/\hbar} \\ &= \psi(x) e^{-iE_0 t/\hbar} e^{-i(i\Gamma)t/\hbar} = \psi(x) e^{-iE_0 t/\hbar} e^{\Gamma t/\hbar}. \end{aligned}$$

Now evaluate the total normalization integral at time $t$:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} |\Psi(x, t)|^2 dx = e^{2\Gamma t/\hbar} \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} |\psi(x)|^2 dx = e^{2\Gamma t/\hbar}.$$

For conservation of probability to hold for all times $t$, we require:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{2\Gamma t/\hbar}\right) = \frac{2\Gamma}{\hbar}e^{2\Gamma t/\hbar} = 0 \implies \Gamma = 0.$$

Hence, the imaginary part of $E$ must vanish, proving $E = E_0 \in \mathbb{R}$.

Part (b): Proof that $\psi(x)$ can always be taken as real

The Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation (TISE) is:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi(x)}{dx^2} + V(x)\psi(x) = E\psi(x).$$

Since $\hbar, m, V(x),$ and $E$ are purely real, taking the complex conjugate gives:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi^*(x)}{dx^2} + V(x)\psi^*(x) = E\psi^*(x).$$

Thus, if $\psi(x)$ satisfies the TISE with energy $E$, then its complex conjugate $\psi^*(x)$ is also an exact solution with the same energy $E$. Constructing real linear combinations:

$$\psi_1(x) \equiv \frac{\psi(x) + \psi^*(x)}{2} = \text{Re}[\psi(x)], \qquad \psi_2(x) \equiv \frac{\psi(x) - \psi^*(x)}{2i} = \text{Im}[\psi(x)].$$

Both $\psi_1(x)$ and $\psi_2(x)$ are real eigenfunctions with energy $E$.

Part (c): Proof of definite parity for symmetric potentials $V(-x) = V(x)$

Given $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi(x)}{dx^2} + V(x)\psi(x) = E\psi(x)$, substitute $x \to -x$. Using $V(-x) = V(x)$:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi(-x)}{dx^2} + V(x)\psi(-x) = E\psi(-x).$$

Constructing the symmetric (even) and antisymmetric (odd) superpositions:

$$\psi_{\text{even}}(x) \equiv \frac{\psi(x) + \psi(-x)}{2}, \qquad \psi_{\text{odd}}(x) \equiv \frac{\psi(x) - \psi(-x)}{2}.$$

Both are valid energy eigenstates with eigenvalue $E$, having definite parity $\psi_{\text{even}}(-x) = \psi_{\text{even}}(x)$ and $\psi_{\text{odd}}(-x) = -\psi_{\text{odd}}(x)$.

Final Result / Conclusion:
Proven: (a) $E$ must be strictly real ($\Gamma = 0$); (b) $\psi(x)$ can always be chosen real; (c) for symmetric potentials $V(-x) = V(x)$, eigenstates have definite even or odd parity.
Problem 2.2 Minimum Energy Theorem ★

Show that $E$ must exceed the minimum value of $V(x)$, for every normalizable solution to the time-independent Schrödinger equation: $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + V(x)\psi = E\psi$. What is the classical analog to this statement?

Hint / Note
Rewrite the time-independent Schrödinger equation in the form $$\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2} [V(x) - E] \psi;$$ if $E < V_{\text{min}}$, then $\psi$ and its second derivative always have the same sign—argue that such a function cannot be normalized.
Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Rewrite the Schrödinger equation in curvature form.

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + V(x)\psi = E\psi \implies \frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}[V(x) - E]\psi(x).$$

Step 2: Sign analysis of the second derivative for $E < V_{\text{min}}$.

Let $V_{\text{min}}$ be the global minimum of $V(x)$, so $V(x) \ge V_{\text{min}}$ for all $x \in (-\infty, \infty)$.

If $E < V_{\text{min}}$, then $V(x) - E > 0$ strictly for all $x$. Therefore, the factor $\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}[V(x) - E]$ is always positive.

This means $\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}$ always has the exact same sign as $\psi(x)$:

  • Where $\psi(x) > 0$, $\psi''(x) > 0 \implies$ the function is concave upwards (curves away from the $x$-axis).
  • Where $\psi(x) < 0$, $\psi''(x) < 0 \implies$ the function is concave downwards (curves away from the $x$-axis).

Step 3: Why normalizability fails.

For $\psi(x)$ to be normalizable, we must have $\psi(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$.

Assume $\psi(x) > 0$ as $x \to -\infty$ with $\psi(-\infty) = 0$. Since $\psi > 0$, its slope must be positive ($\psi' > 0$) as it rises away from $0$. Since $\psi'' > 0$, the slope $\psi'$ continues to increase monotonically. For $\psi(x)$ to ever return to $0$ as $x \to +\infty$, its slope would have to turn negative ($\psi' < 0$), which requires a region of downward concavity ($\psi'' < 0$). But $\psi''$ can never be negative while $\psi > 0$.

Thus, $\psi(x)$ diverges exponentially to $+\infty$ as $x \to +\infty$. Such a solution cannot be normalized ($\int_{-\infty}^\infty |\psi|^2 dx = \infty$).

Therefore, we must have $E > V_{\text{min}}$ for every normalizable stationary state.

Step 4: Classical analog.

Classically, total mechanical energy is $E = T + V(x) = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V(x)$. Because momentum is real, kinetic energy $T = \frac{p^2}{2m} \ge 0$, which mandates $E \ge V(x) \ge V_{\text{min}}$. A particle cannot have total energy less than the lowest potential energy in the system.

Final Result / Conclusion:
Proven: $E > V_{\text{min}}$ for all normalizable states. Classical analog: kinetic energy is non-negative ($T = E - V \ge 0 \implies E \ge V_{\text{min}}$).

Example 2.2 Parabolic Initial State in the Infinite Square Well

A particle in the infinite square well potential ($V(x) = 0$ for $0 \le x \le a$, $\infty$ otherwise) has the initial wave function:

$$\Psi(x, 0) = Ax(a - x), \quad (0 \le x \le a),$$

for some constant $A$ (see Figure 2.3). Outside the well, $\Psi = 0$. Find $\Psi(x, t)$.

Figure 2.3: The starting wave function $\Psi(x, 0) = Ax(a - x)$ in Example 2.2.
Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Normalization of $\Psi(x, 0) = Ax(a - x)$.

$$\begin{aligned} 1 &= \int_0^a |\Psi(x, 0)|^2 dx = A^2 \int_0^a x^2(a - x)^2 dx \\ &= A^2 \int_0^a (a^2 x^2 - 2ax^3 + x^4) dx \\ &= A^2 \left[ a^2\frac{x^3}{3} - 2a\frac{x^4}{4} + \frac{x^5}{5} \right]_0^a \\ &= A^2 a^5 \left( \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{5} \right) = A^2 a^5 \left( \frac{10 - 15 + 6}{30} \right) = A^2 \frac{a^5}{30}. \end{aligned}$$

$$1 = A^2 \frac{a^5}{30} \implies A = \sqrt{\frac{30}{a^5}}.$$

Step 2: Explicit Integration for Fourier coefficients $c_n$.

The expansion coefficients are $c_n = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sqrt{\frac{30}{a^5}} I_n = \frac{2\sqrt{15}}{a^3} I_n$, where $I_n \equiv \int_0^a (ax - x^2)\sin(kx) dx$ with $k \equiv \frac{n\pi}{a}$.

Performing integration by parts with $u = ax - x^2, dv = \sin(kx)dx$:

$$\begin{aligned} I_n &= \left[ -(ax - x^2)\frac{\cos(kx)}{k} \right]_0^a + \frac{1}{k}\int_0^a (a - 2x)\cos(kx) dx \\ &= 0 + \frac{1}{k}\left( \left[ (a - 2x)\frac{\sin(kx)}{k} \right]_0^a - \int_0^a (-2)\frac{\sin(kx)}{k} dx \right) \\ &= \frac{2}{k^2}\left[ -\frac{\cos(kx)}{k} \right]_0^a = \frac{2}{k^3}[1 - \cos(n\pi)] = \frac{2a^3}{n^3\pi^3}[1 - (-1)^n]. \end{aligned}$$

Thus $c_n = 0$ for even $n$, and for odd $n$:

$$c_n = \frac{2\sqrt{15}}{a^3} \cdot \frac{4a^3}{n^3\pi^3} = \frac{8\sqrt{15}}{\pi^3 n^3}.$$

Step 3: Assemble the full time-dependent wave function $\Psi(x, t)$.

$$\Psi(x, t) = \frac{16\sqrt{30}}{\pi^3\sqrt{a}} \sum_{n=1,3,5,\dots}^\infty \frac{1}{n^3}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right) \exp\left(-i\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar}{2ma^2}t\right).$$

Final Result / Conclusion:
$\Psi(x, t) = \frac{16\sqrt{30}}{\pi^3\sqrt{a}} \sum_{n=1,3,5,\dots}^\infty \frac{1}{n^3}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right)\exp\left(-i\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar}{2ma^2}t\right)$.
Example 2.3 Energy Probability and Expectation Value for Parabolic State

Check that the probability conservation sum $\sum_{n=1}^\infty |c_n|^2 = 1$ is satisfied for the wave function in Example 2.2: where $\Psi(x, 0) = \sqrt{\frac{30}{a^5}}x(a - x)$ and $c_n = \frac{8\sqrt{15}}{\pi^3 n^3}$ for odd $n$. If you measured the energy of a particle in this state, what is the most probable result? What is the expectation value of the energy $\langle H \rangle$?

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Verify probability conservation $\sum_{n=1}^\infty |c_n|^2 = 1$.

$$\sum_{n=1,3,5,\dots} |c_n|^2 = \frac{960}{\pi^6} \sum_{n=1,3,5,\dots} \frac{1}{n^6} = \frac{960}{\pi^6} \left(\frac{\pi^6}{960}\right) = 1. \quad \checkmark$$

Step 2: Most probable energy measurement.

For ground state $n = 1$: $P(E_1) = |c_1|^2 = \frac{960}{\pi^6} \approx 0.998555 \quad (99.86\%)$.

Step 3: Calculate expectation value $\langle H \rangle = \sum |c_n|^2 E_n$.

$$\begin{aligned} \langle H \rangle &= \sum_{n=1,3,5,\dots} \left(\frac{960}{\pi^6 n^6}\right) \left(\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}\right) \\ &= \frac{480\hbar^2}{\pi^4 ma^2} \sum_{n=1,3,5,\dots} \frac{1}{n^4} \\ &= \frac{480\hbar^2}{\pi^4 ma^2} \left(\frac{\pi^4}{96}\right) = \frac{5\hbar^2}{ma^2}. \end{aligned}$$

Final Result / Conclusion:
$\sum |c_n|^2 = 1$; most probable energy is $E_1 = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}$ ($99.86\%$ probability); $\langle H \rangle = \frac{5\hbar^2}{ma^2} \approx 1.013 E_1$.
Problem 2.3 Absence of Zero and Negative Energy Solutions

Show that there is no acceptable solution to the (time-independent) Schrödinger equation for the infinite square well with $E = 0$ or $E < 0$. (This is a special case of the general theorem in Problem 2.2, but this time do it by explicitly solving the Schrödinger equation, and showing that you cannot satisfy the boundary conditions $\psi(0) = \psi(a) = 0$.)

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

Inside the well ($0 \le x \le a$), $V(x) = 0$. The TISE is $\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = -\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\psi$.

Case 1: $E = 0$.

$$\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = 0 \implies \psi(x) = Ax + B.$$

Boundary conditions: $\psi(0) = B = 0 \implies \psi(x) = Ax$. Then $\psi(a) = A a = 0 \implies A = 0$. Thus $\psi(x) \equiv 0$ (trivial).

Case 2: $E < 0$.

Let $E = -|E|$ and $k \equiv \frac{\sqrt{2m|E|}}{\hbar} > 0$. The solution is $\psi(x) = C\sinh(kx) + D\cosh(kx)$.

Boundary conditions: $\psi(0) = D = 0 \implies \psi(x) = C\sinh(kx)$.

Then $\psi(a) = C\sinh(ka) = 0$. Since $ka > 0$, $\sinh(ka) > 0$ strictly, forcing $C = 0 \implies \psi(x) \equiv 0$.

Thus, no physical state exists for $E \le 0$.

Final Result / Conclusion:
No non-trivial solution exists for $E \le 0$ because satisfying boundary conditions $\psi(0) = \psi(a) = 0$ forces $\psi(x) = 0$ everywhere.
Problem 2.4 Uncertainties in the Infinite Square Well ★

Calculate $\langle x \rangle$, $\langle x^2 \rangle$, $\langle p \rangle$, $\langle p^2 \rangle$, $\sigma_x$, and $\sigma_p$, for the $n$th stationary state of the infinite square well:

$$\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right).$$

Check that the Heisenberg uncertainty principle $\sigma_x \sigma_p \ge \hbar/2$ is satisfied. Which state comes closest to the uncertainty limit?

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

For $\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sin(kx)$ with $k = \frac{n\pi}{a}$:

1. Position expectation values $\langle x \rangle$ and $\langle x^2 \rangle$:

$$\langle x \rangle = \frac{2}{a}\int_0^a x\sin^2(kx) dx = \frac{a}{2}.$$

$$\begin{aligned} \langle x^2 \rangle &= \frac{2}{a}\int_0^a x^2\sin^2(kx) dx = \frac{1}{a}\int_0^a x^2(1 - \cos(2kx)) dx \\ &= \frac{a^2}{3} - \frac{1}{a}\left( \left[ x^2\frac{\sin(2kx)}{2k} \right]_0^a - \int_0^a 2x\frac{\sin(2kx)}{2k} dx \right) \\ &= \frac{a^2}{3} - \frac{a^2}{2n^2\pi^2} = a^2\left(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{2n^2\pi^2}\right). \end{aligned}$$

$$\sigma_x = a\sqrt{\frac{1}{12} - \frac{1}{2n^2\pi^2}}.$$

2. Momentum expectation values $\langle p \rangle$ and $\langle p^2 \rangle$:

$$\langle p \rangle = 0, \qquad \langle p^2 \rangle = \left(\frac{n\pi\hbar}{a}\right)^2 \implies \sigma_p = \frac{n\pi\hbar}{a}.$$

3. Uncertainty product $\sigma_x \sigma_p$:

$$\sigma_x \sigma_p = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sqrt{\frac{n^2\pi^2}{3} - 2} \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}.$$

Final Result / Conclusion:
$\langle x \rangle = \frac{a}{2}$, $\langle x^2 \rangle = a^2(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{2n^2\pi^2})$, $\langle p \rangle = 0$, $\langle p^2 \rangle = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{a^2}$, $\sigma_x\sigma_p = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sqrt{\frac{n^2\pi^2}{3}-2} \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}$.
Problem 2.5 Superposition of Ground and First Excited States ★

A particle in the infinite square well has as its initial wave function an even mixture of the first two stationary states:

$$\Psi(x, 0) = A [\psi_1(x) + \psi_2(x)].$$

  • (a)
    Normalize $\Psi(x, 0)$.
  • (b)
    Find $\Psi(x, t)$ and $|\Psi(x, t)|^2$.
  • (c)
    Compute $\langle x \rangle$. Notice that it oscillates in time. What is the angular frequency of the oscillation? What is the amplitude of the oscillation?
  • (d)
    Compute $\langle p \rangle$ using $\langle p \rangle = m \frac{d\langle x \rangle}{dt}$.
  • (e)
    If you measured the energy of this particle, what values might you get, and what is the probability of getting each of them? Find the expectation value of $H$.
Detailed Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Find Normalization Constant $A$

$$1 = |A|^2(1 + 1) = 2|A|^2 \implies A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.$$

Part (b): Construct $\Psi(x, t)$ and $|\Psi(x, t)|^2$

$$\Psi(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\psi_1(x)e^{-iE_1 t/\hbar} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\psi_2(x)e^{-iE_2 t/\hbar}.$$

$$|\Psi(x, t)|^2 = \frac{1}{2}\psi_1(x)^2 + \frac{1}{2}\psi_2(x)^2 + \psi_1(x)\psi_2(x)\cos(3\omega_0 t), \quad \text{where } \omega_0 \equiv \frac{\pi^2\hbar}{2ma^2}.$$

Part (c): Compute $\langle x \rangle(t)$

$$\begin{aligned} \langle x \rangle(t) &= \int_0^a x |\Psi(x, t)|^2 dx \\ &= \frac{1}{2}\int_0^a x\psi_1^2 dx + \frac{1}{2}\int_0^a x\psi_2^2 dx + \cos(3\omega_0 t)\int_0^a x\psi_1\psi_2 dx \\ &= \frac{a}{2} - \frac{16a}{9\pi^2}\cos(3\omega_0 t). \end{aligned}$$

Angular frequency is $\omega = 3\omega_0 = \frac{3\pi^2\hbar}{2ma^2}$. Amplitude is $M = \frac{16a}{9\pi^2} \approx 0.18a < a/2$.

Part (d): Compute $\langle p \rangle(t)$ via Ehrenfest's Theorem

$$\begin{aligned} \langle p \rangle(t) &= m\frac{d\langle x \rangle}{dt} = m \frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{a}{2} - \frac{16a}{9\pi^2}\cos(3\omega_0 t)\right] \\ &= m \frac{16a}{9\pi^2}(3\omega_0)\sin(3\omega_0 t) = \frac{8\hbar}{3a}\sin\left(\frac{3\pi^2\hbar}{2ma^2}t\right). \end{aligned}$$

Part (e): Energy Measurements & Expectation Value

$P(E_1) = 1/2$, $P(E_2) = 1/2$, and $\langle H \rangle = \frac{1}{2}(E_1 + E_2) = \frac{5\pi^2\hbar^2}{4ma^2}$.

Final Result / Conclusion:
(a) $A = 1/\sqrt{2}$; (b) $|\Psi|^2 = \frac{1}{2}\psi_1^2 + \frac{1}{2}\psi_2^2 + \psi_1\psi_2\cos(3\omega_0 t)$; (c) $\langle x \rangle = \frac{a}{2} - \frac{16a}{9\pi^2}\cos(3\omega_0 t)$; (d) $\langle p \rangle = \frac{8\hbar}{3a}\sin(3\omega_0 t)$; (e) $P(E_1)=P(E_2)=1/2$, $\langle H \rangle = \frac{5\pi^2\hbar^2}{4ma^2}$.
Problem 2.6 Effect of Relative Phase

Suppose we change the relative phase of $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ in Problem 2.5: $\Psi(x, 0) = A [\psi_1(x) + e^{i\phi}\psi_2(x)]$. Find $\Psi(x, t)$, $|\Psi(x, t)|^2$, and $\langle x \rangle$, and compare your results with what you got before. Study the special cases $\phi = \pi/2$ and $\phi = \pi$.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Normalization and full wave function $\Psi(x, t)$.

$$1 = |A|^2(1 + |e^{i\phi}|^2) = 2|A|^2 \implies A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.$$

$$\Psi(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\psi_1(x)e^{-iE_1 t/\hbar} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\psi_2(x)e^{i\phi}e^{-iE_2 t/\hbar}.$$

Step 2: Probability density $|\Psi(x, t)|^2$ and $\langle x \rangle(t)$.

$$|\Psi(x, t)|^2 = \frac{1}{2}\psi_1(x)^2 + \frac{1}{2}\psi_2(x)^2 + \psi_1(x)\psi_2(x)\cos(\omega t - \phi), \quad \text{where } \omega \equiv \frac{3\pi^2\hbar}{2ma^2}.$$

$$\langle x \rangle(t) = \frac{a}{2} - \frac{16a}{9\pi^2}\cos(\omega t - \phi).$$

Final Result / Conclusion:

Problem 2.7 Symmetric Triangular Wave Function ★

Example 2.4 Expectation Values via Ladder Operators

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Write down the ladder operator eigenvalue relation.

$$\hat{a}_+|n\rangle = \sqrt{n + 1}|n + 1\rangle, \qquad \hat{a}_-|n\rangle = \sqrt{n}|n - 1\rangle.$$

Step 2: Express position $\hat{x}$ and momentum $\hat{p}$ in terms of ladder operators.

$$\hat{x} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}(\hat{a}_+ + \hat{a}_-), \qquad \hat{p} = i\sqrt{\frac{\hbar m\omega}{2}}(\hat{a}_+ - \hat{a}_-).$$

Step 3: Calculate expectation value $\langle x \rangle = \langle n|\hat{x}|n\rangle$.

$$\langle x \rangle = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\langle n|(\hat{a}_+ + \hat{a}_-)|n\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}[\sqrt{n+1}\langle n|n+1\rangle + \sqrt{n}\langle n|n-1\rangle] = 0.$$

Step 4: Calculate expectation value $\langle x^2 \rangle = \langle n|\hat{x}^2|n\rangle$.

$$\begin{aligned} \langle x^2 \rangle &= \frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\langle n|(\hat{a}_+^2 + \hat{a}_+\hat{a}_- + \hat{a}_-\hat{a}_+ + \hat{a}_-^2)|n\rangle \\ &= \frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}(0 + n + (n+1) + 0) = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{\hbar}{m\omega}. \end{aligned}$$

Step 5: Calculate expectation value $\langle p^2 \rangle = \langle n|\hat{p}^2|n\rangle$.

$$\begin{aligned} \langle p^2 \rangle &= -\frac{\hbar m\omega}{2}\langle n|(\hat{a}_+^2 - \hat{a}_+\hat{a}_- - \hat{a}_-\hat{a}_+ + \hat{a}_-^2)|n\rangle \\ &= -\frac{\hbar m\omega}{2}(0 - n - (n+1) + 0) = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar m\omega. \end{aligned}$$

Step 6: Verify the uncertainty principle.

$$\sigma_x\sigma_p = \sqrt{\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)^2 \hbar^2} = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}.$$

Final Result / Conclusion:
$\langle x^2 \rangle = (n + 1/2)\frac{\hbar}{m\omega}$, $\langle p^2 \rangle = (n + 1/2)\hbar m\omega$, and $\sigma_x\sigma_p = (n + 1/2)\hbar \ge \hbar/2$.

Problem 2.10 Commutation Relations and Raising Property ★

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{a}_-\hat{a}_+ &= \frac{1}{2\hbar m\omega}(i\hat{p} + m\omega\hat{x})(-i\hat{p} + m\omega\hat{x}) \\ &= \frac{1}{2\hbar m\omega}(\hat{p}^2 + m^2\omega^2\hat{x}^2 + im\omega[\hat{x}, \hat{p}]) \\ &= \frac{\hat{H}}{\hbar\omega} + \frac{i}{2\hbar}(i\hbar) = \frac{\hat{H}}{\hbar\omega} - \frac{1}{2}. \end{aligned}$$

Applying to $\psi$ gives $\hat{H}(\hat{a}_+\psi) = (E + \hbar\omega)(\hat{a}_+\psi)$.

Final Result / Conclusion:
Proven: $[\hat{a}_-, \hat{a}_+] = 1$ and $\hat{H}(\hat{a}_+\psi) = (E + \hbar\omega)(\hat{a}_+\psi)$.

Problem 2.23 Properties of the Delta Function and Step Function ★

Delta functions live under integral signs, and two expressions ($D_1(x)$ and $D_2(x)$) involving delta functions are said to be equal if $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} f(x) D_1(x)\, dx = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} f(x) D_2(x)\, dx$ for every test function $f(x)$.

  • (a)
    Show that: $$\delta(cx) = \frac{1}{|c|}\delta(x),$$ where $c$ is a real constant. (Be sure to check the case where $c$ is negative.)
  • (b)
    Let $\theta(x)$ be the step function: $$\theta(x) \equiv \begin{cases} 1, & x > 0, \\ 0, & x < 0. \end{cases}$$ (In the rare case where it actually matters, define $\theta(0) = 1/2$.) Show that $\frac{d\theta}{dx} = \delta(x)$.
Detailed Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Prove $\delta(cx) = \frac{1}{|c|}\delta(x)$

Let $f(x)$ be an arbitrary smooth test function. Consider the integral $\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\delta(cx) dx$.

Perform the $u$-substitution: let $y = cx \implies x = y/c \implies dx = \frac{dy}{c}$.

Case 1 ($c > 0$): As $x \to -\infty, y \to -\infty$ and as $x \to +\infty, y \to +\infty$:

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\delta(cx) dx = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f\left(\frac{y}{c}\right)\delta(y)\frac{dy}{c} = \frac{1}{c}f\left(\frac{0}{c}\right) = \frac{1}{c}f(0) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\left[\frac{1}{c}\delta(x)\right] dx.$$

Case 2 ($c < 0$): As $x \to -\infty, y \to +\infty$ and as $x \to +\infty, y \to -\infty$. Reversing the limits introduces a minus sign:

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\delta(cx) dx = \int_{+\infty}^{-\infty} f\left(\frac{y}{c}\right)\delta(y)\frac{dy}{c} = -\frac{1}{c}\int_{-\infty}^\infty f\left(\frac{y}{c}\right)\delta(y) dy = -\frac{1}{c}f(0) = \frac{1}{|c|}f(0).$$

Combining both cases yields: $\delta(cx) = \frac{1}{|c|}\delta(x)$.

Part (b): Prove $\frac{d\theta}{dx} = \delta(x)$

The step function is defined as $\theta(x) = 1$ for $x > 0$ and $0$ for $x < 0$.

Apply integration by parts on $\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\frac{d\theta}{dx} dx$ where $f(x)$ vanishes at $\pm\infty$:

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\frac{d\theta}{dx} dx = [f(x)\theta(x)]_{-\infty}^\infty - \int_{-\infty}^\infty f'(x)\theta(x) dx = 0 - \int_0^\infty f'(x) dx = -[f(\infty) - f(0)] = f(0).$$

Since $\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\delta(x) dx = f(0)$, this proves $\frac{d\theta}{dx} = \delta(x)$.

Final Result / Conclusion:
Proven: (a) $\delta(cx) = \frac{1}{|c|}\delta(x)$; (b) $\frac{d\theta}{dx} = \delta(x)$.
Problem 2.24 Uncertainty Principle for the Delta-Function Bound State ★★

Check the uncertainty principle for the delta-function bound-state wave function:

$$\psi(x) = \frac{\sqrt{m\alpha}}{\hbar} e^{-m\alpha|x|/\hbar^2}.$$

Hint / Note
Calculating $\langle p^2 \rangle$ can be tricky, because the derivative of $\psi$ has a step discontinuity at $x = 0$. You may want to use the result in Problem 2.23(b): $\frac{d\theta}{dx} = \delta(x)$. Partial answer: $\langle p^2 \rangle = (m\alpha/\hbar)^2$.
Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

The delta bound state is $\psi(x) = \sqrt{\kappa}e^{-\kappa |x|}$ where $\kappa = \frac{m\alpha}{\hbar^2}$.

Step 1: Calculate $\langle x \rangle$ and $\langle x^2 \rangle$.

By parity, $\psi(-x) = \psi(x) \implies |\psi(x)|^2$ is symmetric $\implies \langle x \rangle = 0$.

$$\langle x^2 \rangle = \int_{-\infty}^\infty x^2 |\psi(x)|^2 dx = 2\kappa \int_0^\infty x^2 e^{-2\kappa x} dx.$$

Integrating by parts twice: $\int_0^\infty x^2 e^{-2\kappa x} dx = \frac{2!}{(2\kappa)^3} = \frac{2}{8\kappa^3} = \frac{1}{4\kappa^3}$.

$$\langle x^2 \rangle = 2\kappa\left(\frac{1}{4\kappa^3}\right) = \frac{1}{2\kappa^2} \implies \sigma_x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\kappa} = \frac{\hbar^2}{\sqrt{2}m\alpha}.$$

Step 2: Calculate $\langle p \rangle$ and $\langle p^2 \rangle$.

By parity, $\langle p \rangle = 0$.

For $\langle p^2 \rangle = -\hbar^2 \int_{-\infty}^\infty \psi(x)\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} dx$, we compute the distributional derivative:

$$\frac{d\psi}{dx} = -\kappa \text{sgn}(x)\psi(x) = -\kappa [2\theta(x) - 1]\psi(x).$$

Differentiating using the product rule and $\theta'(x) = \delta(x)$:

$$\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = -\kappa [2\delta(x)]\psi(x) - \kappa[2\theta(x) - 1]\frac{d\psi}{dx} = -2\kappa\delta(x)\psi(0) + \kappa^2 \psi(x).$$

Substitute this into the expectation value integral:

$$\langle p^2 \rangle = -\hbar^2 \int_{-\infty}^\infty \psi(x)\left[-2\kappa\delta(x)\psi(0) + \kappa^2\psi(x)\right] dx = -\hbar^2\left[-2\kappa|\psi(0)|^2 + \kappa^2\int |\psi|^2 dx\right].$$

Since $|\psi(0)|^2 = \kappa$ and $\int |\psi|^2 dx = 1$:

$$\langle p^2 \rangle = -\hbar^2[-2\kappa(\kappa) + \kappa^2(1)] = -\hbar^2[-\kappa^2] = \hbar^2\kappa^2 = \left(\frac{m\alpha}{\hbar}\right)^2 \implies \sigma_p = \frac{m\alpha}{\hbar}.$$

Step 3: Uncertainty product $\sigma_x \sigma_p$.

$$\sigma_x \sigma_p = \left(\frac{\hbar^2}{\sqrt{2}m\alpha}\right)\left(\frac{m\alpha}{\hbar}\right) = \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707\hbar > 0.5\hbar = \frac{\hbar}{2}. \quad \checkmark$$

Final Result / Conclusion:
$\sigma_x = \frac{\hbar^2}{\sqrt{2}m\alpha}$, $\sigma_p = \frac{m\alpha}{\hbar}$, and $\sigma_x\sigma_p = \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2}} \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}$.
Problem 2.25 Orthogonality of Bound and Scattering States

Check that the bound state of the delta-function well ($\psi(x) = \frac{\sqrt{m\alpha}}{\hbar} e^{-m\alpha|x|/\hbar^2}$) is orthogonal to the scattering states:

$$\psi_k(x) = \begin{cases} A e^{ikx} + B e^{-ikx}, & x < 0, \\ F e^{ikx}, & x > 0. \end{cases}$$

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Write down the overlap integral.

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \psi_b^*(x)\psi_k(x) dx = \int_{-\infty}^0 \sqrt{\kappa}e^{\kappa x}(A e^{ikx} + B e^{-ikx}) dx + \int_0^\infty \sqrt{\kappa}e^{-\kappa x}(F e^{ikx}) dx$$

$$= \sqrt{\kappa}\left[ A\int_{-\infty}^0 e^{(\kappa + ik)x} dx + B\int_{-\infty}^0 e^{(\kappa - ik)x} dx + F\int_0^\infty e^{-(\kappa - ik)x} dx \right].$$

Step 2: Explicit integration.

$$= \sqrt{\kappa}\left[ \left[\frac{e^{(\kappa + ik)x}}{\kappa + ik}\right]_{-\infty}^0 + \left[\frac{e^{(\kappa - ik)x}}{\kappa - ik}\right]_{-\infty}^0 + \left[\frac{e^{-(\kappa - ik)x}}{-(\kappa - ik)}\right]_0^\infty \right] = \sqrt{\kappa}\left[ \frac{A}{\kappa + ik} + \frac{B}{\kappa - ik} + \frac{F}{\kappa - ik} \right].$$

Combine over a common denominator $(\kappa + ik)(\kappa - ik) = \kappa^2 + k^2$:

$$= \frac{\sqrt{\kappa}}{\kappa^2 + k^2}[A(\kappa - ik) + B(\kappa + ik) + F(\kappa + ik)] = \frac{\sqrt{\kappa}}{\kappa^2 + k^2}[\kappa(A + B + F) - ik(A - B - F)].$$

Step 3: Apply boundary conditions.

Continuity at $x = 0 \implies A + B = F \implies A + B + F = 2F$.

Derivative jump at $x = 0 \implies \psi'(0^+) - \psi'(0^-) = ikF - (ikA - ikB) = -2\kappa F \implies -ik(A - B - F) = -2\kappa F$.

Substitute these into the numerator:

$$\text{Numerator} = \kappa(2F) + (-2\kappa F) = 2\kappa F - 2\kappa F = 0.$$

Thus the overlap integral is strictly zero, proving bound and scattering states are orthogonal.

Final Result / Conclusion:
Bound and scattering states of the delta-function potential are strictly orthogonal ($\int_{-\infty}^\infty \psi_b^* \psi_k dx = 0$).

Problem 2.36 Centered Infinite Square Well

Solve the time-independent Schrödinger equation with appropriate boundary conditions for the “centered” infinite square well: $V(x) = 0$ (for $-a < x < +a$), $V(x) = \infty$ (otherwise). Check that your allowed energies are consistent with $E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m(2a)^2}$, and confirm that your $\psi$s can be obtained from the standard well by the substitution $x \to (x + a)/2$ (and appropriate renormalization). Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Solve the TISE inside the well ($-a < x < a$).

Inside the well, $V(x) = 0$, so $\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = -k^2\psi$ with $k = \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}$.

General solution: $\psi(x) = A\cos(kx) + B\sin(kx)$.

Step 2: Apply boundary conditions $\psi(\pm a) = 0$.

  • Even Solutions ($B = 0$): $\psi(x) = A\cos(kx)$. Boundary condition $\psi(\pm a) = A\cos(ka) = 0 \implies ka = \frac{n\pi}{2}$ for odd integers $n = 1, 3, 5, \dots$.
  • Odd Solutions ($A = 0$): $\psi(x) = B\sin(kx)$. Boundary condition $\psi(\pm a) = B\sin(ka) = 0 \implies ka = \frac{n\pi}{2}$ for even integers $n = 2, 4, 6, \dots$.

Allowed energy levels (with total width $W = 2a$):

$$k_n = \frac{n\pi}{2a} \implies E_n = \frac{\hbar^2 k_n^2}{2m} = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m(2a)^2} = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2}, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

Step 3: Normalization.

$$\int_{-a}^a |A|^2\cos^2\left(\frac{n\pi x}{2a}\right) dx = |A|^2 a = 1 \implies A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}.$$

Similarly $B = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}$.

Step 4: Coordinate transformation to standard well $[0, 2a]$.

Let $x' = x + a \in [0, 2a] \implies x = x' - a$.

For odd $n$: $\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{2a}\right) = \cos\left(\frac{n\pi(x' - a)}{2a}\right) = \cos\left(\frac{n\pi x'}{2a} - \frac{n\pi}{2}\right) = \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x'}{2a}\right)$ (up to an overall sign), matching the standard wave functions on width $2a$.

Final Result / Conclusion:
$\psi_n(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{2a}\right) & n = 1, 3, 5, \dots \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{2a}\right) & n = 2, 4, 6, \dots \end{cases}$ with $E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2}$.
Problem 2.37 Cubic Sine Initial State in the Infinite Square Well

A particle in the infinite square well ($0 \le x \le a$) has the initial wave function:

$$\Psi(x, 0) = A \sin^3\left(\frac{\pi x}{a}\right) \quad (0 \le x \le a).$$

Determine $A$, find $\Psi(x, t)$, and calculate $\langle x \rangle$ as a function of time. What is the expectation value of the energy?

Hint: Use the trigonometric identity $\sin^3\theta = \frac{3}{4}\sin\theta - \frac{1}{4}\sin(3\theta)$.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Trigonometric expansion of $\sin^3\left(\frac{\pi x}{a}\right)$.

Using Euler's identity $\sin\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2i}$:

$$\sin^3\theta = \left(\frac{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2i}\right)^3 = -\frac{1}{8i}(e^{3i\theta} - 3e^{i\theta} + 3e^{-i\theta} - e^{-3i\theta}) = \frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2i}\right) - \frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{e^{3i\theta} - e^{-3i\theta}}{2i}\right) = \frac{3}{4}\sin\theta - \frac{1}{4}\sin(3\theta).$$

Therefore, setting $\theta = \frac{\pi x}{a}$:

$$\Psi(x, 0) = A\left[ \frac{3}{4}\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{a}\right) - \frac{1}{4}\sin\left(\frac{3\pi x}{a}\right) \right].$$

Step 2: Express in stationary states $\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right)$.

$$\Psi(x, 0) = A\sqrt{\frac{a}{2}}\left[ \frac{3}{4}\psi_1(x) - \frac{1}{4}\psi_3(x) \right].$$

Normalize using orthonormality:

$$1 = |A|^2\frac{a}{2}\left[ \left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^2 + \left(-\frac{1}{4}\right)^2 \right] = |A|^2\frac{a}{2}\left( \frac{9 + 1}{16} \right) = |A|^2\frac{a}{2}\left(\frac{10}{16}\right) = |A|^2\frac{5a}{16}.$$

$$A = \sqrt{\frac{16}{5a}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{5a}}.$$

Substituting $A$: $\Psi(x, 0) = \frac{3}{\sqrt{10}}\psi_1(x) - \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\psi_3(x)$.

Step 3: Time Evolution $\Psi(x, t)$ and Expectation Values.

$$\Psi(x, t) = \frac{3}{\sqrt{10}}\psi_1(x)e^{-iE_1 t/\hbar} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\psi_3(x)e^{-iE_3 t/\hbar}, \quad \text{where } E_1 = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}, E_3 = 9E_1.$$

Energy Expectation Value: $\langle H \rangle = |c_1|^2 E_1 + |c_3|^2 E_3 = \left(\frac{9}{10}\right)E_1 + \left(\frac{1}{10}\right)(9E_1) = \frac{18}{10}E_1 = \frac{9}{5}E_1 = \frac{9\pi^2\hbar^2}{10ma^2}$.

Position Expectation Value: Both $\psi_1(x)$ and $\psi_3(x)$ are symmetric about the center of the well $x = a/2$. Therefore, the cross-term $\int_0^a x\psi_1\psi_3 dx = \int_{-a/2}^{a/2} (u + a/2)\psi_1(u)\psi_3(u) du = 0 + \frac{a}{2}(0) = 0$.

Thus, $\langle x \rangle(t) = \frac{a}{2}$ remains strictly constant in time.

Final Result / Conclusion:
$A = \frac{4}{\sqrt{5a}}$, $\Psi(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}[3\psi_1(x)e^{-iE_1 t/\hbar} - \psi_3(x)e^{-i 9E_1 t/\hbar}]$, $\langle H \rangle = \frac{9\pi^2\hbar^2}{10ma^2}$, and $\langle x \rangle(t) = \frac{a}{2}$.
Problem 2.38 Quantum Revival Time vs Classical Period

Consider revivals in the infinite square well:

  • (a)
    Show that the wave function of a particle in the infinite square well returns to its original form after a quantum revival time $T = \frac{4ma^2}{\pi\hbar}$. That is: $\Psi(x, T) = \Psi(x, 0)$ for any state (not just a stationary state).
  • (b)
    What is the classical revival time (period) $T_{\text{cl}}$ for a particle of energy $E$ bouncing back and forth between the walls?
  • (c)
    For what energy are the two revival times equal?
Detailed Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Quantum Revival Time $T$

For the infinite square well, $E_n = n^2 E_1$ with $E_1 = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}$.

The time-dependent wave function is $\Psi(x, t) = \sum c_n \psi_n(x) e^{-i E_n t/\hbar} = \sum c_n \psi_n(x) e^{-i n^2 E_1 t/\hbar}$.

At time $T = \frac{4ma^2}{\pi\hbar}$:

$$\frac{E_1 T}{\hbar} = \left(\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}\right)\left(\frac{4ma^2}{\pi\hbar}\right)\frac{1}{\hbar} = 2\pi.$$

Therefore, the phase factor for level $n$ is: $e^{-i n^2 (2\pi)} = (e^{-2\pi i})^{n^2} = (1)^{n^2} = 1$ for every integer $n$.

Thus: $\Psi(x, T) = \sum c_n \psi_n(x)(1) = \Psi(x, 0)$ for *any* arbitrary state!

Part (b): Classical Period $T_{\text{cl}}$

A classical particle with kinetic energy $E = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ travels at speed $v = \sqrt{2E/m}$.

In one complete back-and-forth cycle, it traverses a round-trip distance of $2a$:

$$T_{\text{cl}} = \frac{2a}{v} = \frac{2a}{\sqrt{2E/m}} = a\sqrt{\frac{2m}{E}}.$$

Part (c): Energy for Equal Revival Times

Equating $T_{\text{cl}} = T$:

$$a\sqrt{\frac{2m}{E}} = \frac{4ma^2}{\pi\hbar} \implies \sqrt{\frac{2m}{E}} = \frac{4ma}{\pi\hbar} \implies \frac{2m}{E} = \frac{16m^2 a^2}{\pi^2\hbar^2} \implies E = \frac{2m\pi^2\hbar^2}{16m^2 a^2} = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2} = \frac{1}{4}E_1.$$

Final Result / Conclusion:
(a) Proven: $T = \frac{4ma^2}{\pi\hbar}$; (b) $T_{\text{cl}} = a\sqrt{2m/E}$; (c) $E = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2} = \frac{1}{4}E_1$.

Even States: For $x > 0$, $\psi(x) = A\sin(k(a - x))$. At $x = 0$, $\psi(0) = A\sin(ka)$ and $\psi'(0^+) = -kA\cos(ka)$.

Derivative jump: $\psi'(0^+) - \psi'(0^-) = 2\psi'(0^+) = -2kA\cos(ka) = \frac{2m\alpha}{\hbar^2}A\sin(ka)$.

$$\implies -\cot(ka) = \frac{m\alpha}{\hbar^2 k}.$$

Final Result / Conclusion:
Odd states: unaffected, $E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2}$ ($n$ even). Even states: allowed energies satisfy $-\cot(ka) = \frac{m\alpha}{\hbar^2 k}$.
Problem 2.44 Non-Degeneracy of One-Dimensional Bound States

If two (or more) distinct solutions to the time-independent Schrödinger equation have the same energy $E$, these states are said to be degenerate. Prove the following theorem: In one dimension ($-\infty < x < \infty$) there are no degenerate bound states.

Hint / Note
Suppose there are two solutions, $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$, with the same energy $E$. Multiply the Schrödinger equation for $\psi_1$ by $\psi_2$, and the Schrödinger equation for $\psi_2$ by $\psi_1$, and subtract, to show that $\left(\psi_2 \frac{d\psi_1}{dx} - \psi_1 \frac{d\psi_2}{dx}\right)$ is constant. Use normalizability ($\psi \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$) to show that this constant is zero. Conclude that $\psi_2$ is a multiple of $\psi_1$.
Detailed Step-by-Step Solution

Suppose $\psi_1(x)$ and $\psi_2(x)$ are two bound state solutions with the same energy $E$:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi_1'' + V\psi_1 = E\psi_1, \qquad -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi_2'' + V\psi_2 = E\psi_2.$$

Multiplying the first by $\psi_2$ and the second by $\psi_1$ and subtracting:

$$\psi_1\psi_2'' - \psi_2\psi_1'' = 0 \implies \frac{d}{dx}[\psi_1\psi_2' - \psi_2\psi_1'] = 0 \implies W(x) = \text{constant}.$$

For bound states, $\psi_1(x) \to 0$ and $\psi_2(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$. Thus $W(\infty) = 0$, which means $W(x) = 0$ everywhere:

$$\psi_1\psi_2' - \psi_2\psi_1' = 0 \implies \frac{\psi_1'}{\psi_1} = \frac{\psi_2'}{\psi_2} \implies \ln\psi_1 = \ln\psi_2 + \text{const} \implies \psi_2(x) = C\psi_1(x).$$

Thus $\psi_2$ is a constant multiple of $\psi_1$, proving that the state is non-degenerate.

Final Result / Conclusion:
Proven: All 1D bound states are non-degenerate because the Wronskian of any two solutions vanishes identically.
Problem 2.45 Node Theorem for 1D Bound States

In this problem you will show that the number of nodes of the stationary states of a one-dimensional potential always increases with energy. Consider two real, normalized solutions ($\psi_n$ and $\psi_m$) with energies $E_n > E_m$.

  • (a)
    Show that: $$\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{d\psi_m}{dx}\psi_n - \psi_m\frac{d\psi_n}{dx}\right] = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E_n - E_m)\psi_m\psi_n.$$
  • (b)
    Let $x_1$ and $x_2$ be two adjacent nodes of $\psi_m(x)$. Show that: $$\psi'_m(x_2)\psi_n(x_2) - \psi'_m(x_1)\psi_n(x_1) = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E_n - E_m)\int_{x_1}^{x_2} \psi_m\psi_n\, dx.$$
  • (c)
    If $\psi_n(x)$ has no nodes between $x_1$ and $x_2$, then it must have the same sign everywhere in the interval. Show that (b) then leads to a contradiction. Therefore, between every pair of nodes of $\psi_m(x)$, $\psi_n(x)$ must have at least one node.
Detailed Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Differentiating the Cross-Wronskian

$$\frac{d}{dx}[\psi_m'\psi_n - \psi_m\psi_n'] = \psi_m''\psi_n - \psi_m\psi_n'' = \left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(V - E_m)\psi_m\right)\psi_n - \psi_m\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(V - E_n)\psi_n\right) = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E_n - E_m)\psi_m\psi_n.$$

Part (b): Integration between Adjacent Nodes $x_1, x_2$ of $\psi_m$

Integrating from $x_1$ to $x_2$, where $\psi_m(x_1) = \psi_m(x_2) = 0$:

$$[\psi_m'\psi_n - \psi_m\psi_n']_{x_1}^{x_2} = \psi_m'(x_2)\psi_n(x_2) - \psi_m'(x_1)\psi_n(x_1) = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E_n - E_m)\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\psi_m\psi_n dx.$$

Part (c): Proof by Contradiction

Assume $\psi_n$ has no node in $[x_1, x_2]$, so both $\psi_m > 0$ and $\psi_n > 0$ on $(x_1, x_2)$.

Then the RHS integral is strictly positive (since $E_n > E_m$).

On the LHS: $\psi_m'(x_1) > 0$ (entering positive region) and $\psi_m'(x_2) < 0$ (leaving positive region).

Thus $\text{LHS} = \psi_m'(x_2)\psi_n(x_2) - \psi_m'(x_1)\psi_n(x_1) = (-)(+) - (+)(+) < 0$.

This contradicts $\text{RHS} > 0$. Hence $\psi_n(x)$ must cross zero at least once between $x_1$ and $x_2$.

Final Result / Conclusion:
Proven: Between any two consecutive nodes of an eigenstate $\psi_m$, every higher-energy state $\psi_n$ ($E_n > E_m$) has at least one node.

Final Result / Conclusion:
$\psi_n^\pm(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}e^{\pm i 2\pi n x/L}$ with $E_n = \frac{2n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{mL^2}$. Degeneracy occurs because periodic boundaries allow a non-zero Wronskian.

$$M_{11} = S_{21} - \frac{S_{22}S_{11}}{S_{12}}, \quad M_{12} = \frac{S_{22}}{S_{12}}, \quad M_{21} = -\frac{S_{11}}{S_{12}}, \quad M_{22} = \frac{1}{S_{12}}.$$

$$T_l = \frac{1}{|M_{22}|^2}, \qquad R_l = \left|\frac{M_{21}}{M_{22}}\right|^2.$$

Part (b): Composition Law $\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{M}_2 \mathbf{M}_1$

Let middle region amplitudes be $(C, D)$. Then $\begin{pmatrix} C \\ D \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{M}_1 \begin{pmatrix} A \\ B \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} F \\ G \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{M}_2 \begin{pmatrix} C \\ D \end{pmatrix} \implies \begin{pmatrix} F \\ G \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{M}_2 \mathbf{M}_1 \begin{pmatrix} A \\ B \end{pmatrix}$.

Parts (c) & (d): Single and Double Delta M-Matrices

For delta well at $x = x_0$ with $\beta = \frac{m\alpha}{\hbar^2}$:

$$\mathbf{M}(x_0) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + i\frac{\beta}{k} & i\frac{\beta}{k}e^{-2ikx_0} \\ -i\frac{\beta}{k}e^{2ikx_0} & 1 - i\frac{\beta}{k} \end{pmatrix}.$$

For double delta $V = -\alpha[\delta(x+a) + \delta(x-a)]$, $\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{M}(+a)\mathbf{M}(-a)$. The $(2,2)$ element is:

$$M_{22} = \left(1 - i\frac{\beta}{k}\right)^2 + \frac{\beta^2}{k^2}e^{-4ika} = 1 - 2i\frac{\beta}{k} - \frac{\beta^2}{k^2}(1 - e^{-4ika}).$$

$$T = \frac{1}{|M_{22}|^2} = \left[1 + \frac{4\beta^2}{k^2}\left(\cos(2ka) + \frac{\beta}{k}\sin(2ka)\right)^2\right]^{-1}.$$

This matches Problem 2.28.

Final Result / Conclusion:
$\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{M}_2\mathbf{M}_1$. Double delta transmission $T = [1 + \frac{4\beta^2}{k^2}(\cos(2ka) + \frac{\beta}{k}\sin(2ka))^2]^{-1}$, matching Problem 2.28.

Problem 2.58 Quantum Model for the Cohesion of Metals ★★★

In a monovalent metal, one electron per atom is free to roam throughout the object. What holds such a material together—why doesn’t it simply fall apart into a pile of individual atoms?

Figure 2.23: (a) $N$ electrons in individual wells of width $a$. (b) $N$ electrons in a single well of width $Na$ (Problem 2.58).
  • (a)
    Estimate the energy of $N$ isolated atoms, by treating each one as an electron in the ground state of an infinite square well of width $a$ (Figure 2.23(a)).
  • (b)
    When these atoms come together to form a metal, we get $N$ electrons in a single large infinite square well of width $Na$ (Figure 2.23(b)). Applying the Pauli exclusion principle (one electron per spatial state), what is the total ground-state energy for this system?
  • (c)
    The difference of these two energies is the cohesive energy of the metal. Find the cohesive energy per atom in the limit of large $N$.
  • (d)
    For a typical atomic separation $a \approx 4\text{ \AA}$, calculate the numerical value of the cohesive energy per atom in $\text{eV}$, and compare with experimental values ($2\text{--}4\text{ eV}$).
Detailed Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Energy of $N$ Isolated Atoms

Each isolated atom holds one electron in the ground state of an infinite well of width $a$:

$$E_{\text{isolated}} = N E_1 = N \left(\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}\right).$$

Part (b): Energy of $N$ Electrons in a Metal Box of Width $Na$

The energy levels of a well of width $W = Na$ are $\epsilon_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m(Na)^2} = \frac{n^2}{N^2}E_1$.

By the Pauli exclusion principle (one electron per spatial state), the $N$ electrons fill levels $n = 1, 2, \dots, N$:

$$E_{\text{metal}} = \sum_{n=1}^N \epsilon_n = \frac{E_1}{N^2}\sum_{n=1}^N n^2 = \frac{E_1}{N^2}\left[\frac{N(N+1)(2N+1)}{6}\right] \approx \frac{E_1}{N^2}\left(\frac{2N^3}{6}\right) = \frac{N E_1}{3}.$$

Part (c): Cohesive Energy per Atom

$$\frac{\Delta E}{N} = \frac{E_{\text{isolated}} - E_{\text{metal}}}{N} = E_1 - \frac{E_1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}E_1 = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{3ma^2}.$$

Part (d): Numerical Evaluation for $a = 4\text{ \AA}$

$$E_1 = \frac{\pi^2(1.0546\times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s})^2}{2(9.109\times 10^{-31}\text{ kg})(4\times 10^{-10}\text{ m})^2} = 3.763\times 10^{-19}\text{ J} = 2.349\text{ eV}.$$

$$\text{Cohesive energy per atom} = \frac{2}{3}(2.349\text{ eV}) = 1.57\text{ eV}.$$

This simple 1D quantum model gives remarkable order-of-magnitude agreement with typical experimental cohesive energies ($2\text{--}4\text{ eV}$).

Final Result / Conclusion:
(a) $E_{\text{iso}} = N E_1$; (b) $E_{\text{metal}} \approx \frac{1}{3}N E_1$; (c) cohesive energy per atom $= \frac{2}{3}E_1 = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{3ma^2}$; (d) for $a=4\text{ \AA}$, $\Delta E/N \approx 1.57\text{ eV}$.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Solution in terms of Airy Functions

The TISE for $x > 0$ is $\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(mgx - E)\psi$.

Final Result / Conclusion:
$E_n = -\left(\frac{mg^2\hbar^2}{2}\right)^{1/3} a_n$ where $a_n$ are the zeros of the Airy function $\text{Ai}(z)$.
Problem 2.60 The $1/x^2$ Potential and Scale Anomaly ★★★

$$V(x) = \begin{cases} -\alpha/x^2, & x > 0, \\ \infty, & x \le 0. \end{cases}$$

  • (a)

  • (b)

  • (c)
    Show that $\psi_\kappa(x) = A\sqrt{x} K_{ig}(\kappa x)$ satisfies the Schrödinger equation (where $K_{ig}$ is the modified Bessel function of imaginary order $ig$, with $g \equiv \sqrt{2m\alpha/\hbar^2 - 1/4}$). Show that this wave function has an infinite number of nodes near $x = 0$.

    Introduce a short-distance cutoff at $x = \epsilon$: $$V(x) = \begin{cases} -\alpha/x^2, & x > \epsilon, \\ \infty, & x \le \epsilon. \end{cases}$$ Show that the ground state energy now takes the form $E_0 = -\frac{\alpha}{\epsilon^2} f(\beta)$ for some dimensionless function $f$.
Detailed Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Dimensional Argument

To form energy $[E] = M L^2 T^{-2}$ from $m, \hbar, \alpha$: $[m^a \hbar^b \alpha^c] = M^{a+b+c} L^{2b+4c} T^{-b-2c}$.

Introducing a short-distance boundary at $x = \epsilon$ breaks continuous scale invariance into a discrete spectrum (quantum limit cycle): $E_0 = -\frac{\alpha}{\epsilon^2}f(\beta)$.

Dimensional analysis forbids a ground state without a cutoff length scale $\epsilon$; regularized ground state is $E_0 = -\frac{\alpha}{\epsilon^2}f(\beta)$.

The Boltzmann distribution gives the thermal probability of finding a system in state $n$ with energy $E_n$ at temperature $T$:

$$P(n) = \frac{1}{Z} e^{-\beta E_n}, \quad Z \equiv \sum_n e^{-\beta E_n}, \quad \beta \equiv \frac{1}{k_B T}.$$

  • (a)
    Show that the thermal average energy is $\bar{E} = -\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial \beta}$.
  • (b)
    For a quantum harmonic oscillator with $E_n = (n + 1/2)\hbar\omega$, sum the geometric series to show that: $$Z = \frac{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega/2}}{1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}.$$
  • (c)
    Show that the average energy of the quantum oscillator is: $$\bar{E} = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\left(\frac{1 + e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}{1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}\right) = \hbar\omega\left(\frac{1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1} + \frac{1}{2}\right).$$
  • (d)
    Model a crystal of $N$ atoms as $3N$ independent quantum oscillators. Show that the heat capacity per atom is: $$C = 3k_B \left(\frac{\theta_E}{T}\right)^2 \frac{e^{\theta_E/T}}{(e^{\theta_E/T} - 1)^2},$$ where $\theta_E \equiv \hbar\omega/k_B$ is the Einstein temperature.

Part (a): Average Energy Formula

$$\bar{E} = \frac{\sum E_n e^{-\beta E_n}}{\sum e^{-\beta E_n}} = -\frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial Z}{\partial \beta} = -\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial \beta}.$$

Part (b): Oscillator Partition Function $Z$

$$Z = \sum_{n=0}^\infty e^{-\beta(n + 1/2)\hbar\omega} = e^{-\beta\hbar\omega/2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty (e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})^n = \frac{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega/2}}{1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}.$$

Part (c): Oscillator Mean Energy $\bar{E}$

$$\bar{E} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta}\left[-\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2} - \ln(1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})\right] = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2} + \frac{\hbar\omega e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}{1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}} = \hbar\omega\left(\frac{1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1} + \frac{1}{2}\right).$$

Part (d) & (e): Heat Capacity of Einstein Solid

For $3N$ oscillators with $\beta = \frac{1}{k_B T}$ and $\theta_E = \frac{\hbar\omega}{k_B}$:

$$C = 3 \frac{\partial \bar{E}}{\partial T} = 3 \hbar\omega \frac{e^{\theta_E/T}(\theta_E/T^2)}{(e^{\theta_E/T} - 1)^2} = 3k_B \left(\frac{\theta_E}{T}\right)^2 \frac{e^{\theta_E/T}}{(e^{\theta_E/T} - 1)^2}.$$

Final Result / Conclusion:
$C = 3k_B \left(\frac{\theta_E}{T}\right)^2 \frac{e^{\theta_E/T}}{(e^{\theta_E/T} - 1)^2}$. Matches Dulong-Petit $3k_B$ at high $T$ and freezes out as $T \to 0$.
Problem 2.64 Legendre's Differential Equation and Legendre Polynomials

Legendre’s differential equation reads:

$$(1 - x^2)\frac{d^2 f}{dx^2} - 2x \frac{df}{dx} + \ell(\ell + 1)f = 0,$$

where $\ell$ is a non-negative real number.

  • (a)
    Assume a power series solution $f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n x^n$, and obtain a recursion relation for the constants $a_n$.
  • (b)
    Argue that unless the series truncates (which can only happen if $\ell$ is an integer), the solution will diverge at $x = \pm 1$.
  • (c)
    When $\ell$ is an integer, show that one of the two independent series truncates into a polynomial of degree $\ell$ (Legendre polynomials $P_\ell(x)$). Find $P_0(x)$, $P_1(x)$, $P_2(x)$, and $P_3(x)$ explicitly from the recursion relation.
Detailed Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Series Recursion Relation

Substitute $f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n x^n$ into the differential equation:

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty (n + 1)(n + 2)a_{n+2} x^n - \sum_{n=0}^\infty n(n - 1)a_n x^n - 2\sum_{n=0}^\infty n a_n x^n + \ell(\ell + 1)\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n x^n = 0.$$

$$a_{n+2} = \frac{n(n + 1) - \ell(\ell + 1)}{(n + 1)(n + 2)}a_n = \frac{(n - \ell)(n + \ell + 1)}{(n + 1)(n + 2)}a_n.$$

Part (b): Divergence at $x = \pm 1$

For large $n$, $\frac{a_{n+2}}{a_n} \to \frac{n^2}{n^2} = 1$, which is identical to the series for $\ln(1 - x^2)$. Thus unless the series terminates, $f(x)$ diverges logarithmically at $x = \pm 1$.

Part (c): Legendre Polynomials $P_\ell(x)$

When $\ell$ is an integer, $a_{n+2} = 0$ for $n = \ell$, terminating the series into a polynomial of degree $\ell$. With the standard convention $P_\ell(1) = 1$:

  • $\ell = 0$: $P_0(x) = 1$.
  • $\ell = 1$: $P_1(x) = x$.
  • $\ell = 2$: $a_2 = -\frac{2(3)}{2}a_0 = -3a_0 \implies P_2(x) = \frac{1}{2}(3x^2 - 1)$.
  • $\ell = 3$: $a_3 = -\frac{2(5)}{6}a_1 = -\frac{5}{3}a_1 \implies P_3(x) = \frac{1}{2}(5x^3 - 3x)$.
Final Result / Conclusion:
Recursion $a_{n+2} = \frac{(n - \ell)(n + \ell + 1)}{(n + 1)(n + 2)}a_n$. Polynomials: $P_0(x) = 1, P_1(x) = x, P_2(x) = \frac{1}{2}(3x^2 - 1), P_3(x) = \frac{1}{2}(5x^3 - 3x)$.
