Every solid is magnetic in some way. What kind depends on whether its atoms have permanent moments (unpaired electrons) and on how neighbouring moments line up: not at all, randomly, parallel, antiparallel and equal, antiparallel and unequal. Magnetic terminology

Put a solid in a magnetic field and it becomes magnetised: tiny current loops inside it line up, or are created, and add their own field. Three quantities describe this. The applied field strength $H$ (A/m) is set by the coils outside. The magnetisation $M$ (also A/m) is the magnetic moment per unit volume the solid develops. The flux density $B$ (tesla) is the total, field plus material. The susceptibility measures how strongly the solid responds:

$$\chi=\frac MH$$

In SI, $M$ and $H$ are both in A/m, so $\chi$ is dimensionless (in general a tensor; a scalar for isotropic media). Per gram-molecule it is the molar susceptibility $\chi_m$.

$$\mathbf B=\mu_0(\mathbf H+\mathbf M)=\mu_0(1+\chi)\mathbf H=\mu\mathbf H=\mu_0\mu_r\mathbf H,\qquad \mu_r=1+\chi$$ $\mu_0=4\pi\times10^{-7}$ H/m. $B$ in Wb/$\mathrm{m}^{2}$ (tesla). In vacuum $M=0$, $\chi=0$, $\mu_r=1$, $B=\mu_0H$.

 Where an atomic moment comes from

Moving charges are currents, and a current loop is a magnet. An atom has three sources of moment:

  • Orbital motion of the electrons: an electron going round a nucleus is a tiny loop current, with moment $\boldsymbol\mu_l=-\dfrac{e}{2m}\mathbf L$.
  • Spin of the electrons: $\boldsymbol\mu_s=-g\dfrac{e}{2m}\mathbf S$ with $g\approx2$, so one spin-$\tfrac12$ electron carries about one Bohr magneton.
  • Nuclear spin: smaller by about $m/M_p\approx1/1836$, ignored here.

Filled shells cancel in pairs (every spin up has a partner down, every orbit has a counter-rotating twin), so the moment of an atom comes from its unpaired electrons. The natural unit is the Bohr magneton,

$$\mu_B=\frac{e\hbar}{2m}=9.27\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{A\,m^2}\ (\mathrm{J\,T^{-1}})$$

Fe has four unpaired 3d electrons, $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$ five, so their moments are a few $\mu_B$. Atoms with only closed shells (inert gases, $\mathrm{Cu^{+}}$, $\mathrm{Cl^{-}}$) have none.

The five types Pick a type, then switch the field on. Diamagnets have no arrows until a field creates small opposing ones. Paramagnets have arrows that point anywhere; a field only tilts them a little. In the three ordered types the arrangement is there without a field, held by the exchange interaction between neighbours.

Above a critical temperature the differences among the ordered types disappear and every magnetic material becomes paramagnetic: heat scrambles the arrangement.

How to tell them apart from data. Sign of $\chi$: negative means diamagnetic. Size: $10^{-5}$–$10^{-3}$ means dia- or paramagnetic, $10^{2}$–$10^{5}$ means ferro- or ferrimagnetic. Temperature: plot $1/\chi$ against $T$ above the critical point; it is a straight line through the origin (para), cutting the axis at $+T_c$ (ferro) or extrapolating to $-\theta$ (antiferro).

Diamagnetism is present in every material; it is just usually swamped. A substance is called diamagnetic only when it has no permanent moments.

 A sample has $\chi=-2\times10^{-5}$ that does not change from 100 K to 400 K. What is it, and what can you say about its atoms?

Diamagnetic: negative, small, temperature independent. Its atoms or ions have no permanent moment (closed shells), so only the induced, Lenz-law response is left.

 Why does a filled shell contribute no permanent moment?

In a filled shell every orbital state $m_l$ is matched by $-m_l$ and every spin up by a spin down, so both the total orbital and the total spin angular momentum are zero, and the moments, being proportional to them, cancel.
