A field pulls the bound charges of a dielectric slightly apart: polarisation $P$. An atom feels not the average field $E$ but the local field $E+P/3\varepsilon_0$. Putting that into $P=N\alpha E_{\text{loc}}$ gives the Clausius–Mossotti relation between $\varepsilon_r$ and $\alpha$. Polarisation and susceptibility

Dielectrics are insulators: no free carriers, so no current flows. But their bound positive and negative charges can shift by a fraction of an atomic diameter in a field. Each atom or molecule becomes a small dipole $p$ (charge $\times$ separation), and the polarisation $P$ is the dipole moment per unit volume. The bound charges that appear on the surfaces make a polarisation field $E_p$ opposing the applied field:

$$\mathbf E=\mathbf E_0+\mathbf E_p,\qquad \mathbf P=\varepsilon_0\chi\mathbf E$$

$\chi$: the electric susceptibility. This is the electric twin of the magnetic story: $P$ plays the part of $M$, and $\chi$ measures how easily the material polarises.

$$\mathbf D=\varepsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r\mathbf E,\qquad \varepsilon_r=1+\chi,\qquad P=\varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r-1)E$$ $P$ in C/$\mathrm{m}^{2}$ (it equals the bound surface charge density), $\varepsilon_0=8.85\times10^{-12}$ F/m.

 The local field

The field at the site of an atom is different from the macroscopic $E$: it includes the fields of all the other polarised atoms, and in a dense solid they are close. It is built up in four pieces, $E_{\text{loc}}=E_0+E_1+E_2+E_3$:

The four contributions. $E_0$ from the plates; $E_1$ from the polarisation charges on the slab's outer faces (together, the macroscopic $E$); $E_2$ from the charges on the wall of an imaginary sphere round the atom, the Lorentz cavity field; $E_3$ from the dipoles inside the sphere, zero for a cubic site.

Lorentz relation (cubic symmetry): $$E_{\text{loc}}=E+\frac{P}{3\varepsilon_0}$$ The atom feels more than the average field, because its polarised neighbours help. In a gas $P$ is tiny and $E_{\text{loc}}\approx E$.

 Dielectric constant and polarisability $$\mathbf D=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r\mathbf E=\varepsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\varepsilon_r=1+\frac{P}{\varepsilon_0E}=1+\chi$$

$\varepsilon_r$, the relative permittivity or dielectric constant, is a macroscopic property, measured with a capacitor. The polarisability $\alpha$, the dipole moment per unit local field ($p=\alpha E_{\text{loc}}$), is an atomic one. With $N$ atoms per unit volume, $P=N\alpha E_{\text{loc}}$ (in general $\sum_jN_jp_j$). Combining with the Lorentz field links the two:

Clausius–Mossotti as a graph. Without the local-field correction, $\varepsilon_r$ would grow in a straight line, $1+N\alpha/\varepsilon_0$. With it, each polarised atom boosts its neighbours' field, the growth runs away, and $\varepsilon_r$ diverges as $N\alpha/3\varepsilon_0\to1$. That runaway is the "polarisation catastrophe" behind ferroelectricity.

$$\frac{\varepsilon_r-1}{\varepsilon_r+2}=\frac{N\alpha}{3\varepsilon_0}=\frac{1}{3\varepsilon_0}\sum_jN_j\alpha_j$$ It connects a measured $\varepsilon_r$ to atomic polarisabilities. Exact for cubic crystals and gases; approximate for polar liquids like water.

Do not use $\varepsilon_r=1+N\alpha/\varepsilon_0$ for a solid: that ignores the local field and is only right for dilute gases (where it agrees with C–M anyway). And $\varepsilon_r$ is dimensionless, but $\alpha$ is in F $\mathrm{m}^{2}$ (C $\mathrm{m}^{2}$ $\mathrm{V}^{-1}$).

 A dielectric has $\varepsilon_r=4.0$ and $N=3\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$. Find $\alpha$.

$\dfrac{\varepsilon_r-1}{\varepsilon_r+2}=\dfrac36=0.5=\dfrac{N\alpha}{3\varepsilon_0}$, so $\alpha=\dfrac{3\times8.85\times10^{-12}\times0.5}{3\times10^{28}}=4.4\times10^{-40}\ \mathrm{F\,m^2}$. Check: $4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3$ with this $\alpha$ gives $R\approx1.6$ Å, a sensible atomic size.

 Argon gas at NTP has $N=2.7\times10^{25}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$ and $\varepsilon_r=1.00055$. Find $\alpha$. Does the local field matter here?

For a gas $\varepsilon_r-1=N\alpha/\varepsilon_0$, so $\alpha=\dfrac{8.85\times10^{-12}\times5.5\times10^{-4}}{2.7\times10^{25}}=1.8\times10^{-40}\ \mathrm{F\,m^2}$ ($\alpha/4\pi\varepsilon_0=1.6$ $\mathrm{Å}^{3}$). Clausius–Mossotti gives the same to about 0.02%: with $P$ so small, $E_{\text{loc}}\approx E$.
