Problems 1–5: the solved examples. Problems 6–14: the end-of-chapter problems, worked through, with the printed answers checked.

Find $\chi_{\text{dia}}$ of atomic hydrogen gas at STP, ground state $\psi=(\pi a_0^3)^{-1/2}e^{-r/a_0}$, $a_0=0.46$ Å.

$\chi=-\dfrac{\mu_0NZe^2}{6m}\langle r^2\rangle$ with $\langle r^2\rangle=\int\psi^*r^2\psi\,d\tau$.

$\langle r^2\rangle=\dfrac{4\pi}{\pi a_0^3}\int_0^\infty r^4e^{-2r/a_0}dr$; with $t=2r/a_0$ and $\int_0^\infty t^4e^{-t}dt=24$: $\langle r^2\rangle=3a_0^2$.

So $\chi=-\dfrac{\mu_0NZe^2a_0^2}{2m}$. $N=6.02\times10^{23}/2.24\times10^{-2}=2.69\times10^{25}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, $Z=1$:

$\chi=-\dfrac{2.69\times10^{25}\times4\pi\times10^{-7}\times(1.6\times10^{-19})^2\times(0.46\times10^{-10})^2}{2\times9.1\times10^{-31}}\approx-1.0\times10^{-9}$ (SI).

Helium has $Z=2$ and $\langle r^2\rangle\approx0.36$ $\mathrm{Å}^{2}$. Estimate $\chi$ for the solid with $N\approx2\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$.

A paramagnetic gas with $L=0$, $S=\tfrac12$ has $N=10^{28}$ atoms per $\mathrm{m}^{3}$. Find the populations of the levels and the magnetisation at 300 K and 5 K in $B=2.5$ T.

$J=\tfrac12$, $g=2$, $m_J=\pm\tfrac12$: energies $\pm\mu_BB$. Populations $\propto e^{\mp\mu_BB/kT}$, normalised to $N$.

$N_{\pm}=\dfrac{N}{2}\dfrac{e^{\pm\mu_BB/kT}}{\cosh(\mu_BB/kT)}$, $M=\mu_B(N_+-N_-)=N\mu_B\tanh\dfrac{\mu_BB}{kT}$.

$\mu_BB/k=9.27\times10^{-24}\times2.5/1.38\times10^{-23}=1.68$ K.

300 K: $N_\pm=4.97,\ 5.03\times10^{27}$ (the larger one aligned), $M=9.27\times10^{4}\tanh(0.0056)=519\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$.

5 K: $3.38$ and $6.62\times10^{27}$, $M=3.0\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$.

At what temperature would 90% of the moments be aligned in 2.5 T? ($\tanh x=0.8$, $x=1.10$, $T\approx1.5$ K.)

$\mathrm{Dy^{3+}}$ has outer configuration $4f^9\,5s^2p^6$. Find the susceptibility of a salt containing one kg-mole of $\mathrm{Dy^{3+}}$ at 300 K.

Hund's rules for $J$, $L$, $S$; Landé $g$; $\chi=\dfrac{\mu_0Ng^2J(J+1)\mu_B^2}{3kT}$.

9 electrons in 7 f orbitals: 5 unpaired, $S=\tfrac52$; $L=3+2+1+0-1=5$; more than half full, so $J=L+S=\tfrac{15}{2}$.

$g=1+\dfrac{J(J+1)+S(S+1)-L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)}=1.33$.

$N=6.023\times10^{26}$: $\chi=\dfrac{4\pi\times10^{-7}\times6.023\times10^{26}\times(9.27\times10^{-24})^2\times1.33^2\times\frac{15}{2}\cdot\frac{17}{2}}{3\times1.38\times10^{-23}\times300}\approx5.9\times10^{-4}$ (per kg-mole, SI).

Find $p_{\text{eff}}=g\sqrt{J(J+1)}$ for $\mathrm{Dy^{3+}}$. (About 10.6.)

A ferromagnet with $J=\tfrac32$, $g=2$ has $T_c=125$ K. Find the exchange flux density near 0 K, and the ratio of $M$ at 300 K in an external field of 1 mT to the spontaneous $M$ at 0 K.

$B_E=\lambda M_s(0)=\lambda NgJ\mu_B$ with $T_c=\lambda Ng^2\mu_B^2J(J+1)/3k$. Above $T_c$, $M=\dfrac{Ng^2\mu_B^2J(J+1)B}{3k(T-T_c)}$.

$B_E=\dfrac{3kT_c}{g\mu_B(J+1)}=\dfrac{3\times1.38\times10^{-23}\times125}{2\times9.27\times10^{-24}\times2.5}=111.6$ T.

$\dfrac{M}{M_s(0)}=\dfrac{g\mu_BB(J+1)}{3k(T-T_c)}=\dfrac{2\times9.27\times10^{-24}\times10^{-3}\times2.5}{3\times1.38\times10^{-23}\times175}=6.4\times10^{-6}$.

Repeat for $T_c=1043$ K (iron, take $J=1$, $g=2$). How large is $B_E$?

NaCl has refractive index 1.5 and static dielectric constant 5.6. Find the percentage of ionic polarisability.

Clausius–Mossotti statically ($\alpha_e+\alpha_i$) and at optical frequency ($\alpha_e$ only, $\varepsilon_r=n^2$).

$\dfrac{\alpha_e}{\alpha_e+\alpha_i}=\dfrac{(n^2-1)/(n^2+2)}{(\varepsilon_r-1)/(\varepsilon_r+2)}=\dfrac{1.25/4.25}{4.6/7.6}=0.486$.

Ionic share $=(1-0.486)\times100=51.4\%$.

KCl: $n=1.49$, $\varepsilon_r=4.84$. Find the ionic share.

Find the diamagnetic susceptibility of copper, assuming each atom contributes one electron and the mean square distance of that electron from the nucleus equals the square of the radius of $\mathrm{Cu^{+}}$ (0.96 Å). Copper is fcc with $a=3.61$ Å.

$\chi=-\dfrac{\mu_0NZe^2}{6m}\langle r^2\rangle$ with $Z=1$, $\langle r^2\rangle=(0.96\ \text{Å})^2$, and $N$ from the fcc cell (4 atoms).

$N=\dfrac{4}{(3.61\times10^{-10})^3}=8.50\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$.

$\chi=-\dfrac{4\pi\times10^{-7}\times8.50\times10^{28}\times(1.6\times10^{-19})^2\times(0.96\times10^{-10})^2}{6\times9.1\times10^{-31}}=-4.62\times10^{-6}$.

Silver is fcc with $a=4.09$ Å and $\mathrm{Ag^{+}}$ radius 1.26 Å. Same assumptions: find $\chi$. (About $-5.5\times10^{-6}$.)

$\mathrm{Cr^{2+}}$ has configuration $3d^4\,4s^0$. Find the susceptibility of a salt containing one kg-mole of $\mathrm{Cr^{2+}}$ ions at 300 K. (Hint: the crystal field quenches the orbital angular momentum.)

Quenched: $L$ drops out, so $J=S$ and $g=2$. Four 3d electrons, all parallel by Hund's first rule: $S=2$. $\chi=\dfrac{\mu_0Ng^2J(J+1)\mu_B^2}{3kT}$.

$p_{\text{eff}}^2=g^2J(J+1)=4\times2\times3=24$.

$\chi=\dfrac{4\pi\times10^{-7}\times6.02\times10^{26}\times24\times(9.27\times10^{-24})^2}{3\times1.38\times10^{-23}\times300}=1.25\times10^{-4}$ (per kg-mole).

$\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$ ($3d^5$, $S=\tfrac52$) under the same conditions. ($p_{\text{eff}}^2=35$, $\chi=1.8\times10^{-4}$.)

$\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ has nine electrons in the 3d shell. What field must be applied to a salt containing $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ at 1 K so that 90% of the ions are in the ground state?

Hund's rules for $3d^9$ (one hole): $S=\tfrac12$, $L=2$, more than half full so $J=L+S=\tfrac52$, $g=1+\dfrac{J(J+1)+S(S+1)-L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)}=1.2$. The six levels $m_J=-\tfrac52\dots\tfrac52$ are spaced by $g\mu_BB$; neighbouring populations differ by $q=e^{-g\mu_BB/kT}$.

Ground-state fraction $=\dfrac{1}{1+q+q^2+\dots+q^5}=\dfrac{1-q}{1-q^6}=0.9$, so $q\approx0.1$ ($q^6$ is negligible).

$\dfrac{g\mu_BB}{kT}=\ln10=2.30\Rightarrow B=\dfrac{2.30\times1.38\times10^{-23}\times1}{1.2\times9.27\times10^{-24}}=2.9\ \mathrm T\approx3$ T.

The standard answer, 3 T, treats the full $J=\tfrac52$. If you quench the orbit instead ($J=S=\tfrac12$, $g=2$, two levels $2\mu_BB$ apart, ratio 9:1), you get $B=kT\ln9/2\mu_B=1.6$ T. State which model you use.

The paramagnetism of copper sulphate comes mainly from $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ ions with $S=\tfrac12$. Show that $M=N\mu_B\tanh\dfrac{\mu_BB}{kT}$.

Quenched orbit, $J=S=\tfrac12$, $g=2$: two levels, moment $+\mu_B$ along $B$ (energy $-\mu_BB$) or $-\mu_B$ (energy $+\mu_BB$).

Boltzmann: $N_\uparrow=\dfrac{Ne^{x}}{e^{x}+e^{-x}}$, $N_\downarrow=\dfrac{Ne^{-x}}{e^{x}+e^{-x}}$ with $x=\dfrac{\mu_BB}{kT}$.

$M=\mu_B(N_\uparrow-N_\downarrow)=N\mu_B\dfrac{e^{x}-e^{-x}}{e^{x}+e^{-x}}=N\mu_B\tanh\dfrac{\mu_BB}{kT}$.

Small $x$: $\tanh x\approx x$, so $M=\dfrac{N\mu_B^2B}{kT}$ and $\chi=\dfrac{\mu_0N\mu_B^2}{kT}$: a Curie law, the same as $\dfrac{\mu_0Np_{\text{eff}}^2\mu_B^2}{3kT}$ with $p_{\text{eff}}^2=3$.

Large $x$: what does $M$ tend to, and what does that mean physically? ($N\mu_B$: every moment aligned, saturation.)

Iron is ferromagnetic with $T_c=1043$ K and an effective moment of $2.2\,\mu_B$ per atom. Compute the internal field.

Classical Weiss: $T_c=\dfrac{\lambda C}{\mu_0}$ with $C=\dfrac{\mu_0N\mu^2}{3k}$, so $\lambda=\dfrac{3kT_c}{N\mu^2}$, and $B_E=\lambda M_s=\lambda N\mu=\dfrac{3kT_c}{\mu}$.

$B_E=\dfrac{3\times1.38\times10^{-23}\times1043}{2.2\times9.27\times10^{-24}}=2.1\times10^{3}$ T.

A magnetic dipole field between neighbours is about 0.1 T: the Weiss field is $10^4$ times larger, so it cannot be magnetic in origin. It is exchange.

Nickel: $T_c=631$ K, $0.6\,\mu_B$ per atom. Find $B_E$. (About 4700 T.)

EuO is ferromagnetic with $T_c=70$ K; $\mathrm{Eu^{2+}}$ has $J=\tfrac72$, $g=2$. Find the internal flux density, and the ratio of the magnetisation at 300 K in 0.05 T to that at 0 K.

Quantum Weiss: $B_E=\dfrac{3kT_c}{g(J+1)\mu_B}$. Above $T_c$: $M=\dfrac{Ng^2J(J+1)\mu_B^2B}{3k(T-T_c)}$; at 0 K, $M_s(0)=NgJ\mu_B$.

$B_E=\dfrac{3\times1.38\times10^{-23}\times70}{2\times4.5\times9.27\times10^{-24}}=34.7$ T.

$\dfrac{M}{M_s(0)}=\dfrac{g(J+1)\mu_BB}{3k(T-T_c)}=\dfrac{2\times4.5\times9.27\times10^{-24}\times0.05}{3\times1.38\times10^{-23}\times230}=4.38\times10^{-4}$.

At what temperature above $T_c$ would the same field give a ratio of $10^{-3}$? ($T-T_c=101$ K, $T\approx171$ K.)

Nickel (fcc, $a=3.52$ Å) has $T_c=358$ °C. $\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}$ has 8 electrons in the 3d shell. With the orbital moment quenched, find the saturation magnetisation, the Weiss constant, the internal field and the Curie constant.

$3d^8$: two holes, $S=1$; quenched, $J=1$, $g=2$. $N$ from the fcc cell. $M_s=NgJ\mu_B$, $C=\dfrac{\mu_0Ng^2J(J+1)\mu_B^2}{3k}$, $\lambda=\dfrac{\mu_0T_c}{C}$, $B_E=\lambda M_s$.

$N=\dfrac{4}{(3.52\times10^{-10})^3}=9.17\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$; $T_c=631$ K.

$M_s=9.17\times10^{28}\times2\times1\times9.27\times10^{-24}=1.70\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$.

$C=\dfrac{4\pi\times10^{-7}\times9.17\times10^{28}\times4\times2\times(9.27\times10^{-24})^2}{3\times1.38\times10^{-23}}=1.92$ K.

$\lambda=\dfrac{4\pi\times10^{-7}\times631}{1.92}=4.14\times10^{-4}$ (T m $\mathrm{A^{-1}}$).

$B_E=\lambda M_s=4.14\times10^{-4}\times1.70\times10^{6}=704$ T.

Measured, nickel saturates at $0.6\,\mu_B$ per atom, not $2\,\mu_B$. What does that tell you about the "$\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}$ ion" model? (Ferromagnetism in Ni is from itinerant d-band electrons, not localised ions.)

The optical refractive index and the dielectric constant of water are 1.33 and 8.1. Find the percentage of ionic polarisability.

As in the NaCl example: the optical value ($\varepsilon_r=n^2$) contains only $\alpha_e$; the static value contains everything else too. Here "ionic" means everything that is not electronic.

Optical: $\dfrac{n^2-1}{n^2+2}=\dfrac{0.769}{3.769}=0.204$. Static: $\dfrac{8.1-1}{8.1+2}=\dfrac{7.1}{10.1}=0.703$.

Electronic share $=0.204/0.703=0.29$, so the rest is $71\%$.

Real water has a static $\varepsilon_r\approx80$ (mostly dipolar). With that value, what share is not electronic? ($(80-1)/(82)=0.963$; $1-0.204/0.963=79\%$.)

Below its Curie point $\mathrm{BaTiO_{3}}$ turns tetragonal with $c=4.03$ Å and $a=3.98$ Å. Relative to Ba, the $\mathrm{Ti^{4+}}$ ion moves 0.06 Å one way, the $\mathrm{O^{2-}}$ ions on the side faces 0.06 Å and those on the top and bottom faces 0.08 Å the other way. Find the polarisation.

$P$ = dipole moment per unit volume = $\sum q_i\delta_i$ over one cell ÷ cell volume. Count shared ions: per cell 1 Ti (body centre), 2 O on side faces (4 faces, each shared by 2 cells), 1 O on top/bottom (2 faces shared by 2).

Ti: $(+4e)(+0.06)=+0.24\,e$Å. Side O: $2\times(-2e)(-0.06)=+0.24\,e$Å. Top/bottom O: $1\times(-2e)(-0.08)=+0.16\,e$Å. Total $p=0.64\,e$Å $=0.64\times1.6\times10^{-19}\times10^{-10}=1.02\times10^{-29}$ C m.

Volume $a^2c=(3.98\times10^{-10})^2\times4.03\times10^{-10}=6.38\times10^{-29}\ \mathrm{m^3}$.

$P=\dfrac{1.02\times10^{-29}}{6.38\times10^{-29}}=0.16\ \mathrm{C\,m^{-2}}$.

Why do both oxygen displacements add to the Ti one, although they are in the opposite direction? (Opposite charge, opposite shift: $q\delta$ has the same sign.)
