Diamagnetism: a field speeds up or slows down each electron orbit by the Larmor frequency $eB/2m$; the extra current opposes $B$. Paramagnetism: permanent moments turn towards $B$ against thermal disorder; the Boltzmann average gives the Langevin function and the Curie law $\chi=C/T$. Diamagnetism

When the flux through an orbit changes, an induced current opposes the change (Lenz's law). Switch on a field and every electron orbit is gently sped up or slowed down, in whichever sense makes a moment against the field. The magnetic field of that induced current is opposite to the applied field: a small, negative $\chi$. It exists in all materials, because every atom has orbiting electrons.

The Larmor picture. The orbit as a whole precesses about $\mathbf B$ at the Larmor frequency $\omega_L=eB/2m$. Each electron then carries an extra current $-Ze\,\omega_L/2\pi$, and its loop area is set by the mean square distance from the field axis, $\langle\rho^2\rangle=\tfrac23\langle r^2\rangle$ for a spherical atom. That is where the $\langle r^2\rangle$ and the 6 in the result come from:

$$\chi_{\text{dia}}=-\frac{\mu_0NZe^2}{6m}\langle r^2\rangle$$ Negative, independent of temperature, and larger for big atoms with many electrons (it grows with $Z\langle r^2\rangle$). Typical size $-10^{-5}$ to $-10^{-6}$.

Quantum theory. With a field the Hamiltonian gains a term $\dfrac{e^2B^2}{8m}(x^2+y^2)$ (plus one in $L_z$). This energy is second order in $B$; equating it to $-\tfrac12\chi B^2/\mu_0$ gives $\chi_{\text{dia}}=-\dfrac{\mu_0NZe^2}{6m}\langle r^2\rangle$, identical to the classical result, with $\langle r^2\rangle$ now taken from the wavefunction. Agreement is good for light elements and rare gases (He about $-1.9\times10^{-6}$).

Paramagnetism

Now give every atom a permanent moment $\mu$. Without a field they point at random and cancel. A field tries to line them up (energy $-\mu B\cos\theta$), heat tries to scramble them ($kT$). The outcome of that contest is the Boltzmann average of $\cos\theta$. Paramagnetism is seen in:

  • metals (conduction electrons);
  • atoms and molecules with an odd number of electrons (free Na atoms, NO gas);
  • a few with an even number ($\mathrm{O_{2}}$);
  • atoms and ions with a partly filled inner shell: rare earths, actinides, transition-metal ions such as $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$.
The Langevin function $L(a)=\coth a-1/a$ (classical moments, any direction allowed) and $\tanh a$ (spin $\tfrac12$, only two directions). Both start as straight lines and level off at full alignment. Ordinary fields at room temperature give $a\approx0.002$, far down on the straight start: that straight start is the Curie law.

Curie law $\chi=C/T$, $C=\mu_0N\mu^2/3k$, for $\mu B\ll kT$. Saturation $M_s=N\mu$ when $\mu B\gg kT$ (very low temperature, very strong field).

 The quantum theory

Quantum mechanics allows only $2J+1$ orientations of the moment, with energies $m_Jg\mu_BB$, $m_J=-J,\dots,J$. Averaging over those few levels instead of over all angles replaces $L(a)$ by the Brillouin function $B_J(x)$, which tends to $L(a)$ as $J\to\infty$ and to $\tanh x$ for $J=\tfrac12$. In small fields the result is again the Curie law, with the classical $\mu^2$ replaced:

$p_{\text{eff}}=g\sqrt{J(J+1)}$ is the effective Bohr magneton number, the number the experiments measure. $g$ is the Landé factor, $g=1+\dfrac{J(J+1)+S(S+1)-L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)}$.

Hund's rules (ground state of an incomplete shell): (i) spins add to the largest $S$ allowed by Pauli; (ii) then the largest $L$; (iii) $J=|L-S|$ if the shell is less than half full, $J=L+S$ if more than half full; half full gives $L=0$, $J=S$.

Quenching. In iron-group salts the crystal field of the neighbouring ions locks the orbits in place, so $L$ contributes almost nothing: use $J=S$, $g=2$. Rare-earth 4f shells lie deep inside the ion, are shielded, and keep the full $J$. (Problems on $\mathrm{Cr^{2+}}$, $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}$ all use quenching.)

The quantum result uses $\mu^2=p_{\text{eff}}^2\mu_B^2=g^2J(J+1)\mu_B^2$, not $g^2J^2\mu_B^2$. The difference is the "$+1$".

 Why does the paramagnetic susceptibility fall as $1/T$, while the diamagnetic one does not depend on $T$ at all?

Paramagnetism is a contest: the field aligns permanent moments, thermal agitation randomises them, and the average alignment is $\mu B/3kT$. Diamagnetism is not a contest: the induced moment comes from the change in every orbit, whatever the temperature, so $T$ never enters.

 Find $p_{\text{eff}}$ for $\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}$ ($3d^{5}$).

Half-filled shell: five parallel spins, $S=\tfrac52$, $L=0$, $J=\tfrac52$, $g=2$. $p_{\text{eff}}=2\sqrt{\tfrac52\cdot\tfrac72}=2\sqrt{8.75}=5.92$, close to the measured 5.9.
