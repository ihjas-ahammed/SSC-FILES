$f$ is convex when its graph lies below every chord. For differentiable $f$ that is the same as the tangent lying below the graph, and for twice differentiable $f$ the same as $\nabla^2f\succeq0$. Convex functions are exactly the functions with convex epigraphs, they satisfy Jensen's inequality, and their sublevel sets are convex. Definition and extended values (§3.1.1–3.1.2)

$f:\mathbf R^n\to\mathbf R$ is convex if $\dom f$ is convex and $$f(\theta\mathbf x+(1-\theta)\mathbf y)\le\theta f(\mathbf x)+(1-\theta)f(\mathbf y)\quad\text{for all }\mathbf x,\mathbf y\in\dom f,\ 0\le\theta\le1.$$ Strictly convex: strict inequality for $\mathbf x\ne\mathbf y$, $0\lt\theta\lt1$. Concave: $-f$ convex. Affine functions are both convex and concave (and only they).

Restriction to a line. $f$ is convex iff $g(t)=f(\mathbf x+t\mathbf v)$ is convex on $\{t\mid\mathbf x+t\mathbf v\in\dom f\}$ for every $\mathbf x\in\dom f$ and $\mathbf v$. Convex functions are continuous on the relative interior of their domain.

Extended-value extension $\tilde f(\mathbf x)=f(\mathbf x)$ on $\dom f$, $+\infty$ outside. Then the inequality makes sense for all $\mathbf x,\mathbf y$ (with $\infty$ arithmetic) and encodes $\dom f$ convex. Example 3.1: the indicator $\tilde I_C(\mathbf x)=0$ on $C$, $\infty$ off $C$, is convex iff $C$ is; minimising $f+\tilde I_C$ is minimising $f$ over $C$.

First-order condition (§3.1.3)

Differentiable $f$ (open convex domain) is convex iff $$f(\mathbf y)\ge f(\mathbf x)+\nabla f(\mathbf x)^T(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)\quad\text{for all }\mathbf x,\mathbf y\in\dom f.$$ The first-order Taylor approximation is a global under-estimator. Consequence: $\nabla f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf 0$ ⇒ $\mathbf x$ is a global minimiser. Strict convexity ⇔ strict inequality for $\mathbf x\ne\mathbf y$.

 Chord, tangent, epigraph, sublevel set — four faces of convexity. Second-order condition (§3.1.4)

Twice differentiable $f$ with open convex domain is convex iff $\nabla^2f(\mathbf x)\succeq0$ for all $\mathbf x\in\dom f$. $\nabla^2f\succ0$ everywhere ⇒ strictly convex (not conversely: $x^4$ is strictly convex with $f''(0)=0$). On $\mathbf R$: $f''\ge0$, the derivative is nondecreasing.

Example 3.2. $f=\tfrac12\mathbf x^TP\mathbf x+\mathbf q^T\mathbf x+r$ ($P\in\mathbf S^n$): $\nabla^2f=P$, so $f$ is convex iff $P\succeq0$, concave iff $P\preceq0$.

The domain must be convex. $f=1/x^2$ on $\{x\ne0\}$ has $f''\gt0$ but is not convex.

 The list of examples (§3.1.5)

On $\mathbf R$. $e^{ax}$ convex. $x^a$ on $\mathbf R_{++}$: convex for $a\ge1$ or $a\le0$, concave for $0\le a\le1$. $|x|^p$ convex for $p\ge1$. $\log x$ concave on $\mathbf R_{++}$. $x\log x$ convex on $\mathbf R_{++}$ (negative entropy, $f''=1/x$).

On $\mathbf R^n$. Every norm (triangle inequality + homogeneity: $f(\theta\mathbf x+(1-\theta)\mathbf y)\le\theta f(\mathbf x)+(1-\theta)f(\mathbf y)$). $\max\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$ convex. Quadratic-over-linear $x^2/y$ ($y\gt0$) convex. Log-sum-exp $\log\sum e^{x_k}$ convex. Geometric mean $(\prod x_k)^{1/n}$ concave on $\mathbf R^n_{++}$. $\log\det X$ concave on $\mathbf S^n_{++}$.

$\log\det X$ is concave (restrict to a line). $g(t)=\log\det(Z+tV)$ with $Z\succ0$: $g(t)=\log\det Z+\sum_i\log(1+t\lambda_i)$, $\lambda_i$ the eigenvalues of $Z^{-1/2}VZ^{-1/2}$. So $g''(t)=-\sum_i\lambda_i^2/(1+t\lambda_i)^2\le0$.

Geometric mean is concave. $\nabla^2f=-\dfrac{f(\mathbf x)}{n^2}\left(n\diag(1/x_1^2,\dots,1/x_n^2)-\mathbf q\mathbf q^T\right)$, $q_i=1/x_i$; $\mathbf v^T\nabla^2f\mathbf v=-\dfrac{f}{n^2}\left(n\sum v_i^2/x_i^2-(\sum v_i/x_i)^2\right)\le0$ by Cauchy–Schwarz.

Sublevel sets and epigraph (§3.1.6–3.1.7)

The $\alpha$-sublevel set $C_\alpha=\{\mathbf x\in\dom f\mid f(\mathbf x)\le\alpha\}$ of a convex function is convex (if $f(\mathbf x),f(\mathbf y)\le\alpha$ then $f(\theta\mathbf x+(1-\theta)\mathbf y)\le\alpha$). Not conversely: $-e^x$ is not convex, but its sublevel sets are intervals.
The epigraph $\epi f=\{(\mathbf x,t)\mid\mathbf x\in\dom f,\ f(\mathbf x)\le t\}$. $f$ is convex iff $\epi f$ is a convex set.

Example 3.3. Geometric mean ≥ $\alpha$ times arithmetic mean: $\{\mathbf x\in\mathbf R^n_+\mid(\prod x_i)^{1/n}\ge\alpha\sum x_i/n\}$ is convex for $0\le\alpha\le1$ (a superlevel set of the concave $G-\alpha A$).

Example 3.4 (matrix fractional function). $f(\mathbf x,Y)=\mathbf x^TY^{-1}\mathbf x$ on $\mathbf R^n\times\mathbf S^n_{++}$ is convex: $\epi f=\left\{(\mathbf x,Y,t)\,\Big|\,\begin{pmatrix}Y&\mathbf x\\\mathbf x^T&t\end{pmatrix}\succeq0,\ Y\succ0\right\}$ by the Schur complement, an LMI in $(\mathbf x,Y,t)$, hence convex.

The first-order condition in epigraph language: $(\nabla f(\mathbf x),-1)$ defines a supporting hyperplane to $\epi f$ at $(\mathbf x,f(\mathbf x))$.

Jensen's inequality and its consequences (§3.1.8–3.1.9)

Jensen: $f(\theta_1\mathbf x_1+\dots+\theta_k\mathbf x_k)\le\theta_1f(\mathbf x_1)+\dots+\theta_kf(\mathbf x_k)$ for $\theta_i\ge0$, $\sum\theta_i=1$; in general $f(\mathbf E\,\mathbf x)\le\mathbf E\,f(\mathbf x)$.

Arithmetic–geometric mean from concavity of $\log$: $\log\frac{a+b}2\ge\frac{\log a+\log b}2$, so $\sqrt{ab}\le\frac{a+b}2$; with weights, $a^\theta b^{1-\theta}\le\theta a+(1-\theta)b$.

Hölder's inequality $\sum x_iy_i\le\left(\sum|x_i|^p\right)^{1/p}\left(\sum|y_i|^q\right)^{1/q}$ ($1/p+1/q=1$, $p\gt1$): apply the weighted AM–GM with $a=|x_i|^p/\sum|x_j|^p$, $b=|y_i|^q/\sum|y_j|^q$, $\theta=1/p$, and sum over $i$.

Prove that $f(x)=x^2$ is convex from the definition.

$\theta x^2+(1-\theta)y^2-(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)^2=\theta(1-\theta)(x-y)^2\ge0$.

 Use the second-order condition to show $f(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2+x_1x_2+x_2^2$ is convex.

$\nabla^2f=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, leading minors $2$ and $3$: positive definite, so (strictly) convex.

 Show every norm is convex.

$\|\theta\mathbf x+(1-\theta)\mathbf y\|\le\|\theta\mathbf x\|+\|(1-\theta)\mathbf y\|=\theta\|\mathbf x\|+(1-\theta)\|\mathbf y\|$ (triangle inequality, then homogeneity).
