To prove a set is convex, rarely use the definition: build it from known convex sets by operations that keep convexity. Intersections (even infinite), affine images and pre-images, perspective and linear-fractional images and pre-images. Intersection (§2.3.1)

If every $S_\alpha$ ($\alpha\in\mathcal A$) is convex, so is $\bigcap_{\alpha}S_\alpha$. Conversely every closed convex set is the intersection of (possibly infinitely many) halfspaces: those containing it.

Example 2.7. $\mathbf S^n_+=\bigcap_{\mathbf z\ne\mathbf 0}\{X\in\mathbf S^n\mid\mathbf z^TX\mathbf z\ge0\}$: for each $\mathbf z$, $\mathbf z^TX\mathbf z$ is linear in $X$, so each set is a halfspace in $\mathbf S^n$.

Example 2.8. $S=\{\mathbf x\in\mathbf R^m\mid|p(t)|\le1\text{ for }|t|\le\pi/3\}$ with $p(t)=\sum_{k=1}^mx_k\cos kt$. $S=\bigcap_{|t|\le\pi/3}S_t$, $S_t=\{\mathbf x\mid-1\le(\cos t,\dots,\cos mt)^T\mathbf x\le1\}$, each a slab (two halfspaces). So $S$ is convex.

Affine functions (§2.3.2)

$f(\mathbf x)=A\mathbf x+\mathbf b$. If $S$ is convex, the image $f(S)=\{f(\mathbf x)\mid\mathbf x\in S\}$ and the inverse image $f^{-1}(C)=\{\mathbf x\mid f(\mathbf x)\in C\}$ (of a convex $C$) are convex.

Proof of the image. $\mathbf y_i=A\mathbf x_i+\mathbf b$ with $\mathbf x_i\in S$; then $\theta\mathbf y_1+(1-\theta)\mathbf y_2=A(\theta\mathbf x_1+(1-\theta)\mathbf x_2)+\mathbf b\in f(S)$. Pre-image: if $f(\mathbf x_1),f(\mathbf x_2)\in C$ then $f(\theta\mathbf x_1+(1-\theta)\mathbf x_2)=\theta f(\mathbf x_1)+(1-\theta)f(\mathbf x_2)\in C$.

Special cases: scaling $\alpha S$ and translation $S+\mathbf a$; projection $\{\mathbf x_1\mid(\mathbf x_1,\mathbf x_2)\in S\}$; the sum $S_1+S_2=\{\mathbf x+\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x\in S_1,\mathbf y\in S_2\}$ (image of $S_1\times S_2$ under $(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\mapsto\mathbf x+\mathbf y$); the Cartesian product $S_1\times S_2$; the partial sum.

Example 2.9. Polyhedron $\{\mathbf x\mid A\mathbf x\preceq\mathbf b,\ C\mathbf x=\mathbf d\}$ = inverse image of $\mathbf R^m_+\times\{\mathbf 0\}$ under $\mathbf x\mapsto(\mathbf b-A\mathbf x,\ \mathbf d-C\mathbf x)$.

Example 2.10. The solution set of a linear matrix inequality $A(\mathbf x)=x_1A_1+\dots+x_nA_n\preceq B$ ($A_i,B\in\mathbf S^m$) is convex: inverse image of $\mathbf S^m_+$ under the affine $\mathbf x\mapsto B-A(\mathbf x)$.

Example 2.11. Hyperbolic cone $\{\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x^TP\mathbf x\le(\mathbf c^T\mathbf x)^2,\ \mathbf c^T\mathbf x\ge0\}$ ($P\in\mathbf S^n_+$): inverse image of the second-order cone under $\mathbf x\mapsto(P^{1/2}\mathbf x,\mathbf c^T\mathbf x)$.

Example 2.12. The ellipsoid is the image of the unit ball under $\mathbf u\mapsto P^{1/2}\mathbf u+\mathbf x_c$, and the inverse image of the unit ball under $\mathbf x\mapsto P^{-1/2}(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_c)$.

Perspective and linear-fractional functions (§2.3.3)

Perspective $P:\mathbf R^{n+1}\to\mathbf R^n$, $P(\mathbf z,t)=\mathbf z/t$, $\dom P=\mathbf R^n\times\mathbf R_{++}$ (normalise the last coordinate to $1$ and drop it; a pinhole camera). Images and inverse images of convex sets under $P$ are convex.

Linear-fractional $f(\mathbf x)=\dfrac{A\mathbf x+\mathbf b}{\mathbf c^T\mathbf x+d}$, $\dom f=\{\mathbf c^T\mathbf x+d\gt0\}$: the perspective composed with an affine map. It also preserves convexity of sets.

Why the perspective keeps segments. For $\mathbf x=(\tilde{\mathbf x},x_{n+1})$, $\mathbf y=(\tilde{\mathbf y},y_{n+1})$ with positive last coordinates,

$$P(\theta\mathbf x+(1-\theta)\mathbf y)=\frac{\theta\tilde{\mathbf x}+(1-\theta)\tilde{\mathbf y}}{\theta x_{n+1}+(1-\theta)y_{n+1}}=\mu P(\mathbf x)+(1-\mu)P(\mathbf y),\quad\mu=\frac{\theta x_{n+1}}{\theta x_{n+1}+(1-\theta)y_{n+1}}\in[0,1].$$

As $\theta$ runs over $[0,1]$, $\mu$ runs over $[0,1]$: the segment $[\mathbf x,\mathbf y]$ maps onto the segment $[P(\mathbf x),P(\mathbf y)]$. Hence convex sets go to convex sets. Inverse image: if $(\mathbf x/t),(\mathbf y/s)\in C$ then $(\theta\mathbf x+(1-\theta)\mathbf y)/(\theta t+(1-\theta)s)=\mu(\mathbf x/t)+(1-\mu)(\mathbf y/s)\in C$.

Example 2.13 (conditional probabilities). With $p_{ij}=\mathbf{prob}(u=i,v=j)$, the conditional $f_{ij}=p_{ij}/\sum_kp_{kj}$ is linear-fractional in $p$, so if $C$ is a convex set of joint distributions, the set of resulting conditional distributions is convex.

Prove that the sum $S_1+S_2$ of two convex sets is convex directly.

Take $\mathbf x_1+\mathbf y_1$ and $\mathbf x_2+\mathbf y_2$ ($\mathbf x_i\in S_1$, $\mathbf y_i\in S_2$). $\theta(\mathbf x_1+\mathbf y_1)+(1-\theta)(\mathbf x_2+\mathbf y_2)=[\theta\mathbf x_1+(1-\theta)\mathbf x_2]+[\theta\mathbf y_1+(1-\theta)\mathbf y_2]\in S_1+S_2$.

 Is $\{\mathbf x\mid A(\mathbf x)\preceq B\}$ convex when $A(\mathbf x)=\sum x_iA_i$? Give the one-line reason.

Yes: it is the inverse image of the convex cone $\mathbf S^m_+$ under the affine map $\mathbf x\mapsto B-A(\mathbf x)$.
