Slice the graph of $f:\mathbf R^n\to\mathbf R$ by a vertical plane through $\boldsymbol\xi$ in the direction of a unit vector $\mathbf u$. The slope of the slice at $\boldsymbol\xi$ is the directional derivative $D_{\mathbf u}f(\boldsymbol\xi)$. Partial derivatives are the directional derivatives along the coordinate axes. Scalar and vector fields

A scalar field is a real-valued function of a vector variable, $f:\mathbf R^n\to\mathbf R$ (a height over each point). A vector field (vector function) is $f:\mathbf R^n\to\mathbf R^m$ with $m\ge2$; it has component functions $f^1,\dots,f^m$, each a scalar field: $f(\mathbf x)=(f^1(\mathbf x),\dots,f^m(\mathbf x))$.

For $n=2$ a scalar field is a surface $y=f(x_1,x_2)$. A contour is the curve $f(x_1,x_2)=c$: points of the surface above it are all at the same height.

Directional derivative (§19.1)

The points $\mathbf x=\boldsymbol\xi+t\mathbf u$ ($t\in\mathbf R$, $\|\mathbf u\|=1$) form the line through $\boldsymbol\xi$ in direction $\mathbf u$, and $t$ is the distance along it. Put $F(t)=f(\boldsymbol\xi+t\mathbf u)$: this is the curve cut out of the surface by the vertical plane through that line. Its slope at $t=0$ is

$$D_{\mathbf u}f(\boldsymbol\xi)=F'(0)=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{f(\boldsymbol\xi+t\mathbf u)-f(\boldsymbol\xi)}{t},\qquad\|\mathbf u\|=1.$$ The slope (rate of increase) of $f$ at $\boldsymbol\xi$ in the direction $\mathbf u$. The same formula is used for vector functions and for non-unit $\mathbf u$, but then it is not a slope.

 Partial derivatives (§19.3–19.4)

For $f:\mathbf R^2\to\mathbf R$,

$$f_1(\xi_1,\xi_2)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(\xi_1+h,\xi_2)-f(\xi_1,\xi_2)}{h},\qquad f_2(\xi_1,\xi_2)=\lim_{k\to0}\frac{f(\xi_1,\xi_2+k)-f(\xi_1,\xi_2)}{k}.$$

To compute $f_1$, differentiate with respect to $x_1$ holding $x_2$ constant. With $\mathbf i=(1,0)$, $\mathbf j=(0,1)$: $D_{\mathbf i}f(\boldsymbol\xi)=f_1(\boldsymbol\xi)$ and $D_{\mathbf j}f(\boldsymbol\xi)=f_2(\boldsymbol\xi)$. So $f_1$ is the slope of the slice by the plane $x_2=\xi_2$, the graph $y=f(x_1,\xi_2)$.

Examples 19.4. (i) $f=x_1^2x_2+x_2^3x_1$: $f_1=2x_1x_2+x_2^3$, $f_2=x_1^2+3x_2^2x_1$. (ii) $g=\sin(x_1x_2+x_2^2)$: $g_1=x_2\cos(x_1x_2+x_2^2)$, $g_2=(x_1+2x_2)\cos(x_1x_2+x_2^2)$. (iii) $h=x_1+x_2x_3$: $h_1=1$, $h_2=x_3$, $h_3=x_2$.

Notation, and which variable is held constant (§19.5–19.6)

$f_1$ is also written $f_{x_1}$ or $\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial x_1}$ when $y=f(x_1,x_2)$; $\left(\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial x_1}\right)_{x_2}$ says "holding $x_2$ constant". This matters. Example 19.6: $u=x+y$, $v=x-y$, so $x=\tfrac12(u+v)$, $y=\tfrac12(u-v)$. Then

$$\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right)_y=1,\qquad\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial u}\right)_v=\tfrac12,\qquad\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right)_v=2\ (\text{from }u=2x-v).$$

So $\partial u/\partial x\ne(\partial x/\partial u)^{-1}$ in general: different variables were held constant. With the same variable held constant, $(\partial u/\partial x)_v=2=(\partial x/\partial u)_v^{-1}$.

Find the slope of $f(x_1,x_2)=x_1x_2$ at $(1,2)$ in the direction $(1,1)$.

$\mathbf u=(1,1)/\sqrt2$. $f'(1,2)=(x_2,x_1)=(2,1)$, so $D_{\mathbf u}f=(2+1)/\sqrt2=3/\sqrt2$.

 Explain the geometric meaning of $f_2(\xi_1,\xi_2)$.

Slice the surface $y=f(x_1,x_2)$ by the vertical plane $x_1=\xi_1$. The curve $y=f(\xi_1,x_2)$ has slope $f_2(\xi_1,\xi_2)$ at $x_2=\xi_2$: the slope of the surface in the direction of the $x_2$-axis.
