$\boldsymbol\xi$ is a stationary point of a real-valued $f$ when $f'(\boldsymbol\xi)=\mathbf 0$: all partial derivatives vanish and the tangent hyperplane is horizontal. Every differentiable local extremum is stationary, but stationary points can also be saddles. Least squares is the model example: setting the derivative to zero gives the normal equations. Definition and theorem 19.28

$\boldsymbol\xi$ is stationary if $f'(\boldsymbol\xi)=\mathbf 0$ (the $1\times n$ zero row: $f_1(\boldsymbol\xi)=\dots=f_n(\boldsymbol\xi)=0$). Geometrically the tangent hyperplane $y=f(\boldsymbol\xi)+f'(\boldsymbol\xi)(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi)$ reduces to $y=f(\boldsymbol\xi)$: horizontal.

Theorem 19.28. If $f$ is differentiable at $\boldsymbol\xi\in A$ and has a local maximum or minimum there, then $f'(\boldsymbol\xi)=\mathbf 0$. (Proof: corollary 19.9 gives all partials $0$, and by 19.16 they are the entries of $f'(\boldsymbol\xi)$.)

Stationary points come as local maxima, local minima, saddle points, and occasionally more exotic types (like $x^3$ at $0$ in one variable).

Example 19.29. $f=x_1x_2$: $f'(0,0)=(0,0)$ but $f=x_1^2\gt0$ on $x_1=x_2$ and $f=-x_1^2\lt0$ on $x_1=-x_2$. A saddle; its contours are the hyperbolas $x_1x_2=\pm1$, and the surface looks like a saddle.

Quadratic surfaces as contour maps. Tap the chips: with both eigenvalues positive the stationary point is a minimum, both negative a maximum, mixed signs a saddle (like $x_1x_2$), and a zero eigenvalue gives a flat line. Section 19.37 explains why. Example 19.30: least squares

An inconsistent system $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$ has no solution, so we look for $\mathbf x$ minimising $\|A\mathbf x-\mathbf b\|$.

The best $A\mathbf x$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $\mathbf b$ to the range of $A$.

The geometry suggests a minimiser exists; but a picture is not a proof, and most problems are not this transparent. So we need a way to classify stationary points: the second derivative.

Find the stationary points of $f=x_1^2+x_1x_2+x_2^2-3x_1$.

$f_1=2x_1+x_2-3=0$, $f_2=x_1+2x_2=0$, so $x_1=2$, $x_2=-1$. One stationary point, $(2,-1)$.

 Fit $y=cx$ to the data $(1,1),(2,3)$ by least squares.

$A=(1,2)^T$, $\mathbf b=(1,3)^T$. $A^TA=5$, $A^T\mathbf b=7$, so $c=7/5$.
