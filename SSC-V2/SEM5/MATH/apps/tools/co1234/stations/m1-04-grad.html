For a real-valued $f$ the derivative is a row vector, the gradient $\nabla f(\boldsymbol\xi)$. It is normal to the contour through $\boldsymbol\xi$, points in the direction of steepest increase, and its length is that steepest slope. The one-variable rules (linear, product, chain, inverse) survive for vectors and matrices once they are written so that the sizes match. The gradient and what it means (§19.19)

$\nabla f(\boldsymbol\xi)=f'(\boldsymbol\xi)=(f_1(\boldsymbol\xi),\dots,f_n(\boldsymbol\xi))$. The tangent hyperplane to $y=f(\mathbf x)$ is $y-f(\boldsymbol\xi)=f'(\boldsymbol\xi)(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi)$; setting $y=f(\boldsymbol\xi)$ gives the tangent hyperplane to the contour $f(\mathbf x)=f(\boldsymbol\xi)$:

$$\langle\nabla f(\boldsymbol\xi),\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi\rangle=0,$$

so $\nabla f(\boldsymbol\xi)$ is a normal to the contour. Its length: $D_{\mathbf u}f(\boldsymbol\xi)=\langle\nabla f(\boldsymbol\xi),\mathbf u\rangle$, and by Cauchy–Schwarz $|D_{\mathbf u}f(\boldsymbol\xi)|\le\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol\xi)\|\,\|\mathbf u\|=\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol\xi)\|$, with equality for $\mathbf u=\nabla f(\boldsymbol\xi)/\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol\xi)\|$.

$\nabla f(\boldsymbol\xi)$ points in the direction in which the slope of $f$ at $\boldsymbol\xi$ is greatest, and that greatest slope is $\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol\xi)\|$. It is normal to the contour through $\boldsymbol\xi$.

Example 19.20. For $f=x_1^2x_2+x_2^3x_1$, $\nabla f(2,1)=(5,10)$: normal to the contour $x_1^2x_2+x_2^3x_1=6$ at $(2,1)$, direction of fastest increase, rate $\sqrt{25+100}=5\sqrt5$.

Manipulating derivatives (§19.21)

The six one-variable formulas (I) $\tfrac{d}{dx}(Lx+c)=L$, (II) $\tfrac{d}{dx}(Lx^2)=2Lx$, (III) $\tfrac{d}{dx}(Ly+Mz)=L\tfrac{dy}{dx}+M\tfrac{dz}{dx}$, (IV) product rule, (V) chain rule, (VI) $\tfrac{dy}{dx}=(\tfrac{dx}{dy})^{-1}$ stay true with column vectors and matrices whenever they make sense. (II) and (IV) must be rewritten first:

$$\text{(II)}'\ \ \frac{d}{d\mathbf x}(\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^T(A^T+A),\qquad\text{(IV)}'\ \ \frac{d}{d\mathbf x}(\mathbf y^T\mathbf z)=\mathbf z^T\frac{d\mathbf y}{d\mathbf x}+\mathbf y^T\frac{d\mathbf z}{d\mathbf x}.$$ For symmetric $A$: derivative $2\mathbf x^TA$, gradient (as a column) $2A\mathbf x$.

Binmore prints the symmetric case of (II)′ as $2A\mathbf x$; as a derivative (a row) it is $2\mathbf x^TA$, and $2A\mathbf x$ is its transpose. Either is accepted if you say which you mean. Formula (VI) only makes sense when $d\mathbf y/d\mathbf x$ is square and non-singular.

 Differentiate $f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf c^T\mathbf x+\tfrac12\mathbf x^TQ\mathbf x$ with $Q$ symmetric, and find its stationary point.

$f'(\mathbf x)=\mathbf c^T+\mathbf x^TQ$ (by (I) and (II)′). Stationary: $Q\mathbf x=-\mathbf c$, so $\mathbf x=-Q^{-1}\mathbf c$ if $Q$ is non-singular.

 At $(1,2)$, in which direction does $f=x_1^2+x_2^2$ increase fastest, and how fast?

$\nabla f=(2x_1,2x_2)=(2,4)$: direction $(1,2)/\sqrt5$, rate $\|(2,4)\|=2\sqrt5$.
