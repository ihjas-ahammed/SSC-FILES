Two disjoint convex sets can always be split by a hyperplane. A convex set has a supporting hyperplane at every boundary point. These two facts underlie duality, theorems of alternatives and optimality conditions. The separating hyperplane theorem (§2.5.1)

If $C$ and $D$ are nonempty disjoint convex sets, there exist $\mathbf a\ne\mathbf 0$ and $b$ with $$\mathbf a^T\mathbf x\le b\ \ \forall\mathbf x\in C,\qquad\mathbf a^T\mathbf x\ge b\ \ \forall\mathbf x\in D.$$ The hyperplane $\{\mathbf a^T\mathbf x=b\}$ separates $C$ and $D$. Strict separation ($\lt$ and $\gt$) needs more, e.g. $C$ closed and $D$ a single point, or $C$ closed and $D$ compact.

 The construction used in the proof: closest points, then the perpendicular bisector.

The general case (distance zero, or not attained) is handled differently (exercise 2.22); the statement above holds in full generality. The converse is false: a separating hyperplane can exist for intersecting sets ($C=D=\{\mathbf 0\}$, $\mathbf a^T\mathbf x=0$ separates). But: if $C$ is convex and open and $D$ convex, then $C\cap D=\emptyset$ iff a separating hyperplane exists.

Example 2.19 (affine and convex set). $C$ convex, $D=\{F\mathbf u+\mathbf g\}$ affine, disjoint. Then $\mathbf a^T\mathbf x\le b$ on $C$ and $\mathbf a^TF\mathbf u\ge b-\mathbf a^T\mathbf g$ for all $\mathbf u$. A linear function bounded below on $\mathbf R^m$ is constant, so $\mathbf a^TF=0$: the hyperplane contains a translate of $D$'s direction.

Example 2.20 (point and closed convex set). $C$ closed convex, $\mathbf x_0\notin C$: strictly separable. The closest point $\mathbf x_C$ exists; $\mathbf a=\mathbf x_0-\mathbf x_C$ and the hyperplane through the midpoint does it. Hence: a closed convex set is the intersection of all halfspaces that contain it.

Example 2.21 (alternatives for strict linear inequalities). $A\mathbf x\prec\mathbf b$ is infeasible iff the convex sets $C=\{\mathbf b-A\mathbf x\}$ and $D=\mathbf R^m_{++}$ do not meet; separation gives $\boldsymbol\lambda\ne\mathbf 0$, $\boldsymbol\lambda\succeq0$, $A^T\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf 0$, $\boldsymbol\lambda^T\mathbf b\le0$. Exactly one of these two systems is solvable: $A\mathbf x\prec\mathbf b$, or $\boldsymbol\lambda\ne\mathbf 0$, $\boldsymbol\lambda\succeq0$, $A^T\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf 0$, $\boldsymbol\lambda^T\mathbf b\le0$.

Supporting hyperplanes (§2.5.2)

If $\mathbf x_0\in\mathbf{bd}\,C$ and $\mathbf a\ne\mathbf 0$ satisfies $\mathbf a^T\mathbf x\le\mathbf a^T\mathbf x_0$ for all $\mathbf x\in C$, then $\{\mathbf a^T\mathbf x=\mathbf a^T\mathbf x_0\}$ is a supporting hyperplane to $C$ at $\mathbf x_0$. Supporting hyperplane theorem: a nonempty convex $C$ has a supporting hyperplane at every boundary point.

 $C$ lies entirely in the halfspace $\mathbf a^T\mathbf x\le\mathbf a^T\mathbf x_0$.

Proof. If $\intr C\ne\emptyset$, separate the convex set $\intr C$ from the point $\{\mathbf x_0\}$ (disjoint, since $\mathbf x_0$ is on the boundary): $\mathbf a^T\mathbf x\le\mathbf a^T\mathbf x_0$ on $\intr C$, hence on $\cl C\supseteq C$. If $\intr C=\emptyset$, $C$ lies in an affine set of lower dimension and any hyperplane containing it supports it. Partial converse (exercise 2.27): a closed set with nonempty interior that has a supporting hyperplane at every boundary point is convex.

Give two disjoint closed convex sets that cannot be strictly separated.

$C=\{(x,y)\mid y\le0\}$ and $D=\{(x,y)\mid x\gt0,\ xy\ge1\}$. They are disjoint and closed, and $\mathbf{dist}(C,D)=0$ (the hyperbola approaches the axis), so no hyperplane separates them strictly (exercise 2.23).
