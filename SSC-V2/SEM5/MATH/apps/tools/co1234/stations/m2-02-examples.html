The standard list, with the one-line reason each is convex: hyperplanes and halfspaces (linear), balls and ellipsoids (triangle inequality / affine image of a ball), norm cones, polyhedra (intersections of halfspaces), simplexes, and the cone of positive semidefinite matrices (an intersection of halfspaces in matrix space). Hyperplanes and halfspaces (§2.2.1)

Hyperplane $\{\mathbf x\mid\mathbf a^T\mathbf x=b\}$, $\mathbf a\ne\mathbf 0$: affine. Halfspace $\{\mathbf x\mid\mathbf a^T\mathbf x\le b\}$: convex, not affine. With $\mathbf x_0$ on the hyperplane they are $\mathbf a^T(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0)=0$ and $\mathbf a^T(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0)\le0$: $\mathbf x_0+\mathbf a^\perp$, and the side making an obtuse angle with the outward normal $\mathbf a$.

 Hyperplane through $\mathbf x_0$ with normal $\mathbf a$; the shaded side is $\mathbf a^T\mathbf x\le b$. The interior $\{\mathbf a^T\mathbf x\lt b\}$ is an open halfspace. Euclidean balls and ellipsoids (§2.2.2)

$B(\mathbf x_c,r)=\{\mathbf x\mid\|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_c\|_2\le r\}=\{\mathbf x_c+r\mathbf u\mid\|\mathbf u\|_2\le1\}$. Convex: if $\|\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x_c\|_2,\|\mathbf x_2-\mathbf x_c\|_2\le r$ then

$$\|\theta\mathbf x_1+(1-\theta)\mathbf x_2-\mathbf x_c\|_2\le\theta\|\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x_c\|_2+(1-\theta)\|\mathbf x_2-\mathbf x_c\|_2\le r.$$

Ellipsoid $\mathcal E=\{\mathbf x\mid(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_c)^TP^{-1}(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_c)\le1\}$, $P\succ0$ (symmetric positive definite). Semi-axes $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ along the eigenvectors of $P$; $P=r^2I$ is a ball. Equivalently $\mathcal E=\{\mathbf x_c+A\mathbf u\mid\|\mathbf u\|_2\le1\}$ with $A=P^{1/2}$ (any non-singular $A$ works).

 An ellipsoid with its semi-axes, and the unit balls of three norms. Norm balls and norm cones (§2.2.3)

For any norm, the ball $\{\mathbf x\mid\|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_c\|\le r\}$ is convex (same proof, triangle inequality and homogeneity). The norm cone $C=\{(\mathbf x,t)\mid\|\mathbf x\|\le t\}\subseteq\mathbf R^{n+1}$ is a convex cone.

Example 2.3. The second-order cone (Lorentz, ice-cream cone) is the Euclidean norm cone $\{(\mathbf x,t)\mid\|\mathbf x\|_2\le t\}=\left\{(\mathbf x,t)\,\Big|\,\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf x\\t\end{pmatrix}^T\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf x\\t\end{pmatrix}\le0,\ t\ge0\right\}$.

Polyhedra and simplexes (§2.2.4)

A polyhedron is the solution set of finitely many linear inequalities and equalities: $\mathcal P=\{\mathbf x\mid A\mathbf x\preceq\mathbf b,\ C\mathbf x=\mathbf d\}$ ($\preceq$ componentwise). It is an intersection of halfspaces and hyperplanes, hence convex. Bounded polyhedra are polytopes.

 A polyhedron as an intersection of halfspaces with outward normals $\mathbf a_i$.

Example 2.4. The nonnegative orthant $\mathbf R^n_+=\{\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x\succeq0\}$ is a polyhedron and a cone (a polyhedral cone).

Simplexes. If $\mathbf v_0,\dots,\mathbf v_k$ are affinely independent ($\mathbf v_1-\mathbf v_0,\dots,\mathbf v_k-\mathbf v_0$ linearly independent), the simplex $C=\conv\{\mathbf v_0,\dots,\mathbf v_k\}$ has affine dimension $k$. Example 2.5: a 1-simplex is a segment, a 2-simplex a triangle, a 3-simplex a tetrahedron; the unit simplex $\{\mathbf x\succeq0,\ \mathbf 1^T\mathbf x\le1\}$ ($n$-dimensional) and the probability simplex $\{\mathbf x\succeq0,\ \mathbf 1^T\mathbf x=1\}$ ($(n-1)$-dimensional). A simplex is a polyhedron: write $\mathbf x=\mathbf v_0+B\mathbf y$ with $B=[\mathbf v_1-\mathbf v_0\ \cdots\ \mathbf v_k-\mathbf v_0]$, $\mathbf y\succeq0$, $\mathbf 1^T\mathbf y\le1$; $B$ has full column rank, so a non-singular $A=\begin{pmatrix}A_1\\A_2\end{pmatrix}$ has $AB=\begin{pmatrix}I\\0\end{pmatrix}$, giving $A_2\mathbf x=A_2\mathbf v_0$, $A_1\mathbf x\succeq A_1\mathbf v_0$, $\mathbf 1^TA_1\mathbf x\le1+\mathbf 1^TA_1\mathbf v_0$: linear equalities and inequalities.

Convex hull description. $\conv\{\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_k\}$ is a bounded polyhedron, but turning a vertex description into inequalities (and back) can be hard. A generalised form $\{\theta_1\mathbf v_1+\dots+\theta_k\mathbf v_k\mid\theta_1+\dots+\theta_m=1,\ \theta_i\ge0\}$ ($m\le k$) mixes points and directions.

The positive semidefinite cone (§2.2.5)

$\mathbf S^n$: symmetric $n\times n$ matrices. $\mathbf S^n_+=\{X\in\mathbf S^n\mid X\succeq0\}$ (all $\mathbf z^TX\mathbf z\ge0$) is a convex cone: if $\theta_1,\theta_2\ge0$ and $A,B\in\mathbf S^n_+$, then $\mathbf z^T(\theta_1A+\theta_2B)\mathbf z=\theta_1\mathbf z^TA\mathbf z+\theta_2\mathbf z^TB\mathbf z\ge0$. $\mathbf S^n_{++}$: positive definite.

Example 2.6. $X=\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\y&z\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbf S^2_+$ ⇔ $x\ge0$, $z\ge0$, $xz\ge y^2$. In $(x,y,z)$-space this is a round cone (fig. 2.12).

Show the set of $\mathbf x$ with $\|\mathbf x-\mathbf a\|_2\le\|\mathbf x-\mathbf b\|_2$ is a halfspace.

Square and expand: $-2\mathbf a^T\mathbf x+\|\mathbf a\|^2\le-2\mathbf b^T\mathbf x+\|\mathbf b\|^2$, i.e. $2(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)^T\mathbf x\le\|\mathbf b\|^2-\|\mathbf a\|^2$ (exercise 2.7, Voronoi).

 Why is $\{X\in\mathbf S^2\mid X\succeq0\}$ not a polyhedron, yet still convex?

Its boundary $xz=y^2$ is curved (a round cone), so finitely many halfspaces cannot describe it; it is an intersection of infinitely many halfspaces $\mathbf z^TX\mathbf z\ge0$, one for each $\mathbf z$.
