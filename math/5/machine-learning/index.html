💡 1. What is Machine Learning? (The Paradigm Shift)

In traditional computer science, a human engineer writes explicit Rules (Algorithms) that take Data as input and produce Answers. In Machine Learning, the system takes Data and corresponding Answers (Outputs) to automatically discover the underlying Rules that map inputs to outputs.

Tom Mitchell's Formal Operational Definition (1997):
A computer program is said to learn from experience $\mathcal{E}$ with respect to some class of tasks $\mathcal{T}$ and performance measure $\mathcal{P}$, if its performance at tasks in $\mathcal{T}$, as measured by $\mathcal{P}$, improves with experience $\mathcal{E}$.

💡 2. Model Generalization, Overfitting & The Bias-Variance Trade-Off

The ultimate goal of ML is Generalization: performing well on unseen, future test data, not just memorizing the training samples.

  • Underfitting (High Bias): The model is overly simplistic to capture the true underlying pattern (e.g., fitting a straight line to quadratic data). Errs significantly on both train and test sets.
  • Overfitting (High Variance): The model is overly complex and captures random noise/idiosyncrasies of the training data (e.g., fitting a 15th-degree polynomial to 10 points). Perfect on training data, disastrous on test data.

💡 3. Resampling Strategies (Cross-Validation) & Empirical Learning Curves

$K$-Fold Cross-Validation: The dataset $\mathcal{D}$ is randomly divided into $K$ equal-sized folds. For each iteration $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$, fold $k$ is held out as the validation set, while the remaining $K-1$ folds are used for training. The overall performance is the average: $$\text{CV}_{(K)} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \text{Loss}_k$$

Learning Curves: Plots of training error and validation error as a function of the number of training samples $m$. They provide an empirical diagnostic tool to detect high bias versus high variance.

(2 Marks) Q2.
Distinguish between Supervised, Unsupervised, and Reinforcement Learning with mathematical formulations.
  • Supervised Learning: Given training data consisting of input-output pairs $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \dots, (x_n, y_n)\}$, where $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and target $y_i \in \mathcal{Y}$. The objective is to learn a mapping function $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ such that $f(x) \approx y$ on unseen test points (e.g., Regression when $y \in \mathbb{R}$, Classification when $y \in \{0, 1\}$).
  • Unsupervised Learning: Given only unlabeled inputs $\mathcal{D} = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\}$ with no ground truth labels $y_i$. The objective is to uncover intrinsic geometric patterns, probability density distributions, or low-dimensional manifolds in the data (e.g., $k$-Means Clustering, PCA).
  • Reinforcement Learning: An agent interacts with a dynamic environment modeled as a Markov Decision Process $(S, A, P, R, \gamma)$. The agent chooses action $a_t \in A$ in state $s_t \in S$ to maximize cumulative discounted expected future reward: $R_t = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \gamma^k r_{t+k+1}$.
(2 Marks) Q3.
What is meant by Inductive Bias in machine learning? Why is it mathematically necessary?
Inductive Bias is the set of explicit or implicit assumptions a learning algorithm makes about the target function in order to generalize beyond observed training examples to unseen data.
Mathematical Necessity: Without inductive bias, an algorithm has no rational basis to prefer one consistent hypothesis over another (all unseen data points could take arbitrary labels). By the No Free Lunch Theorem, a learner with zero inductive bias cannot outperform random guessing on unobserved instances. Examples: Occam's razor (preference for simpler trees), Linearity assumption (in Linear Regression).
(2 Marks) Q4.
Distinguish between Parametric and Non-parametric machine learning models with examples.
  • Parametric Models: Summarize data with a fixed, finite number of parameters $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^p$ that does not grow with the training set size $n$. They make strong assumptions about the functional form of $f(x)$. Examples: Linear Regression, Logistic Regression, Linear Discriminant Analysis. Pros: Fast, memory-efficient. Cons: High bias if functional form is misspecified.
  • Non-parametric Models: Do NOT make strong assumptions about the form of $f(x)$. The effective number of parameters grows flexibly with the dataset size $n$. Examples: $k$-Nearest Neighbors ($k$-NN), Decision Trees, Support Vector Machines with RBF kernel. Pros: Highly flexible, low bias. Cons: Computationally expensive, prone to overfitting.
(3 Marks) Q5.
Compare Mean Squared Error (MSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Huber Loss in terms of mathematical robustness to outliers.
Let the prediction residual be $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$:
  • Mean Squared Error (MSE / L2 Loss): $L(e) = e^2$. Differentiable everywhere with gradient $\frac{\partial L}{\partial e} = 2e$. However, because residuals are squared, extreme outliers exert huge leverage, severely pulling the model towards noise.
  • Mean Absolute Error (MAE / L1 Loss): $L(e) = |e|$. Highly robust to outliers because the penalty grows linearly with $|e|$. However, its derivative is discontinuous at $e = 0$, making gradient-based optimization non-smooth.
  • Huber Loss: Combines the best of both worlds: $$L_\delta(e) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2} e^2 & \text{if } |e| \le \delta \\ \delta (|e| - \frac{1}{2}\delta) & \text{if } |e| > \delta \end{cases}$$ It is quadratic (smooth) for small errors $|e| \le \delta$ and linear (robust) for large errors $|e| > \delta$.

(5 Marks) Q6.
Explain the end-to-end Machine Learning Pipeline. Detail each stage from data acquisition to evaluation.
A real-world machine learning system follows a systematic multi-step mathematical pipeline:
  1. Problem Formulation: Translating a real-world task into a well-defined mathematical problem (e.g., Regression, Binary/Multiclass Classification, Clustering).
  2. Data Collection & Preprocessing: Handling missing values, noise removal, outlier detection, and encoding categorical variables into numeric vectors.
  3. Feature Engineering & Scaling:
    • Standardization (Z-score): $z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$ (zero mean, unit variance).
    • Min-Max Normalization: $x' = \frac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}} \in [0, 1]$.
  4. Data Splitting: Dividing data into mutually disjoint sets: Training Set (60–70% for learning parameters), Validation Set (15–20% for tuning hyperparameters), and Test Set (15–20% for unbiased final evaluation).
  5. Model Training (Optimization): Minimizing the empirical risk (Loss Function) over parameter space $\Theta$: $\hat{\theta} = \arg\min_\theta \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n L(y_i, f(x_i; \theta))$.
  6. Model Evaluation & Validation: Calculating generalization metrics (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1, MSE) using $k$-fold cross-validation.
(6 Marks) Q7.
Explain how Learning Curves are used to diagnose High Bias (Underfitting) and High Variance (Overfitting). What actions should be taken in each case?
Learning curves plot Training Error and Validation Error against the number of training instances $m$:
Case 1: High Bias (Underfitting):
  • Symptom: Both training error and validation error plateau at an unacceptably high value. The gap between training and validation error is very small.
  • Diagnostic: Adding more training data will NOT help. The model lacks capacity to capture the underlying pattern.
  • Corrective Action: Increase model capacity (use a higher-degree polynomial, deeper tree), add more informative features, or reduce regularization ($\lambda \to 0$).
Case 2: High Variance (Overfitting):
  • Symptom: Training error remains very low, but validation error is substantially higher, leaving a large generalization gap between the two curves.
  • Diagnostic: The model has memorized the training noise. As $m$ increases, the validation curve continues to trend downward toward the training curve.
  • Corrective Action: Collect more training examples, introduce/increase regularization (L1/L2 penalty $\lambda$), perform feature selection to remove noisy dimensions, or reduce model complexity.
(5 Marks) Q8.
Explain $K$-Fold Cross-Validation, Stratified $K$-Fold, and Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV). When should Stratified $K$-Fold be preferred?
  • $K$-Fold Cross-Validation: Splits dataset into $K$ equal non-overlapping folds. Trains on $K-1$ folds and evaluates on the $k$-th fold, rotating $K$ times. Average test score gives an unbiased estimator of generalization error. Common choices: $K=5$ or $K=10$.
  • Stratified $K$-Fold: A variation of $K$-fold where each fold is guaranteed to contain approximately the same percentage of samples of each target class as the complete dataset.
    When preferred: Mandatory in imbalanced classification (e.g., 98% negative, 2% positive). Standard random $K$-fold might produce folds containing zero positive instances, yielding misleading zero-recall evaluations.
  • Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV): An extreme case of $K$-fold where $K = n$ (total number of samples). In each of the $n$ iterations, exactly 1 sample is held out for testing and $n-1$ are used for training. Completely deterministic with minimal bias, but computationally prohibitive for large $n$ and has high estimator variance.
(5 Marks) Q9.
Explain Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) and Structural Risk Minimization (SRM). How does regularization implement the SRM principle?
  • True Risk (Generalization Error): The expected loss over the true underlying joint distribution $P(x, y)$: $$R(h) = \mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim P}[L(h(x), y)]$$ Since $P(x, y)$ is unknown, $R(h)$ cannot be computed directly.
  • Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM): Replaces true expectation with average training error: $$\hat{R}_n(h) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n L(h(x_i), y_i)$$ Defect of ERM: When the hypothesis space $\mathcal{H}$ is excessively expressive, minimizing empirical risk alone leads directly to overfitting.
  • Structural Risk Minimization (SRM): Vapnik's principle that balances empirical risk against model capacity (VC-dimension): $$R(h) \le \hat{R}_n(h) + \Omega(\mathcal{H}, n)$$ where $\Omega$ is a complexity penalty.
  • Regularization as SRM: In practice, regularization implements SRM directly by augmenting the loss with a model complexity penalty $\Omega(\theta)$: $$\min_\theta \hat{R}_n(\theta) + \lambda \Omega(\theta)$$ where $\Omega(\theta) = \|\theta\|_2^2$ (L2) or $\|\theta\|_1$ (L1). The hyperparameter $\lambda > 0$ governs the trade-off between fitting training data and controlling complexity.
(5 Marks) Q10.
Define the Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) Learning framework (Valiant). What is Sample Complexity, and state the sample size lower bound for a finite hypothesis space?
1. Formal Definition of PAC Learnability:
A concept class $\mathcal{C}$ is Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) learnable by an algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ using hypothesis space $\mathcal{H}$ if for every target concept $c \in \mathcal{C}$, every distribution $\mathcal{D}$ over instance space $\mathcal{X}$, and any error parameter $\epsilon \in (0, 1/2)$ and failure probability $\delta \in (0, 1/2)$:
Given a training set of $m$ independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) examples drawn from $\mathcal{D}$, algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ outputs a hypothesis $h \in \mathcal{H}$ such that with probability at least $1 - \delta$, the true generalization error $R(h)$ is at most $\epsilon$: $$P_{\mathcal{D}}\left( R(h) \le \epsilon \right) \ge 1 - \delta \quad \text{where} \quad R(h) = P_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}(h(x) \neq c(x))$$
  • Approximately Correct: The generalization error is small ($R(h) \le \epsilon$).
  • Probably: The learner succeeds with high confidence ($1 - \delta$), recognizing that an unrepresentative training sample might be drawn with small probability $\delta$.
2. Sample Complexity for Finite Hypothesis Spaces:
Sample Complexity is the minimum number of training examples $m$ required to guarantee that the output hypothesis satisfies the $(\epsilon, \delta)$ PAC criterion.
For any consistent learner with a finite hypothesis space $\mathcal{H}$, the sample complexity bound is: $$\mathbf{m \ge \frac{1}{\epsilon} \left( \ln|\mathcal{H}| + \ln\frac{1}{\delta} \right)}$$ This guarantees that any consistent hypothesis that makes zero errors on $m$ training points has true error at most $\epsilon$ with confidence $1 - \delta$.

(10 Marks) Q11.
State and prove the Bias-Variance Decomposition of the Expected Squared Error for a regression estimator. Explain the significance of each term.
Theorem (Bias-Variance Decomposition):
Let the true data-generating process be $y = f(x) + \epsilon$, where $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon] = 0$ and $\text{Var}(\epsilon) = \sigma^2$ (independent noise). Let $\hat{f}(x)$ be an estimator trained on a random dataset $\mathcal{D}$. The expected mean squared prediction error at a query point $x$ over all possible training datasets $\mathcal{D}$ is: $$\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}, \epsilon}\left[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2\right] = \underbrace{\left(\text{Bias}[\hat{f}(x)]\right)^2}_{\text{Bias}^2} + \underbrace{\text{Var}[\hat{f}(x)]}_{\text{Variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{Irreducible Error}}$$ where: $$\text{Bias}[\hat{f}(x)] = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\hat{f}(x)] - f(x), \quad \text{Var}[\hat{f}(x)] = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\left(\hat{f}(x) - \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\hat{f}(x)]\right)^2\right]$$ Rigorous Mathematical Proof:
To simplify notation, let $\hat{f} = \hat{f}(x)$, $f = f(x)$, and $\bar{f} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\hat{f}]$.
The error term is: $$y - \hat{f} = (f + \epsilon) - \hat{f} = (f - \bar{f}) + (\bar{f} - \hat{f}) + \epsilon$$ Squaring both sides: $$(y - \hat{f})^2 = \left[ (f - \bar{f}) + (\bar{f} - \hat{f}) + \epsilon \right]^2$$ $$= (f - \bar{f})^2 + (\bar{f} - \hat{f})^2 + \epsilon^2 + 2(f - \bar{f})(\bar{f} - \hat{f}) + 2(f - \bar{f})\epsilon + 2(\bar{f} - \hat{f})\epsilon$$ Now take the expectation $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}, \epsilon}[\cdot]$ of each term:
  1. $\mathbb{E}[(f - \bar{f})^2] = (f - \bar{f})^2 = (\bar{f} - f)^2 = \left(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}] - f\right)^2 = \mathbf{\text{Bias}[\hat{f}]^2}$ (since $f$ and $\bar{f}$ are non-random constants).
  2. $\mathbb{E}[(\bar{f} - \hat{f})^2] = \mathbb{E}\left[\left(\hat{f} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}]\right)^2\right] = \mathbf{\text{Var}[\hat{f}]}$.
  3. $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^2] = \text{Var}(\epsilon) + (\mathbb{E}[\epsilon])^2 = \sigma^2 + 0 = \mathbf{\sigma^2}$ (the irreducible noise variance).
  4. Cross-term 1: $\mathbb{E}[2(f - \bar{f})(\bar{f} - \hat{f})] = 2(f - \bar{f}) \mathbb{E}[\bar{f} - \hat{f}] = 2(f - \bar{f})(\bar{f} - \bar{f}) = \mathbf{0}$.
  5. Cross-term 2: $\mathbb{E}[2(f - \bar{f})\epsilon] = 2(f - \bar{f})\mathbb{E}[\epsilon] = 2(f - \bar{f})(0) = \mathbf{0}$.
  6. Cross-term 3: Since noise $\epsilon$ is independent of the training data $\mathcal{D}$,
    $\mathbb{E}[2(\bar{f} - \hat{f})\epsilon] = 2\mathbb{E}[\bar{f} - \hat{f}]\mathbb{E}[\epsilon] = 0 \cdot 0 = \mathbf{0}$.
Summing these terms completes the proof: $$\mathbb{E}\left[(y - \hat{f})^2\right] = \text{Bias}[\hat{f}]^2 + \text{Var}[\hat{f}] + \sigma^2 \quad \blacksquare$$ Significance of the Three Components:
  • Bias$^2$: Measures how far the average prediction over all possible datasets deviates from the true underlying function. High bias reflects wrong model assumptions.
  • Variance: Measures how much the predictions fluctuate across different training datasets. High variance means the model is overly sensitive to training sample fluctuations.
  • Irreducible Error ($\sigma^2$): The fundamental lower bound on error caused by noise in measurement or missing latent variables; cannot be eliminated by any algorithm.

💡 1. Linear Regression vs. Logistic Regression

Linear Regression models a continuous relationship $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_d x_d + \epsilon$ by minimizing residual sum of squares. When the target $y \in \{0, 1\}$ is categorical, linear regression fails because it outputs predictions outside $[0, 1]$.
Logistic Regression resolves this by applying the non-linear Sigmoid (Logistic) Function $\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$, squashing any real number $z = w^T x + b \in (-\infty, \infty)$ into a valid probability $P(y=1|x) \in (0, 1)$.

Figure 2.1: Left: Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) residual minimization. Right: Sigmoid function mapping real inputs to probability.

💡 2. Optimization (Gradient Descent) & Regularization Geometry (L1 vs L2)

Gradient Descent: An iterative first-order optimization algorithm that updates parameters $\theta$ in the direction of steepest descent (negative gradient of loss): $$\theta^{(t+1)} = \theta^{(t)} - \alpha \nabla_\theta J(\theta)$$ where $\alpha > 0$ is the learning rate.

L1 (Lasso) vs L2 (Ridge) Geometry: Why does Lasso produce sparse weights ($\beta_j = 0$) while Ridge only shrinks weights? The L1 constraint region is a diamond (polytope with sharp corners on axes). The elliptical loss contours touch the diamond at the corners, setting parameters exactly to zero! The L2 constraint is a smooth circle/sphere, so the contact point rarely falls directly on an axis.

Figure 2.2: Geometric justification of sparsity in L1 (Lasso) vs. weight shrinkage in L2 (Ridge) regularization.

💡 3. Learning Rate Dynamics & Goodness-of-Fit ($R^2$ Decomposition)

The total variation in target $y$ around its sample mean $\bar{y}$ decomposes into explained and unexplained components: $$SS_{\text{tot}} = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2 = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2}_{SS_{\text{reg}}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}_{SS_{\text{res}}}$$ The Coefficient of Determination $R^2 = \frac{SS_{\text{reg}}}{SS_{\text{tot}}} = 1 - \frac{SS_{\text{res}}}{SS_{\text{tot}}} \in [0, 1]$ represents the proportion of total variance explained by the model.

Short Answer Questions (2/3 Marks)
(2 Marks) Q12.
State the four fundamental Gauss-Markov assumptions of Linear Regression.
For the linear regression model $y = X\beta + \epsilon$:
  1. Linearity in Parameters: The relationship between $y$ and features $x$ is linear in the parameters $\beta$.
  2. Strict Exogeneity: The conditional expectation of the errors is zero: $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon | X] = 0$.
  3. Homoscedasticity & No Autocorrelation: Error variance is constant ($\text{Var}(\epsilon_i) = \sigma^2$) and errors are uncorrelated ($\text{Cov}(\epsilon_i, \epsilon_j) = 0$ for $i \neq j$), i.e., $\text{Var}(\epsilon | X) = \sigma^2 I$.
  4. No Multicollinearity: The feature matrix $X$ has full column rank, so $X^T X$ is strictly invertible.
(2 Marks) Q13.
Why does Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) fail when features are collinear? How does Ridge Regression resolve this?
The closed-form OLS solution is $\hat{\beta} = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T Y$. When features are linearly dependent (multicollinearity), the matrix $X^T X$ is singular (or near-singular), meaning $\det(X^T X) \approx 0$, making matrix inversion impossible or numerically unstable (exploding variances).
Ridge Resolution: Ridge adds a positive diagonal penalty $\lambda I$ ($\lambda > 0$): $$\hat{\beta}_{\text{ridge}} = (X^T X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^T Y$$ Since $X^T X$ is positive semi-definite (eigenvalues $\mu_i \ge 0$), the matrix $(X^T X + \lambda I)$ has strictly positive eigenvalues $(\mu_i + \lambda) > 0$. Thus, it is guaranteed to be non-singular and always invertible.
(2 Marks) Q14.
Define Elastic Net Regularization. What fundamental limitation of Lasso does it overcome?
Elastic Net linearly combines both L1 (Lasso) and L2 (Ridge) penalties in its loss function: $$J(\beta) = \|Y - X\beta\|_2^2 + \lambda_1 \|\beta\|_1 + \lambda_2 \|\beta\|_2^2$$ Limitation Overcome: When features are strongly correlated, Lasso tends to arbitrarily select only one feature from the correlated group and zero out the rest. Elastic Net overcomes this by creating a grouping effect (via the strictly convex L2 term), ensuring that correlated features are selected or dropped together while still performing sparse feature selection.
(3 Marks) Q15.
Compare Batch Gradient Descent, Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), and Mini-batch Gradient Descent.
  • Batch Gradient Descent: Computes the gradient over the entire dataset of $n$ samples at each step: $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \nabla L_i$. Exact, stable convergence, but computationally prohibitive for large datasets.
  • Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD): Updates parameters using a single randomly chosen sample $i$ at each step: $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \nabla L_i$. Fast and escapes local minima, but exhibits high variance and noisy oscillations.
  • Mini-batch Gradient Descent: Computes the gradient over a subset of size $b$ (e.g., $b = 32, 64, 128$): $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \frac{1}{b} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} \nabla L_i$. Strikes the optimal balance: leverages GPU vectorization while maintaining stable convergence.
(3 Marks) Q16.
Compare the closed-form Normal Equations and Gradient Descent for solving Multiple Linear Regression across computational complexity and data scale.
  • Normal Equations ($\hat{\beta} = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T Y$):
    • Pros: No need to choose a learning rate $\alpha$; no iterations required (analytical one-step exact solution).
    • Cons: Inverting the $d \times d$ matrix $X^T X$ has computational complexity $\mathcal{O}(d^3)$. Becomes extremely slow and memory-intensive when feature count $d > 10^4$.
  • Gradient Descent:
    • Pros: Scales efficiently to massive datasets with millions of samples $n$ and millions of features $d$. Computational complexity per step is $\mathcal{O}(nd)$.
    • Cons: Requires tuning hyperparameters (learning rate $\alpha$, batch size, convergence threshold); requires feature scaling to ensure fast convergence.

(5 Marks) Q17.
Derive the analytical Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) estimators $\hat{\beta}_0$ and $\hat{\beta}_1$ for Simple Linear Regression.
Let the simple linear regression model be $y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \epsilon_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$. We want to minimize the Residual Sum of Squares (RSS): $$S(\beta_0, \beta_1) = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i)^2$$ To find the minimum, compute the partial derivatives and set them to zero:
Step 1: Partial derivative with respect to $\beta_0$: $$\frac{\partial S}{\partial \beta_0} = -2 \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i) = 0 \implies \sum_{i=1}^n y_i - n\beta_0 - \beta_1 \sum_{i=1}^n x_i = 0$$ Dividing by $n$ (where $\bar{y} = \frac{1}{n}\sum y_i$, $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i$): $$\bar{y} - \beta_0 - \beta_1 \bar{x} = 0 \implies \mathbf{\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}}$$
Step 2: Partial derivative with respect to $\beta_1$: $$\frac{\partial S}{\partial \beta_1} = -2 \sum_{i=1}^n x_i (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i) = 0 \implies \sum_{i=1}^n x_i (y_i - (\bar{y} - \beta_1 \bar{x}) - \beta_1 x_i) = 0$$ $$\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \left[ (y_i - \bar{y}) - \beta_1 (x_i - \bar{x}) \right] = 0$$ Using the algebraic identity $\sum x_i (y_i - \bar{y}) = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})$ and $\sum x_i (x_i - \bar{x}) = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2$: $$\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) - \beta_1 \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = 0$$ $$\mathbf{\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{\text{Cov}(x, y)}{\text{Var}(x)}} \quad \blacksquare$$
(6 Marks) Q18.
Explain Logistic Regression. Define the Odds Ratio, Logit Function, and derive the derivative of the Sigmoid function.
1. Odds and Logit:
Let $p = P(y=1|x)$ be the probability of the positive class. The Odds are defined as the ratio of success probability to failure probability: $$\text{Odds} = \frac{p}{1-p}$$ Taking the natural logarithm yields the Logit function (Log-Odds), which models a linear combination of inputs: $$\text{logit}(p) = \ln\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) = w^T x + b$$ Solving for $p$: $$\frac{p}{1-p} = e^{w^T x + b} \implies p = e^{w^T x + b}(1-p) \implies p(1 + e^{w^T x + b}) = e^{w^T x + b}$$ $$p = \frac{e^{w^T x + b}}{1 + e^{w^T x + b}} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{1 + e^{-(w^T x + b)}} = \sigma(z)}$$ 2. Derivative of the Sigmoid Function:
Let $\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$. Differentiating with respect to $z$: $$\frac{d\sigma}{dz} = -1(1 + e^{-z})^{-2} \cdot (-e^{-z}) = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2}$$ Rewrite the numerator as $(1 + e^{-z}) - 1$: $$\frac{d\sigma}{dz} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \cdot \frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \left(1 - \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\right) = \mathbf{\sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))} \quad \blacksquare$$ This elegant property makes gradient computation computationally efficient.
(5 Marks) Q19.
Explain the One-vs-Rest (OvR) and One-vs-One (OvO) strategies for Multiclass Classification using binary classifiers.
Suppose we have $K > 2$ distinct target classes $\{1, 2, \dots, K\}$:
  • One-vs-Rest (OvR / One-vs-All):
    • Trains exactly $K$ separate binary classifiers. Classifier $k$ treats class $k$ as positive (+1) and all other $K-1$ classes combined as negative (-1).
    • To predict for a new point $x$, run all $K$ models and assign the class with the highest decision confidence/probability: $$\hat{y} = \arg\max_{k \in \{1,\dots,K\}} f_k(x)$$
    • Pros/Cons: Fast ($K$ classifiers), but classifiers can suffer from class imbalance.
  • One-vs-One (OvO):
    • Trains a binary classifier for every unique pair of classes. The total number of models is $\binom{K}{2} = \frac{K(K-1)}{2}$.
    • To predict, query all $\frac{K(K-1)}{2}$ classifiers. Each classifier votes for one class. The class with the most votes wins.
    • Pros/Cons: Each sub-problem is balanced and trained on a smaller subset, but scales quadratically $\mathcal{O}(K^2)$ with the number of classes.
(6 Marks) Q20.
Prove that in Simple Linear Regression with an intercept, the Total Sum of Squares decomposes as $SS_{\text{tot}} = SS_{\text{reg}} + SS_{\text{res}}$. Why is Adjusted $R^2$ needed in multiple regression?
Proof of Sum of Squares Decomposition:
Write the deviation $(y_i - \bar{y})$ by adding and subtracting the fitted value $\hat{y}_i$: $$y_i - \bar{y} = (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y}) + (y_i - \hat{y}_i)$$ Squaring and summing over all $n$ samples: $$\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2 + \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 + 2\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})(y_i - \hat{y}_i)$$ We must show that the cross-product term is zero: $$\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y}) e_i = \sum_{i=1}^n \hat{y}_i e_i - \bar{y}\sum_{i=1}^n e_i$$ From the first OLS normal equation, $\sum_{i=1}^n e_i = 0$.
From the second OLS normal equation, $\sum_{i=1}^n x_i e_i = 0$. Since $\hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_i$: $$\sum_{i=1}^n \hat{y}_i e_i = \hat{\beta}_0 \sum_{i=1}^n e_i + \hat{\beta}_1 \sum_{i=1}^n x_i e_i = \hat{\beta}_0(0) + \hat{\beta}_1(0) = 0$$ Therefore, the cross-product vanishes completely: $$\mathbf{SS_{\text{tot}} = SS_{\text{reg}} + SS_{\text{res}}} \quad \blacksquare$$ Why Adjusted $R^2$ is Needed:
Standard $R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{\text{res}}}{SS_{\text{tot}}}$ monotonically increases (or remains constant) whenever any new feature is added, even if that feature is completely random noise. Adjusted $R^2$ penalizes model complexity by incorporating degrees of freedom: $$R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - \left[ \frac{(1 - R^2)(n - 1)}{n - p - 1} \right]$$ where $p$ is the number of predictors. Adjusted $R^2$ will increase only if the new feature improves model fit more than what would be expected by pure chance.

State and prove the Gauss-Markov Theorem for Simple Linear Regression. Show that the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) estimator $\hat{\beta}_1$ is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE).
1. Theorem Statement (Gauss-Markov):
Under the standard Gauss-Markov assumptions ($\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_i]=0$, $\text{Var}(\epsilon_i)=\sigma^2$, $\text{Cov}(\epsilon_i, \epsilon_j)=0$ for $i \neq j$), the Ordinary Least Squares estimator $\hat{\beta}_1$ has the minimum variance among all linear, unbiased estimators of $\beta_1$. It is therefore BLUE (Best Linear Unbiased Estimator).

2. Rigorous Mathematical Proof:
The OLS slope estimator can be expressed as a linear combination of target values $y_i$: $$\hat{\beta}_1 = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i y_i \quad \text{where} \quad w_i = \frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{\sum_{j=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{S_{xx}}$$ Notice that: $$\sum_{i=1}^n w_i = 0, \quad \sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i = 1, \quad \sum_{i=1}^n w_i^2 = \frac{1}{S_{xx}} \implies \text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \sigma^2 \sum_{i=1}^n w_i^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}$$ Now consider any arbitrary linear estimator of $\beta_1$: $$\tilde{\beta}_1 = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i y_i = \sum_{i=1}^n (w_i + d_i) y_i$$ where $d_i = c_i - w_i$ represents the deviation of the arbitrary weights from the OLS weights.
Taking expectation (with $y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \epsilon_i$): $$\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\beta}_1] = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i (\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i) = \beta_0 \sum_{i=1}^n c_i + \beta_1 \sum_{i=1}^n c_i x_i$$ For $\tilde{\beta}_1$ to be an unbiased estimator of $\beta_1$ ($\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\beta}_1] = \beta_1$), we must have: $$\sum_{i=1}^n c_i = 0 \implies \sum_{i=1}^n (w_i + d_i) = 0 \implies \mathbf{\sum_{i=1}^n d_i = 0}$$ $$\sum_{i=1}^n c_i x_i = 1 \implies \sum_{i=1}^n (w_i + d_i) x_i = 1 \implies 1 + \sum_{i=1}^n d_i x_i = 1 \implies \mathbf{\sum_{i=1}^n d_i x_i = 0}$$ Now compute the variance of $\tilde{\beta}_1$: $$\text{Var}(\tilde{\beta}_1) = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2 \text{Var}(y_i) = \sigma^2 \sum_{i=1}^n (w_i + d_i)^2 = \sigma^2 \sum_{i=1}^n w_i^2 + \sigma^2 \sum_{i=1}^n d_i^2 + 2\sigma^2 \sum_{i=1}^n w_i d_i$$ Evaluate the cross-product term: $$\sum_{i=1}^n w_i d_i = \sum_{i=1}^n \left( \frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{S_{xx}} \right) d_i = \frac{1}{S_{xx}} \left( \sum_{i=1}^n d_i x_i - \bar{x}\sum_{i=1}^n d_i \right) = \frac{1}{S_{xx}} (0 - 0) = \mathbf{0}$$ Therefore: $$\mathbf{\text{Var}(\tilde{\beta}_1) = \text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1) + \sigma^2 \sum_{i=1}^n d_i^2}$$ Since $\sigma^2 \sum d_i^2 \ge 0$, we have: $$\text{Var}(\tilde{\beta}_1) \ge \text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1)$$ The variance of $\tilde{\beta}_1$ is strictly greater than the OLS variance unless $d_i = 0$ for all $i$ (in which case $\tilde{\beta}_1 \equiv \hat{\beta}_1$). Thus, $\hat{\beta}_1$ has the strictly minimum variance among all linear unbiased estimators. $\blacksquare$

Formulate Softmax Regression (Multinomial Logistic Regression) for $K > 2$ classes. Define the Softmax function, state the Categorical Cross-Entropy loss, and derive its gradient with respect to class logits $z_k$.
1. The Softmax Activation Function:
For multiclass classification with $K$ classes, each class $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ has a linear logit $z_k = w_k^T x + b_k$. The Softmax function normalizes the unconstrained logits into a valid probability distribution over all $K$ classes: $$\hat{y}_k = P(y = k | x) = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^K e^{z_j}} \quad \text{where} \quad \sum_{k=1}^K \hat{y}_k = 1, \quad \hat{y}_k > 0$$ 2. Categorical Cross-Entropy Loss:
Representing the true label as a one-hot encoded vector $y = [y_1, \dots, y_K]^T$ where $y_k \in \{0, 1\}$ and $\sum y_k = 1$, the loss for a single sample is: $$J(z) = -\sum_{k=1}^K y_k \ln(\hat{y}_k)$$ 3. Gradient Derivation with respect to Logits $z_i$:
By the chain rule: $\frac{\partial J}{\partial z_i} = -\sum_{k=1}^K \frac{y_k}{\hat{y}_k} \frac{\partial \hat{y}_k}{\partial z_i}$.
Computing the Jacobian of the Softmax function $\frac{\partial \hat{y}_k}{\partial z_i}$:
  • When $k = i$: $\frac{\partial \hat{y}_i}{\partial z_i} = \frac{e^{z_i} \sum e^{z_j} - (e^{z_i})^2}{(\sum e^{z_j})^2} = \hat{y}_i(1 - \hat{y}_i)$.
  • When $k \neq i$: $\frac{\partial \hat{y}_k}{\partial z_i} = \frac{0 - e^{z_k} e^{z_i}}{(\sum e^{z_j})^2} = -\hat{y}_k \hat{y}_i$.
Substituting these into the loss derivative: $$\frac{\partial J}{\partial z_i} = -\frac{y_i}{\hat{y}_i} \left( \hat{y}_i(1 - \hat{y}_i) \right) - \sum_{k \neq i} \frac{y_k}{\hat{y}_k} (-\hat{y}_k \hat{y}_i) = -y_i(1 - \hat{y}_i) + \sum_{k \neq i} y_k \hat{y}_i$$ $$= -y_i + y_i \hat{y}_i + \hat{y}_i \sum_{k \neq i} y_k = -y_i + \hat{y}_i \left( y_i + \sum_{k \neq i} y_k \right)$$ Since $\sum_{k=1}^K y_k = 1$, this simplifies to: $$\mathbf{\frac{\partial J}{\partial z_i} = \hat{y}_i - y_i} \quad (\text{Predicted Probability } - \text{ True Target}) \quad \blacksquare$$ The gradient is identical in structure to binary logistic regression.

Derive the Normal Equations $\hat{\beta} = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T Y$ for Multiple Linear Regression using matrix calculus. Then derive the Ridge Regression closed-form solution.
Part A: Derivation of the OLS Normal Equations
Let $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 1}$ be the target vector, $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (d+1)}$ be the design matrix, and $\beta \in \mathbb{R}^{(d+1) \times 1}$ be the parameter vector. The residual vector is $e = Y - X\beta$.
The objective is to minimize the sum of squared errors: $$J(\beta) = e^T e = (Y - X\beta)^T (Y - X\beta)$$ Expanding the matrix product: $$J(\beta) = (Y^T - \beta^T X^T)(Y - X\beta) = Y^T Y - Y^T X \beta - \beta^T X^T Y + \beta^T X^T X \beta$$ Notice that $Y^T X \beta$ is a scalar ($1 \times 1$), so $(Y^T X \beta)^T = \beta^T X^T Y$. Thus: $$J(\beta) = Y^T Y - 2 \beta^T X^T Y + \beta^T X^T X \beta$$ Using standard matrix calculus identities: $$\nabla_\beta (\beta^T A) = A, \quad \nabla_\beta (\beta^T A \beta) = (A + A^T)\beta = 2A\beta \quad (\text{if } A \text{ is symmetric})$$ Differentiating $J(\beta)$ with respect to $\beta$: $$\frac{\partial J}{\partial \beta} = -2 X^T Y + 2 X^T X \beta$$ Setting the gradient to the zero vector $\mathbf{0}$: $$-2 X^T Y + 2 X^T X \beta = 0 \implies X^T X \beta = X^T Y$$ Assuming $X$ has full rank, $X^T X$ is invertible: $$\mathbf{\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T Y} \quad \blacksquare$$ Part B: Derivation of Ridge Regression Estimator
Ridge adds an L2 penalty on the coefficient magnitudes (excluding the intercept): $$J_{\text{ridge}}(\beta) = (Y - X\beta)^T (Y - X\beta) + \lambda \beta^T \beta$$ Expanding: $$J_{\text{ridge}}(\beta) = Y^T Y - 2\beta^T X^T Y + \beta^T X^T X \beta + \lambda \beta^T \beta = Y^T Y - 2\beta^T X^T Y + \beta^T (X^T X + \lambda I)\beta$$ Differentiating with respect to $\beta$: $$\frac{\partial J_{\text{ridge}}}{\partial \beta} = -2 X^T Y + 2(X^T X + \lambda I)\beta$$ Setting the gradient to zero: $$(X^T X + \lambda I)\beta = X^T Y \implies \mathbf{\hat{\beta}_{\text{ridge}} = (X^T X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^T Y} \quad \blacksquare$$

Formulate Logistic Regression using Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE). Derive the Binary Cross-Entropy loss function and compute the gradient update rule.
1. Probabilistic Formulation:
Let $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ follow a Bernoulli distribution conditioned on input $x_i$: $$P(y_i = 1 | x_i; \theta) = \hat{y}_i = \sigma(\theta^T x_i), \quad P(y_i = 0 | x_i; \theta) = 1 - \hat{y}_i$$ This can be written in a single compact expression: $$P(y_i | x_i; \theta) = \hat{y}_i^{y_i} (1 - \hat{y}_i)^{1 - y_i}$$ 2. Likelihood and Log-Likelihood:
Assuming the $n$ training instances are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.): $$L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n P(y_i | x_i; \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n \hat{y}_i^{y_i} (1 - \hat{y}_i)^{1 - y_i}$$ Taking the natural log gives the log-likelihood: $$\ell(\theta) = \ln L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \left[ y_i \ln \hat{y}_i + (1 - y_i) \ln(1 - \hat{y}_i) \right]$$ In optimization, we minimize the Negative Log-Likelihood (Binary Cross-Entropy Loss): $$J(\theta) = -\frac{1}{n} \ell(\theta) = \mathbf{-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \left[ y_i \ln(\hat{y}_i) + (1 - y_i) \ln(1 - \hat{y}_i) \right]}$$ 3. Gradient Computation:
Let $z_i = \theta^T x_i$, so $\hat{y}_i = \sigma(z_i)$. By the chain rule: $$\frac{\partial J}{\partial \theta_j} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial \hat{y}_i} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{y}_i}{\partial z_i} \cdot \frac{\partial z_i}{\partial \theta_j}$$ For a single sample:
  • $\frac{\partial}{\partial \hat{y}_i} [ -y_i \ln \hat{y}_i - (1-y_i) \ln(1-\hat{y}_i) ] = -\frac{y_i}{\hat{y}_i} + \frac{1-y_i}{1-\hat{y}_i} = \frac{\hat{y}_i - y_i}{\hat{y}_i(1-\hat{y}_i)}$
  • $\frac{\partial \hat{y}_i}{\partial z_i} = \hat{y}_i (1 - \hat{y}_i)$ (derivative of sigmoid)
  • $\frac{\partial z_i}{\partial \theta_j} = x_{ij}$
Multiplying these together: $$\frac{\partial J}{\partial \theta_j} = \left(\frac{\hat{y}_i - y_i}{\hat{y}_i(1-\hat{y}_i)}\right) \cdot \left(\hat{y}_i(1-\hat{y}_i)\right) \cdot x_{ij} = (\hat{y}_i - y_i) x_{ij}$$ Averaging over all $n$ samples: $$\mathbf{\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{y}_i - y_i) x_i = \frac{1}{n} X^T (\hat{Y} - Y)}$$ Gradient Descent Update Rule: $$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \frac{1}{n} X^T (\sigma(X\theta) - Y) \quad \blacksquare$$

💡 1. $k$-Nearest Neighbors ($k$-NN) & Decision Trees

$k$-Nearest Neighbors ($k$-NN): A non-parametric, lazy learning algorithm that does not construct an explicit model during training. To classify a query point $x_q$, it identifies the $k$ closest labeled points using a distance metric (typically Euclidean distance $d(x, x') = \sqrt{\sum (x_j - x'_j)^2}$) and assigns the majority class.

Decision Trees: Hierarchical partition of the feature space using axis-aligned orthogonal splits. Split selection is governed by Shannon Entropy and Information Gain: $$H(S) = -\sum_{i=1}^c p_i \log_2(p_i), \quad IG(S, A) = H(S) - \sum_{v \in \text{Values}(A)} \frac{|S_v|}{|S|} H(S_v)$$

💡 2. Support Vector Machines (SVM) & Soft-Margin Formulations

An SVM finds the optimal separating hyperplane $w^T x + b = 0$ that separates two classes with the maximum geometric margin $\gamma = \frac{2}{\|w\|}$. (2 Marks) Q25.
What is the "Curse of Dimensionality" and how does it negatively affect $k$-Nearest Neighbors ($k$-NN)?
As the number of feature dimensions $d$ increases, the volume of the feature space grows exponentially ($\mathcal{O}(2^d)$). Consequently, data points become extremely sparse and equidistant from each other.
Impact on $k$-NN: In high dimensions, the distance to the nearest neighbor approaches the distance to the farthest neighbor: $$\lim_{d \to \infty} \frac{\text{dist}_{\max} - \text{dist}_{\min}}{\text{dist}_{\min}} = 0$$ Because all points are nearly equidistant, Euclidean distance loses its discriminative power, causing $k$-NN accuracy to degrade severely unless dimensionality reduction (e.g., PCA) is applied.
(2 Marks) Q26.
Define Shannon Entropy and Gini Impurity for a classification problem. When do they reach their maximum?
For a dataset $S$ with $c$ classes where $p_i$ is the probability of class $i$:
  • Shannon Entropy: $H(S) = -\sum_{i=1}^c p_i \log_2(p_i)$
  • Gini Impurity: $\text{Gini}(S) = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^c p_i^2$
Both measures quantify node impurity. They reach their minimum (0) when a node is completely pure (all samples belong to one class, $p_1 = 1$). They reach their maximum when samples are uniformly distributed across all classes ($p_i = 1/c$ for all $i$), representing maximum uncertainty ($H_{\max} = \log_2(c)$ and $\text{Gini}_{\max} = 1 - 1/c$).
(2 Marks) Q27.
State the role of slack variables $\xi_i$ and the regularization parameter $C$ in Soft-Margin SVM.
  • Slack Variables ($\xi_i \ge 0$): Measure the degree of margin violation for sample $i$. If $\xi_i = 0$, the point is on or outside the correct margin. If $0 < \xi_i \le 1$, the point falls inside the margin but is correctly classified. If $\xi_i > 1$, the point is misclassified.
  • Trade-off Parameter $C > 0$: Controls the trade-off between maximizing margin width ($\frac{1}{2}\|w\|^2$) and penalizing slack violations ($C \sum \xi_i$). A large $C$ heavily penalizes errors (narrow margin, high variance). A small $C$ tolerates more margin violations (wider margin, higher bias).
(2 Marks) Q28.
Compare Pre-pruning and Post-pruning in Decision Tree learning.
  • Pre-pruning (Early Stopping): Halts tree construction during growth if a stopping condition is met (e.g., maximum depth reached, number of samples at node $< k$, or information gain $< \epsilon$). Advantage: Fast computation. Drawback: Susceptible to the horizon effect (may stop too early before discovering valuable feature interactions).
  • Post-pruning (Cost-Complexity Pruning): Allows the tree to grow fully to maximum depth, then prunes non-essential subtrees from the bottom up using validation set error or a penalty $\text{Cost}(T) = \text{Error}(T) + \alpha |T|$. Advantage: Superior generalization performance.
(2 Marks) Q29.
Define Minkowski Distance of order $p$. Show how it generalizes Manhattan and Euclidean distances.
The Minkowski Distance of order $p \ge 1$ between two vectors $x, x' \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is: $$D_p(x, x') = \left( \sum_{j=1}^d |x_j - x'_j|^p \right)^{\frac{1}{p}}$$
  • When $p = 1$: $D_1(x, x') = \sum_{j=1}^d |x_j - x'_j|$ is the Manhattan ($L_1$) Distance.
  • When $p = 2$: $D_2(x, x') = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^d (x_j - x'_j)^2}$ is the Euclidean ($L_2$) Distance.
  • When $p \to \infty$: $D_\infty(x, x') = \max_{j} |x_j - x'_j|$ is the Chebyshev ($L_\infty$) Distance.
(3 Marks) Q30.
What is the "Zero-Frequency Problem" in Naive Bayes? Explain Laplace (Add-1) Smoothing.
If a feature value $x_j$ never appears with class $y$ in the training set, the maximum likelihood estimate is $P(x_j | y) = 0$. Because probabilities are multiplied together: $$P(y) \prod_{k=1}^d P(x_k | y) = 0$$ A single zero probability zeroes out the entire posterior probability, regardless of all other evidence.
Laplace (Add-1) Smoothing: Adds 1 to the numerator and the number of possible feature values $K$ to the denominator: $$P(x_j = v | y) = \frac{\text{Count}(x_j = v, y) + 1}{\text{Count}(y) + K}$$ This ensures every probability is strictly positive ($> 0$), resolving the zero-frequency defect.

Explain Gain Ratio in C4.5. Why does it overcome the fundamental bias of Information Gain towards features with many distinct values?
1. Defect of Information Gain: Information Gain $IG(S, A)$ is heavily biased toward attributes with many distinct values (e.g., a "Customer ID" or "Date" attribute). If a feature splits each record into its own singleton leaf, each child has entropy $H = 0$, giving maximal Information Gain, but the tree has zero predictive power.

2. Explain Gaussian Naive Bayes for continuous attributes. State the probability density formula and derive the Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) classification rule.
When a feature $X_j$ is continuous, Gaussian Naive Bayes assumes that conditioned on class $y = c$, $X_j$ follows a normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu_{cj}, \sigma_{cj}^2)$: $$P(X_j = x_j | y = c) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma_{cj}^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(x_j - \mu_{cj})^2}{2\sigma_{cj}^2} \right)$$ where $\mu_{cj}$ and $\sigma_{cj}^2$ are estimated from training samples of class $c$ using Maximum Likelihood: $$\hat{\mu}_{cj} = \frac{1}{N_c} \sum_{i: y_i = c} x_{ij}, \quad \hat{\sigma}_{cj}^2 = \frac{1}{N_c} \sum_{i: y_i = c} (x_{ij} - \hat{\mu}_{cj})^2$$ MAP Decision Rule: Applying Bayes' theorem with conditional independence: $$\hat{y} = \arg\max_{c} P(y=c) \prod_{j=1}^d P(X_j = x_j | y = c)$$ Taking the natural log to avoid numerical underflow: $$\mathbf{\hat{y} = \arg\max_{c} \left[ \ln P(y=c) - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^d \ln(2\pi \sigma_{cj}^2) - \sum_{j=1}^d \frac{(x_j - \mu_{cj})^2}{2\sigma_{cj}^2} \right]}$$ This defines quadratic or linear decision boundaries depending on whether class variances $\sigma_{cj}^2$ are shared or distinct.

1. The Motivation: If data is not linearly separable in the original input space $\mathbb{R}^d$, we can project it into a higher (or infinite) dimensional feature space $\mathcal{H}$ via a non-linear mapping $\phi(x)$, where it becomes linearly separable.

  1. Polynomial Kernel: $K(x, z) = (x^T z + c)^d$ where $d \in \mathbb{N}, c \ge 0$.
  2. Radial Basis Function (RBF / Gaussian) Kernel: $K(x, z) = \exp\left(-\gamma \|x - z\|^2\right)$ where $\gamma > 0$ (maps to infinite-dimensional Hilbert space).
  3. Sigmoid (Hyperbolic Tangent) Kernel: $K(x, z) = \tanh(\alpha x^T z + c)$.

Show that the Soft-Margin Support Vector Machine optimization problem is mathematically equivalent to Empirical Risk Minimization with L2 Regularization using the Hinge Loss function.
1. The Soft-Margin Primal Formulation:
The standard soft-margin SVM optimization problem with slack variables $\xi_i \ge 0$ is: $$\min_{w, b, \xi} \frac{1}{2}\|w\|^2 + C \sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i(w^T x_i + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i, \quad \xi_i \ge 0 \quad \forall i$$ 2. Expressing Slack as Hinge Loss:
Rearranging the constraint for each sample $i$: $$\xi_i \ge 1 - y_i(w^T x_i + b)$$ Since we also have $\xi_i \ge 0$, and the objective function seeks to minimize $\sum \xi_i$, the optimal choice of $\xi_i$ for any fixed $w, b$ must be as small as possible while satisfying both lower bounds: $$\xi_i^* = \max\left(0, \, 1 - y_i(w^T x_i + b)\right)$$ Notice that this is precisely the definition of the Hinge Loss function: $$L_{\text{hinge}}(y, \hat{f}(x)) = \max(0, \, 1 - y \hat{f}(x)) \quad \text{where} \quad \hat{f}(x) = w^T x + b$$ 3. The trade-off parameter $C = \frac{1}{\lambda}$ is inversely proportional to the regularization strength $\lambda$. $\blacksquare$

Derive the mathematical formulation of the Hard-Margin Support Vector Machine (SVM). Show why maximizing the geometric margin is equivalent to minimizing $\frac{1}{2}\|w\|^2$, and state the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions.
1. Geometric Margin Formulation:
Let the training set be $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_n, y_n)\}$ where $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and $y_i \in \{-1, +1\}$. A hyperplane is defined by $w^T x + b = 0$.
The signed perpendicular distance from any point $x_i$ to the hyperplane is: $$\gamma_i = \frac{y_i (w^T x_i + b)}{\|w\|}$$ The geometric margin of the hyperplane with respect to the dataset is the distance to the closest point: $$\gamma = \min_{i=1,\dots,n} \frac{y_i (w^T x_i + b)}{\|w\|}$$ We want to find $w$ and $b$ to maximize $\gamma$: $$\max_{w, b} \gamma \quad \text{subject to} \quad \frac{y_i (w^T x_i + b)}{\|w\|} \ge \gamma \quad \forall i$$ 2. Transformation to Quadratic Programming:
Notice that scaling $w \to c w$ and $b \to c b$ for any $c > 0$ does not change the hyperplane or geometric margin. Therefore, we can introduce the canonical scaling constraint such that for the closest point: $$\min_{i=1,\dots,n} y_i(w^T x_i + b) = 1$$ Under this convention, the geometric margin becomes: $$\gamma = \frac{1}{\|w\|}$$ Maximizing $\frac{1}{\|w\|}$ is mathematically identical to minimizing $\|w\|$, or equivalently minimizing $\frac{1}{2}\|w\|^2$ (which is strictly convex and differentiable).
Thus, the Primal Optimization Problem is: $$\mathbf{\min_{w, b} \frac{1}{2} \|w\|^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i(w^T x_i + b) \ge 1 \quad \forall i = 1, \dots, n}$$ 3. The Lagrangian Dual Formulation:
Introduce Lagrange multipliers $\alpha_i \ge 0$ for each inequality constraint: $$\mathcal{L}(w, b, \alpha) = \frac{1}{2} \|w\|^2 - \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i \left[ y_i(w^T x_i + b) - 1 \right]$$ Setting partial derivatives with respect to primal variables to zero:
  • $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w} = w - \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i y_i x_i = 0 \implies \mathbf{w = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i y_i x_i}$
  • $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial b} = -\sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i y_i = 0 \implies \mathbf{\sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i y_i = 0}$
Substituting these back into $\mathcal{L}$ yields the Dual Optimization Problem: $$\max_\alpha \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j (x_i^T x_j) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \alpha_i \ge 0, \quad \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i y_i = 0$$ 4. The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) Complementary Slackness: $$\alpha_i \left[ y_i (w^T x_i + b) - 1 \right] = 0 \quad \forall i$$ This reveals the fundamental geometry of SVMs:
  • If $y_i(w^T x_i + b) > 1$ (point is strictly outside margin): $\mathbf{\alpha_i = 0}$. Non-support vectors have zero influence on $w$.
  • If $\mathbf{\alpha_i > 0}$: then $y_i(w^T x_i + b) = 1$. These points lie exactly on the margin boundary and are the Support Vectors that uniquely determine the decision boundary! $\blacksquare$

(2 Marks) Q38.
Define Precision, Recall, and the $F_1$-Score. Why is $F_1$ computed as the harmonic mean?
From the confusion matrix:
  • Precision: Fraction of positive predictions that were correct: $\text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}$.
  • Recall (Sensitivity): Fraction of actual positives identified: $\text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}$.
  • $F_1$-Score: Harmonic mean of precision and recall: $$F_1 = 2 \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}$$
Why Harmonic Mean: The arithmetic mean punishes extreme imbalances poorly (e.g., if Precision = 1.0 and Recall = 0.0, the arithmetic mean is 0.5, giving a false sense of performance). The harmonic mean is dominated by the smaller value: if either Precision or Recall is close to 0, $F_1 \to 0$.
(2 Marks) Q39.
Explain the Elbow Method for determining the optimal number of clusters $K$ in $k$-Means.
The Elbow Method plots the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS / Inertia) as a function of $K$: $$\text{WCSS}(K) = \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{x_i \in C_k} \|x_i - \mu_k\|^2$$ As $K$ increases, WCSS monotonically decreases (reaching 0 when $K = n$). However, the rate of decrease drops sharply after a certain point. The "elbow" of the curve represents the point of diminishing returns, where adding more clusters yields negligible reduction in variance. This point corresponds to the true optimal $K$.
(3 Marks) Q40.
Define the Silhouette Coefficient $s(i)$ for an observation in clustering. How is its value interpreted?
For a sample $i \in C_A$:
  • Let $a(i)$ be the mean distance from $i$ to all other points in the same cluster $C_A$ (cohesion).
  • Let $b(i) = \min_{C_B \neq C_A} \frac{1}{|C_B|} \sum_{j \in C_B} \|x_i - x_j\|$ be the mean distance to points in the nearest neighboring cluster (separation).
The Silhouette Coefficient is: $$s(i) = \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max(a(i), b(i))} \in [-1, +1]$$ Interpretation:
  • $s(i) \approx +1$: Sample is well clustered and far from neighboring clusters.
  • $s(i) \approx 0$: Sample lies on the decision boundary between two clusters.
  • $s(i) < 0$: Sample is closer to a neighboring cluster than its assigned cluster (misclustered).
(3 Marks) Q41.
Compare Single Linkage, Complete Linkage, and Average Linkage in Hierarchical Clustering.
For two clusters $A$ and $B$:
  • Single Linkage (Minimum Distance): $d(A, B) = \min_{x \in A, y \in B} \|x - y\|$. Prone to the chaining effect, producing long, straggly clusters.
  • Complete Linkage (Maximum Distance): $d(A, B) = \max_{x \in A, y \in B} \|x - y\|$. Avoids chaining and enforces compact, spherical clusters, but sensitive to outliers.
  • Average Linkage: $d(A, B) = \frac{1}{|A||B|} \sum_{x \in A} \sum_{y \in B} \|x - y\|$. A robust compromise that averages all pairwise distances.

Explain Ward's Minimum Variance Method in Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering. State its objective function and merge distance formula.
1. Objective: Ward's method treats hierarchical clustering as an optimization problem analogous to $k$-Means. 2. Merge Distance Formula:

(3 Marks) Q43.
What is the exact algebraic connection between Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)?
Let $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ be a zero-centered data matrix. The sample covariance matrix is $\Sigma = \frac{1}{n} X^T X$.
Applying Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to $X$: $$X = U S V^T$$ where $U \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ and $V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ are orthogonal matrices, and $S \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ contains non-negative singular values $s_1 \ge s_2 \ge \dots \ge s_d \ge 0$ on its diagonal.
Computing $X^T X$: $$X^T X = (U S V^T)^T (U S V^T) = V S^T U^T U S V^T = V S^2 V^T \quad (\text{since } U^T U = I)$$ Comparing this to the eigenvalue decomposition of $\Sigma = \frac{1}{n} X^T X = V \Lambda V^T$:
  • The right singular vectors (columns of $V$) are identical to the principal direction eigenvectors of $\Sigma$.
  • The eigenvalues $\lambda_j$ of the covariance matrix are directly related to the singular values: $\lambda_j = \frac{s_j^2}{n}$.
Thus, PCA can be computed directly via SVD of $X$ without explicitly forming the expensive covariance matrix $X^T X$.
(3 Marks) Q44.
Explain the $k$-Means++ initialization algorithm. Why does it dramatically outperform standard random initialization?
Standard $k$-means picks $K$ centroids uniformly at random, often placing multiple centroids in the same dense cluster and leading to poor local minima.
$k$-Means++ Algorithm:
  1. Select the first centroid $\mu_1$ uniformly at random from the dataset.
  2. For each remaining data point $x$, compute $D(x)$, the shortest distance to the closest already chosen centroid.
  3. Select the next centroid $\mu_{new}$ from data points with probability proportional to $D(x)^2$: $$P(x) = \frac{D(x)^2}{\sum_{x'} D(x')^2}$$
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until all $K$ centroids are chosen.
Why it outperforms random initialization: By choosing points that are far away from existing centroids with high probability, $k$-means++ spreads initial centroids across the dataset's natural clusters. Mathematically, it guarantees an expected approximation ratio of $\mathcal{O}(\log K)$ relative to the optimal clustering.

Explain Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering. Describe how a Dendrogram is constructed and how the number of clusters is determined from it.
1. Agglomerative (Bottom-Up) Algorithm:
  1. Start with $n$ singleton clusters, where each data point is its own cluster: $C_i = \{x_i\}$.
  2. Compute the pairwise distance matrix $D \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ between all clusters using a chosen linkage metric (single, complete, or average).
  3. Merge the two clusters $C_A$ and $C_B$ with the smallest inter-cluster distance $d(C_A, C_B)$.
  4. Update the distance matrix to reflect the distance from the new merged cluster to all remaining clusters.
  5. Repeat steps 3 and 4 until a single grand cluster containing all $n$ points remains.
2. Dendrogram Construction & Cluster Determination:
  • The entire merge sequence is plotted as an inverted tree called a Dendrogram. The vertical axis represents the merge distance $d(C_A, C_B)$.
  • To obtain a discrete partition into $K$ clusters, draw a horizontal cut line across the dendrogram at a chosen distance threshold. The number of vertical lines intersected by the horizontal cut corresponds exactly to the number of clusters $K$.
  • The optimal cut is traditionally placed where it intersects the longest vertical branches without any horizontal cross-bars, representing clusters separated by maximum distance.

Show that Principal Component Analysis (PCA) can be derived equivalently by minimizing the Mean Squared Reconstruction Error. Prove its mathematical equivalence to variance maximization.
1. Setup:
Let $x_1, \dots, x_n \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be zero-centered data ($\frac{1}{n}\sum x_i = \mathbf{0}$). We approximate each point $x_i$ using an orthonormal projection matrix $U_k = [u_1, \dots, u_k] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ where $U_k^T U_k = I_k$.
The reconstructed point in the original space is $\hat{x}_i = U_k U_k^T x_i$.
The objective is to minimize the Mean Squared Reconstruction Error: $$J_{\text{recon}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i - \hat{x}_i\|^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i - U_k U_k^T x_i\|^2$$ 2. Pythagorean Decomposition:
Expanding the squared Euclidean norm: $$\|x_i - U_k U_k^T x_i\|^2 = (x_i - U_k U_k^T x_i)^T (x_i - U_k U_k^T x_i)$$ $$= x_i^T x_i - 2 x_i^T U_k U_k^T x_i + x_i^T U_k (U_k^T U_k) U_k^T x_i$$ Since $U_k^T U_k = I_k$, the last two terms combine: $$\|x_i - U_k U_k^T x_i\|^2 = x_i^T x_i - x_i^T U_k U_k^T x_i = \|x_i\|^2 - \|U_k^T x_i\|^2$$ 3. Equivalence Proof:
Averaging over all $n$ observations: $$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i - \hat{x}_i\|^2 = \underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i\|^2}_{\text{Total Dataset Variance (Constant)}} - \underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \|U_k^T x_i\|^2}_{\text{Variance of Projected Data}}$$ Since the total variance $\frac{1}{n}\sum \|x_i\|^2 = \text{Tr}(\Sigma)$ is fixed and independent of $U_k$: $$\arg\min_{U_k} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i - \hat{x}_i\|^2 \iff \arg\max_{U_k} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \|U_k^T x_i\|^2$$ Conclusion: Minimizing reconstruction error is mathematically identical to maximizing projected variance. Both formulations yield the exact same principal eigenvectors of the covariance matrix $\Sigma$. $\blacksquare$
(6 Marks) Q49.
Describe Lloyd's Algorithm for $k$-Means Clustering. Prove that the objective function $J$ is guaranteed to converge monotonically.
1. Lloyd's Algorithm Steps:
  1. Initialization: Choose $K$ initial cluster centroids $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_K \in \mathbb{R}^d$ randomly from the dataset.
  2. Assignment Step: Assign each data point $x_i$ to its nearest centroid: $$c_i^{(t)} = \arg\min_{k \in \{1,\dots,K\}} \|x_i - \mu_k^{(t)}\|^2$$
  3. Update Step: Recalculate each centroid as the arithmetic mean of points assigned to it: $$\mu_k^{(t+1)} = \frac{1}{|C_k^{(t)}|} \sum_{i \in C_k^{(t)}} x_i$$
  4. Convergence Check: Repeat steps 2 and 3 until centroid positions stop changing ($\|\mu_k^{(t+1)} - \mu_k^{(t)}\| < \epsilon$).
2. Monotonic Convergence Proof:
The objective function is $J(c, \mu) = \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i - \mu_{c_i}\|^2$.
  • In the Assignment Step: Centroids $\mu$ are fixed. We assign each $x_i$ to minimize $\|x_i - \mu_{c_i}\|^2$. Thus, $J(c^{(t+1)}, \mu^{(t)}) \le J(c^{(t)}, \mu^{(t)})$.
  • In the Update Step: Assignments $c$ are fixed. For any cluster $k$, taking the derivative with respect to $\mu_k$: $$\frac{\partial}{\partial \mu_k} \sum_{i \in C_k} \|x_i - \mu_k\|^2 = -2 \sum_{i \in C_k} (x_i - \mu_k) = 0 \implies \mu_k = \frac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{i \in C_k} x_i$$ Since the second derivative is positive ($+2 |C_k| > 0$), the centroid mean is the unique global minimizer of $J$ for fixed $c$. Thus, $J(c^{(t+1)}, \mu^{(t+1)}) \le J(c^{(t+1)}, \mu^{(t)})$.
Since $J \ge 0$ is bounded below by zero, and decreases monotonically at every step, and the number of distinct partitions is finite ($K^n$), the algorithm is guaranteed to converge to a local minimum. $\blacksquare$

Provide a complete mathematical derivation of Principal Component Analysis (PCA) using Lagrange Multipliers. Show that the principal directions are the eigenvectors of the empirical covariance matrix.
1. Setup and Formulation:
Let $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ be a data matrix with $n$ observations and $d$ features. Assume without loss of generality that the data is zero-centered (i.e., sample mean $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i = \mathbf{0}$).
The empirical sample covariance matrix $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is: $$\Sigma = \frac{1}{n} X^T X$$ We seek a unit projection vector $u_1 \in \mathbb{R}^d$ ($\|u_1\|^2 = u_1^T u_1 = 1$) such that projecting the data points onto $u_1$ maximizes the variance of the projected points. 2. Sample Variance of the Projected Data:
The projection of sample $x_i$ onto $u_1$ is the scalar $z_{i1} = x_i^T u_1$.
The mean of the projected points is: $$\bar{z}_1 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^T u_1 = \left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\right)^T u_1 = \mathbf{0}^T u_1 = 0$$ The sample variance of the projected data is: $$\text{Var}(z_1) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i^T u_1 - 0)^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (u_1^T x_i)(x_i^T u_1) = u_1^T \left( \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i^T \right) u_1 = \mathbf{u_1^T \Sigma u_1}$$ 3. Constrained Optimization via Lagrange Multipliers:
We want to maximize $u_1^T \Sigma u_1$ subject to the normalization constraint $u_1^T u_1 = 1$: $$\max_{u_1} u_1^T \Sigma u_1 \quad \text{subject to} \quad u_1^T u_1 = 1$$ Formulate the Lagrangian with Lagrange multiplier $\lambda_1$: $$\mathcal{L}(u_1, \lambda_1) = u_1^T \Sigma u_1 - \lambda_1 (u_1^T u_1 - 1)$$ Differentiating with respect to $u_1$ (since $\Sigma$ is symmetric, $\nabla_{u_1} (u_1^T \Sigma u_1) = 2 \Sigma u_1$): $$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u_1} = 2 \Sigma u_1 - 2 \lambda_1 u_1 = \mathbf{0}$$ Dividing by 2 yields the celebrated Eigenvalue Equation: $$\mathbf{\Sigma u_1 = \lambda_1 u_1}$$ This proves that $u_1$ must be an eigenvector of the covariance matrix $\Sigma$, and $\lambda_1$ is the corresponding eigenvalue! 4. Identifying the Maximum:
Pre-multiply the eigenvalue equation by $u_1^T$: $$u_1^T \Sigma u_1 = u_1^T (\lambda_1 u_1) = \lambda_1 (u_1^T u_1) = \lambda_1 (1) = \mathbf{\lambda_1}$$ Therefore, the variance of the projected data is precisely equal to the eigenvalue $\lambda_1$! To maximize variance, $u_1$ must be the eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}$. 5. Subsequent Principal Components:
For the second component $u_2$, we maximize $u_2^T \Sigma u_2$ subject to $u_2^T u_2 = 1$ and the orthogonality constraint $u_1^T u_2 = 0$. By an identical Lagrange multiplier formulation, $u_2$ is the eigenvector corresponding to the second largest eigenvalue $\lambda_2$.
Variance Explained: The total variance in the dataset is $\text{Tr}(\Sigma) = \sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j$. The proportion of variance explained by the first $k$ components is: $$\text{Explained Variance Ratio} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j}{\sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j} \quad \blacksquare$$

Criterion $K$-Means Clustering Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering
Clustering Philosophy Partitioning algorithm; directly divides data into $K$ mutually disjoint subsets. Hierarchical tree construction; builds a multi-level nested sequence of clusters (Dendrogram).
Number of Clusters ($K$) Must be specified strictly in advance before running the algorithm. Does NOT need $K$ in advance. Any $K$ can be chosen retrospectively by cutting the dendrogram.
Time Complexity $\mathcal{O}(n \cdot K \cdot d \cdot I)$ where $I$ is iterations. Highly scalable for large datasets ($n > 10^6$). $\mathcal{O}(n^2 \log n)$ or $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ due to pairwise distance matrix. Prohibitive for large datasets.
Space Complexity $\mathcal{O}((n + K)d)$ — stores only data points and $K$ centroids. $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ — must store the full $n \times n$ distance matrix in memory.
Sensitivity to Initialization Highly sensitive to initial centroid placement; can get trapped in poor local minima (remedied by $k$-means++). Completely deterministic. Running multiple times on the same data produces the identical dendrogram.
Cluster Geometry Assumptions Assumes convex, spherical clusters of roughly equal variance. Fails on elongated or concentric shapes. Can discover arbitrary, non-spherical shapes when using Single Linkage. Complete linkage favors compact spheres.
Sensitivity to Outliers High (mean centroids are heavily pulled by outliers). Moderate to Low (outliers simply merge into singleton branches high up in the tree).
